Abstract
Identifying causal bounds from discrete observational data is a fundamental problem in causal inference.
While exact analytical bounds exist for certain graphs, this is not generally the case. In this paper, we
introduce a framework for estimating such bounds using neural generative models together with a new
extrapolation procedure to estimate these bounds more accurately. Across multiple causal inference
settings, it produces bounds that are consistently closer to the true bounds than those obtained with
existing general neural approaches. We validate the framework on a range of benchmark problems for
which exact bounds are available.
1
Introduction
Estimating the effects of interventions from observational data is a central problem in causal inference. In
many applications, however, the available observations and causal assumptions do not uniquely determine
the causal quantity of interest. Hidden confounding, incomplete measurements, or limited experimental
access may permit multiple structural causal models to generate the same observational distribution while
implying different responses to an intervention. In such settings, reporting a single causal estimate can conceal
uncertainty that persists even with complete knowledge of the observational distribution.
Partial identification provides a principled alternative to point estimation (Manski, 2003). Rather than
selecting a single observationally compatible model, partial identification characterises the range of values
attained by a causal query over all models satisfying the assumed causal structure and observational constraints.
For example, the identified interval for an average treatment effect is obtained by minimising and maximising
the effect over all structural models that reproduce the observed distribution. The resulting interval separates
uncertainty arising from causal non-identifiability from ordinary uncertainty in parameter estimation.
For discrete causal models, causal bounds can be derived using linear, multilinear, or polynomial opti-
misation. Classical results provide sharp bounds in important special cases, such as instrumental-variable
models (Balke and Pearl, 1997). More general approaches use logical relations between counterfactual events,
canonical structural causal models, and automated polynomial-programming formulations (Song et al., 2025;
Sachs et al., 2023; Boushehrian et al., 2026). These approaches offer valuable sharpness or certification
guarantees, but the size of the resulting optimisation problem can increase rapidly with the number of
variables, latent-variable configurations, and possible response functions. Furthermore, they have narrow
applicability to general problems.
Recently, generative neural network approaches have been applied to this problem (Hu et al., 2021; Tan
et al., 2024; Balazadeh Meresht et al., 2022). These attempt to find the extremal functions that satisfy the
bound, providing both the bounds and functions that reach these bounds. While these are applicable to a
wide range of problems, we find that their accuracy is limited.
This paper introduces a differentiable approach to causal partial identification for finite discrete structural
causal models. Each structural mechanism is represented by a neural network whose output defines a

We fit a neural network model by minimising the Energy Distance (Szekely, 2003) between the predictive

extrapolation procedure, with theoretical justification, is introduced to estimate the extremal causal effects
compatible with the observational distribution.

Related Work
2.1
Optimisation-based causal bounds
Partial identification characterises the range of causal quantities compatible with the observed distribution
and the assumed causal structure (Manski, 2003). For discrete structural causal models, classical approaches
represent latent structural mechanisms through deterministic response functions and optimise over their
distributions. This yields linear programmes in important settings, including the instrumental-variable model
with binary (Balke and Pearl, 1997) and general categorical variables (Song et al., 2025). Symbolic methods
extend this construction to broader classes of causal graphs and queries and are implemented in tools such
as causaloptim (Sachs et al., 2023, 2026). More general discrete problems can be expressed as polynomial
programs (Zhang et al., 2022). Autobounds automatically constructs such programs and applies spatial branch-
and-bound to obtain progressively tighter certified bounds (Duarte et al., 2024). Pruning and decomposition
techniques can improve scalability for particular classes of graphs (Shridharan and Iyengar, 2023). These
methods offer sharpness or certification guarantees in their supported settings, but their computational cost
can grow rapidly with the number of variables and categories, and may fail to converge in more complex
settings.
2.2
Neural approaches to partial identification
Neural causal models (Xia et al., 2021) provide an alternative to explicit response-function enumeration
by representing structural mechanisms with flexible function approximators. Their connection to structural
causal models and counterfactual reasoning has been studied theoretically, including representation and
identifiability properties (Xia et al., 2022). Bounded Counterfactual Causal Effects (BCCE) parametrises
structural mechanisms and latent variables using implicit generative models (Goodfellow et al., 2014) and
employs adversarial training to match the observational distribution while optimising the causal effect
(Hu et al., 2021). Related approaches impose distributional constraints using Wasserstein or Sinkhorn
discrepancies and optimise them through Lagrangian procedures (Balazadeh Meresht et al., 2022; Tan et al.,
2024). NeuralPartialID further establishes approximation and consistency results for continuous problems
under regularity conditions such as Lipschitz continuity (Tan et al., 2024). Stochastic causal programming and
implicit-generative approaches also address partial identification with continuous or multivariate treatments
(Balazadeh Meresht et al., 2022; Padh et al., 2023). These methods broaden the range of causal models that
can be considered.
2


2.3
Relation to our method
Our method builds on the neural causal partial-identification approaches. However, there are two key
differences. Firstly, rather than comparing generated and observed samples through a learnt discriminator
or an optimal-transport subproblem, we directly compute the probability assigned to every observed state
and evaluate a closed-form energy distance between the resulting probability vectors. This avoids adversarial
training and stochastic sampling of categorical model outputs.
The main distinction is our treatment of the exact-fit limit. Existing neural approaches attempt to
optimise the causal query subject to, or jointly with, an observational-distribution constraint. In practice,
this optimisation becomes difficult when the distributional discrepancy is required to be extremely small.
We instead solve a signed family of stable penalised problems and extrapolate the causal contrast to the
zero-penalty limit. Our perturbation analysis shows that, within a locally regular regime, the causal contrast
varies linearly with the penalty parameter while the energy-distance loss varies quadratically. This provides
both a principled extrapolation rule and a diagnostic for selecting the range over which extrapolation is valid.

4.5
Training and bound estimation
Motivated by Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, we train models at several positive and negative values of λ and
extrapolate their causal estimates to λ = 0. In practice, we minimise the objective
bJλ(θ) = bLED(θ; bq) + λa(θ).
(14)
A series of different models with different positive and negative λ values are trained. We then regress the
causal estimates on the λ values on the positive and negative branches. The intercept of the positive branch
regression estimates the lower bound, while the one of the negative branch estimates the upper bound. We do
not solve the KKT conditions, rather we solve Equation (14) using SGD.
The regression should use penalties within the local regime described by Theorem 1. However, this analysis
does not consider if the optima can be found, only how the local region around them behaves. Empirically,
using SGD on NNs we observe that convergence near λ = 0 is poor, but good convergence for small λ. Hence,
we have a range of valid λ between the convergence and locally linear regime.
We select the regression points using the studentised-residual procedure given in algorithm 1 in the
Appendix. The method begins with the largest penalty magnitudes and adds points closer to zero while
they remain consistent with the fitted linear trend. The accompanying energy-distance values provide an
additional diagnostic.
For all our experiments, we used 4 equally spaced points for positive and negative λ, |λ| ∈{0.005, 0.010,
0.015, 0.020}. We found this range to be a good trade off between accuracy and training time, and is well
within the linear regime. Further training details are given in Appendix A.

In order to visualise our method, on a fixed ternary IV dataset, we plot the ATE and LED for multiple

5.4
Analysis of the baselines
To investigate why the neural baselines underperform, we test each model using neural networks of varying
widths. All other parameters are held constant, except for the learning rate, which is either scaled using
µP scaling (Yang et al., 2022) or kept fixed. µP scaling promotes stable training across different widths by
ensuring that the magnitudes of parameter updates and gradients remain stable. All models are trained on
a fixed ternary IV SEM. Figure 3 shows the average absolute error between the predicted and analytical
bounds.
Ideally, improving the width and hence representational capacity of the model improves performance.
However, this is not observed for NeuralPartialID and BCCE, the accuracy decreases for larger NN widths.
For BCCE, we attribute this to an instability in the GAN training procedure where the critic becomes too
strong, preventing the generator from learning. Similarly, for NeuralPartialID, this is attributed to unstable
parameter updates and hyperparameter sensitivity. Further analysis can be seen in Appendix G.
6
Conclusion
We introduced a neural approach to partial identification in finite discrete structural causal models. The
method represents each structural mechanism by a neural categorical model and computes the induced
observational and interventional distributions explicitly over the finite state space. Causal bounds are estimated
by solving a signed family of energy-distance-penalised problems and extrapolating the resulting causal
estimates to the zero-penalty limit. Our analysis shows that, along a smooth one-sided solution path, the
causal estimand varies linearly with the penalty parameter to first order, while the energy-distance loss varies
quadratically. The corresponding intercepts recover the lower and upper bounds within the neural model
family. Across several simulations, our resulting extrapolated bounds are consistently more accurate than
those of the evaluated neural baselines and remain competitive with analytical methods.
The approach is limited to discrete models and requires identifying a locally regular penalty range.
Extending the approach to continuous variables and further development on the theory behind the validity of
the linear extrapolation regime would broaden its practical scope. More generally, these results suggest that
optimisation paths, rather than only their limiting iterates, can provide useful information for estimating
causal bounds in partially identified models.

F.2
Observational samples
An observational dataset of size b is obtained by repeatedly sampling the root and noise variables and then
evaluating all structural assignments in topological order.
Since the problem is only partially identified, we do not use the ATE of the SEM used to generate
the dataset. There are many other SEMs with identical observational distributions. Rather, we use the
observational distribution to compute bounds on the ATE. This is done by monte-carlo sampling for q over a
large number of observations, then q is used in the analytic solver for the true bounds.
F.3
Graph details
For each graph, the U node is unobserved, acting as unobserved confounding. In the Binary IV, Proxy and
Mediated graphs, all observed variables are binary. In the Ternary IV graph, the treatment remains binary
(so ATE is easily calculated) while node Z and node Y are both ternary. The datasets are generated using a
random seeds from 0 to 9, with the exception of the Mediated dataset. Many datasets from this graph gave
nearly 0 ATE. Instead, for this graph, the first 10 datasets to give nontrivial ATE values were used. The final
seeds were {1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 16, 17}.

BCCE is a GAN architecture, where a generator and discriminator are adversarially optimised. As the
NN widths were scaled up, we found an instability where the discriminator becomes too strong and no useful
gradient feedback is given to the generator. This can be seen by the high generator error as the with of the
NNs becomes large, as seen in Table 2 where the model is evaluated on a ternary IV example. The error
measured is the largest absolute difference in probability between the predicted and true distributions. This
failure mode is well documented and techniques to enhance their stability have been developed (Salimans
et al., 2016), but is undesirable for the user. As a result of this issue, BCCE struggles on more complex tasks.
Similarly, NeuralPartialID’s performance slightly degrades with larger NN widths. This method is based
on a Lagrange multiplier method, shared with (Balazadeh Meresht et al., 2022) that solves the minimax
optimisation problem,

where S (Pθ, P) denotes the Sinkhorn distance (Cuturi, 2013) between the predicted and observed distributions.
The model parameters θ are updated by gradient descent, while the Lagrange multiplier (χ) is updated
according to χ ←χ + χLR [S (Pθ, P) −α]. The fit quality threshold α is derived from a pretraining phase
where the average treatment effect is not optimised.
In principle, the exact constrained solution is recovered in the limiting case χ →∞and α = 0. In
practice, however, the update rate of χ must be carefully balanced against the convergence rate of the model
parameters. If these rates are poorly matched, training may become prohibitively slow or unstable. Moreover,
the resulting bounds are highly sensitive to the choice of α. In practice, the algorithm converges to α > 0
and the constraint is relaxed, which tends to produce bounds that are wider than the theoretically optimal
bounds. Table 3 displays the fitted α and final χ values for different width models, on the same IV ternary
