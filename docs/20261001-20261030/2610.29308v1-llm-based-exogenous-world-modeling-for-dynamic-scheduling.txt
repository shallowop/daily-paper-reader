Abstract
Dynamic scheduling requires dispatching online arrivals
while anticipating future jobs, as poor decisions can com-
pound downstream costs under nonlinear tardiness objec-
tives. We show that a pretrained large language model (LLM)
can serve as the exogenous component of a structured world
model, forecasting uncertain future job arrivals. Specifi-
cally, we exploit an afterstate decomposition, which separates
known deterministic shop transitions from uncertain exoge-
nous job arrivals, enabling the LLM to forecast only the lat-
ter through a compact belief representation. Building on this,
we develop Afterstate World-Model Planning (AWMP), which
combines an evolved, belief-conditioned dispatching strategy
with short scenario-based rollouts. A surprise-triggered re-
fresh mechanism queries the LLM only when newly observed
arrivals deviate sufficiently from previous forecast, and online
residual self-calibration further conditions the LLM on its re-
cent forecast errors to handle nonstationary arrivals. Across
diverse arrival distributions, AWMP reduces weighted tardi-
ness by 6-21% on dynamic job-shop and 22-46% on flexible
job-shop benchmarks, relative to the best of eight classical
priority-dispatching rules.

Introduction
Scheduling determines how a collection of jobs should be
processed on limited machine resources while satisfying op-
erational constraints such as precedence, machine capacity,
and delivery commitments (Pinedo and Hadavi 1992; Smit
et al. 2025b). Classical scheduling formulations assume that
all jobs and their processing requirements are known in ad-
vance, allowing optimization over a fixed problem instance.
Real manufacturing systems, however, are inherently dy-
namic (Vieira, Herrmann, and Lin 2003; Ouelhadj and Petro-
vic 2009). New jobs arrive dynamically over time while ma-
chines are already processing previously released work, and
the scheduler must repeatedly dispatch operations without
knowing the future workload.
Under such uncertainty, scheduling performance is typ-
ically evaluated by due-date-oriented objectives. A widely
adopted objective is total weighted tardiness, which incurs
no cost before a job’s due date but imposes a growing penalty
once the due date is missed. This nonlinear objective cre-
ates sharp due-date thresholds: a dispatching decision that
appears locally beneficial may occupy critical machine ca-
pacity, delay a later high-priority job past its due date, and
incur a disproportionate downstream penalty. Consequently,
effective dynamic scheduling requires more than reacting to
the currently available operations, but also anticipating how
future job arrivals will reshape shop congestion and incor-
porating those expectations into decisions.
Existing approaches address this challenge either by react-
ing to the current workload or by explicitly modeling the fu-
ture. 1) Classical priority-dispatching rules (PDRs), such as
weighted shortest processing time (WSPT) and apparent tar-
diness cost (ATC), are fast and training-free (Panwalkar and
Iskander 1977; Haupt 1989), but they rank only the currently
available operations and carry no explicit representation of
future arrivals. 2) Anticipatory optimization methods, in-
cluding rolling-horizon replanning, sample-average approx-
imation (SAA), and multiple-scenario approaches (MSA),
evaluate decisions against future scenarios sampled from a
prespecified stochastic model through repeated reoptimiza-
tion (Powell 2007; Kleywegt, Shapiro, and Homem-de Mello
2002; Bent and Van Hentenryck 2004; Li et al. 2025). Their
performance relies heavily on the fidelity of the assumed
model, and the repeated reoptimization can be extremely ex-
pensive. 3) Recent neural methods learn dispatching policies
from simulated trajectories with stochastic arrivals (Chang
et al. 2022; Liu, Piplani, and Toro 2022, 2023; Liu et al.
2024b; Tang et al. 2026). While capable of capturing complex
dynamic patterns, they typically entangle the exogenous ar-
rival process with dispatching decisions and require task- and
distribution-specific training, with retraining needed once
either changes. These limitations motivate an anticipatory
scheduler that can exploit observed arrival patterns without
presupposing a parametric stochastic model, and that adapts
without heavy retraining or reoptimization.
In this paper, we leverage a pretrained large language
model (LLM) to model the exogenous component of a
structured world model by forecasting future job arrivals,
while explicitly accounting for known scheduling dynamics.
Specifically, we employ an afterstate decomposition to sep-
arate the deterministic effects of dispatch actions from the
uncertainty associated with future job arrivals. At each deci-
sion step, a dispatch action transforms the current state into
an afterstate, representing the resulting shop configuration
before subsequent arrivals are incorporated. This afterstate
captures endogenous changes, including machine availabil-
ity, operation progress, and precedence status, governed by

Building on this decomposition, we develop AWMP, an
Afterstate World-Model Planning framework for dynamic
scheduling. At each decision step, AWMP maps every fea-
sible dispatching action to its deterministic afterstate, and
scores the action using both its afterstate features and the
belief-derived features that capture future arrival pressure.
A scenario generator then converts the early-late belief into
concrete arrival scenarios, under which the retained action
candidates are evaluated through short rollouts, and the ac-
tion with the best aggregated evaluation is selected for dis-
patching.
Moreover, AWMP makes the LLM forecast adaptive and
efficient for online planning through three mechanisms.
First, online residual self-calibration feeds recent prediction-
outcome pairs and forecast errors into subsequent prompts,
enabling the LLM to adapt its arrival forecasts to nonsta-
tionary patterns through in-context learning. Second, be-
lief amortization caches the forecast across all candidate
evaluations and rollouts within a decision step, while the
cached belief is also reused across decision steps, where the
LLM is queried only when realized arrivals deviate suffi-
ciently from the current belief. This surprise-triggered re-
fresh reduces the number of LLM calls from one per roll-
out to a handful per episode. Third, meta-evolution searches
over compact, human-readable strategy objects, compris-
ing the scoring and value functions, rollout parameters, and
forecast-refresh threshold, to automatically discover an ef-
fective belief-conditioned planning strategy.
Our main contributions are as follows:
• We formalize dynamic scheduling through an afterstate
decomposition that separates the uncertain, exogenous job
arrivals from known, deterministic scheduling dynamics.
• We instantiate the exogenous arrival-belief component of
the structured world model with an LLM to forecast a
compact arrival belief, and make this forecast adaptive
and efficient through online residual self-calibration and
belief reuse with surprise-triggered refresh.
• We develop AWMP, a belief-conditioned afterstate plan-
ner integrating coarse action screening and short-rollout
evaluation under fixed scenario generation, with the plan-
ning strategy discovered offline via meta-evolution.
• Experiments across four arrival distributions show that
AWMP discovers strategies that outperform the best of
eight classical PDRs, reducing total tardiness by 6-21%
on dynamic job-shop instances and 22-46% on dynamic
flexible job-shop instances, while running in seconds.

Related Work
Classical dynamic scheduling methods. Classical methods
for dynamic scheduling broadly fall into reactive dispatch-
ing and anticipatory optimization. Reactive PDRs, such as
WSPT and ATC, re-rank eligible operations whenever the
shop state changes (Panwalkar and Iskander 1977; Haupt
1989). They are fast and training-free, but evaluate only the
current observable workload with no explicit representation
of future arrivals. Anticipatory methods, including rolling-
horizon optimization, SAA, and MSA, instead evaluate deci-
sions against expected or sampled future workloads (Powell
2007; Kleywegt, Shapiro, and Homem-de Mello 2002; Bent
and Van Hentenryck 2004; Li et al. 2025). Their lookahead
quality depends on the assumed arrival model, while repeat-
edly solving the resulting subproblems can be computation-
ally expensive. AWMP occupies a middle ground: it retains
low-latency online dispatching but augments it with a com-
pact arrival forecast inferred by an LLM from the observed


arrival history, without requiring the arrival pattern to be
specified as a parametric stochastic process.
Neural dynamic scheduling methods. Neural combina-
torial optimization has introduced graph and attention en-
coders combined with policy-gradient or value-based rein-
forcement learning (RL) for routing and scheduling (Kool,
van Hoof, and Welling 2019; Zhang et al. 2020). Recent
methods extend learned dispatching policies to dynamic
job-shop scheduling problems (JSSPs) and flexible job-shop
scheduling problems (FJSPs) with random job arrivals (Luo
2020; Liu, Piplani, and Toro 2022, 2023; Smit et al. 2025a;
Yu et al. 2026). These methods can capture complex in-
teractions among jobs, operations, and machines, but they
generally learn from specific simulated trajectories and may
require retraining when the arrival process or optimization
objective changes. In contrast, AWMP trains no neural pol-
icy, but uses a frozen pretrained LLM to estimate aggregate
arrival pressure, and converts it into dispatching decisions
through a compact planning strategy that is evolved offline
and frozen before evaluation.
Learned world models for planning. Model-based RL
learns a transition model of the environment to evaluate
and select future actions. Representative approaches include
MuZero (Schrittwieser et al. 2020; Antonoglou et al. 2022),
Dreamer (Hafner et al. 2021), and TD-MPC (Hansen, Wang,
and Su 2022). These methods are designed for settings in
which substantial environment dynamics must be learned
from interaction. Dynamic scheduling has a different struc-
ture: once an action is specified, the transitions in shop status
are deterministic and known. Our afterstate decomposition
exploits this structure by retaining exact endogenous dynam-
ics and isolating the future arrival stream as the only exoge-
nous uncertainty. AWMP forms a structured world model
from two parts: a low-dimensional predictor of exogenous
arrival pressure and the deterministic scheduling simulator.
LLMs for optimization and heuristic discovery. LLMs
have recently been used to generate and refine algorithms,
heuristics, and environment dynamics simulators (Romera-
Paredes et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024a; Ye et al. 2024). AWMP
uses LLMs in two separate roles. During online scheduling, a
pretrained LLM instantiates the exogenous component of the
structured world model by forecasting aggregate arrival pres-
sure. During offline meta-evolution, a proposer LLM evolves
a compact, human-readable planning strategy incorporating
the scoring rule, value estimator, and rollout parameters,
rather than an independent solver program.

Methodology
Our methodology is organized around three questions: what
to forecast, how to forecast, and how to plan with a struc-
tured world model, and AWMP addresses them through a
modular design, as illustrated in Fig. 2. First, we formal-
ize dynamic scheduling through an afterstate decomposi-
tion, which separates the known endogenous shop dynam-
ics from unknown exogenous job arrivals. This decomposi-
tion identifies future arrivals, rather than the complete shop
transition, as the only object that requires forecasting (Sec-
tion 3.1). Then, we instantiate the exogenous component with
a frozen, pretrained LLM to forecast a low-dimensional be-
lief of early-late future arrival pressure from the observed ar-
rival history, and introduce residual self-calibration to adapt
the forecast in an online manner, especially under nonsta-
tionary arrivals (Section 3.2). Building on this, we develop
AWMP, a belief-conditioned afterstate planner that first iden-
tifies promising actions with a compact scoring rule, and then
evaluates them through short rollouts under arrival scenarios
generated from the forecast belief (Section 3.3). Intra- and
inter-step belief caching and surprise-triggered belief refresh
amortize LLM calls without repeatedly forecasting at every
rollout or decision step. Finally, meta-evolution discovers a
compact, human-readable planning strategy offline, which is
then frozen for held-out evaluation (Section 3.4).
3.1
Afterstate Decomposition
We consider dynamic JSSPs and FJSPs, where jobs arrive
online and dispatching decisions must be made before ob-
serving future workload. Let t index decision steps and ct
denote the corresponding physical shop time. At decision
step t, the scheduler observes the current shop state st and
selects a feasible dispatch action at ∈A(st), where A(st)
is the set of all feasible operation-machine assignments. In
JSSPs, the machine for each operation is fixed, while in
FJSPs, machine selection is part of the action. The state st
contains the current shop configuration, including machine
availability, job progress, precedence status, and observed
arrival history ht. The effect of an action can be separated
from that of subsequent arrivals. Applying at first produces
an afterstate
σt = T(st, at),
(1)
where T is the known action-to-afterstate transition. The af-
terstate captures the updated machine availability, operation
progress, and, for FJSPs, the selected machine assignment
before any new arrivals are incorporated. These changes are
endogenous: once st and at are specified, they are fully de-
terministic.
We define the set of future job arrivals as:
Zt = {(ρj, ζj) : ρj > ct} ∼Pβ(· | ht),
(2)
where ρj is the release time of future job j, ζj collects its
attributes, such as its processing time, due date, and weight,
and β controls the arrival pattern such as intensity. The arrival
process is exogenous: dispatching actions do not affect which
jobs arrive or their release times.
Nevertheless, an action may change the physical time of the
next decision step, and hence which portion of Zt becomes
observable before that decision. The next state is therefore
determined by an event-advance function g:
st+1 = g(σt, Zt),
(3)
which is likewise known and deterministic given the after-
state σt and a realization of arrival stream Zt. The function
g advances shop time, executes the deterministic scheduling
dynamics, and incorporates exactly the jobs released during
the transition. Consequently, T and g need not be learned, and
the only unknown component required for exact anticipatory
planning is the conditional arrival process Pβ(· | ht).

3.2
LLM-Based Exogenous World Modeling
As discussed above, reliably predicting the complete arrival
stream Zt, such as the exact release times and job attributes,
is difficult and unnecessary. We instead forecast a compact
projection, referred to as the belief, of the future arrival
stream Zt that reflects the arrival pressure of Pβ(· | ht)
over a temporal horizon relevant to dispatching, without re-
quiring a high-dimensional prediction of individual future
jobs. We formulate this compact prediction as the exogenous
arrival-belief component of the structured world model and
instantiate it with a pretrained LLM.

provides prompt-level closed-loop feedback through recent
prediction-outcome residuals.

3.3
Belief-Conditioned Afterstate Planner
The arrival belief provides compact information about future
congestion, but it does not by itself determine which action
should be dispatched. To this end, AWMP converts the belief
bt into dispatching decisions through a coarse-to-fine plan-
ner: a lightweight belief-conditioned scorer first identifies a
small set of promising candidate actions, and short scenario-
based rollouts then compare and select dispatching actions.
The planner is specified by a compact planning strategy:
Θ = {r(·), v(·), K, d, n, τ} ,
(8)
where r(·) is a candidate-action scoring rule, v(·) is a termi-

erated scenarios, and τ is the surprise threshold to trigger
belief refresh. Both r and v are compact, human-readable
functions rather than neural networks. Their structures, to-
gether with the planning parameters, are discovered through
meta-evolution introduced in Section 3.4.

sary to query the LLM-based exogenous predictor at every
step. AWMP therefore applies a similar belief reuse princi-
ple that carries the most recent belief forward by subtracting
newly observed arrivals from the corresponding early or late
count. A new forecast is triggered only when the discrepancy
between the cumulative observed arrivals and those implied
by the cached belief exceeds the surprise threshold τ, or
when the cached prediction horizon no longer covers the
planner’s lookahead. Intra-step sharing is exact since all roll-
outs are conditioned on the same observed history, whereas
inter-step reuse is an intentional approximation that trades
forecast freshness for fewer LLM calls. Section 4.4 reports
the resulting reduction in LLM queries.
3.4
Meta-Evolution of Planning Strategy
Beyond forecast accuracy, final scheduling performance de-
pends critically on the planning strategy Θ that translates the
forecast belief into specific dispatching decisions. Manually
specifying such a strategy is difficult and prone to brittle per-
formance. We therefore formulate the discovery of planning
strategies as an LLM-driven meta-evolution loop that au-
tomatically evolves strategies to effectively leverage arrival
beliefs for producing better dispatching decisions.
Meta-evolution maintains a population of strategies and
produces new candidates through selection, crossover, and
multi-point mutation, with restart-on-stagnation to preserve
diversity. In each iteration, a proposer LLM revises the scor-
ing rule r(·), value estimator v(·), rollout parameters K, d, n,
and refresh threshold τ within predefined safety constraints
(we force K ≤6, d ≤3, and n ≤8 to avoid unrestricted
search) and a structural schema to keep the resulting strate-
gies compact, executable, and human-readable. Candidate
fitness is evaluated by weighted tardiness on the training
instances, with instance-level costs aggregated in the log do-
main to reduce domination by a few large-cost instances.
Within the meta-evolution loop, the proposer LLM modifies
only the strategy object Θ. It neither generates arrival fore-
casts nor alters the forecasting prompt or scenario generator
Γ. The LLM-based exogenous predictor remains frozen and
supplies belief bt independently during strategy evaluation.
After meta-evolution, the best strategy is selected and
frozen. During final evaluation, the scoring rule, value es-
timator, rollout parameters, refresh threshold, forecasting
prompt, scenario generator, and the LLM-based exogenous
predictor all remain fixed, with only the residual-calibration
record updated online as new arrivals are observed.
4
Experiments
We evaluate AWMP through four questions: 1) Does AWMP
improve scheduling performance across different shop con-
figurations and arrival distributions? 2) Does residual self-
calibration improve both forecast accuracy and downstream
scheduling? 3) How effectively does belief reuse reduce LLM
calls? 4) Can meta-evolution improve the planning strategy
and generalize well to held-out instances?
4.1
Experiment Setup
Benchmarks. We construct two dynamic scheduling settings
from standard static benchmarks: dynamic JSSPs, based on
80 Taillard instances (Taillard 1993), and dynamic FJSPs,
based on 15 Brandimarte and 66 Hurink instances (Brandi-
marte 1993; Hurink, Jurisch, and Thole 1994). For each
benchmark instance, we preserve its original shop configura-
tion and processing data (e.g., operation precedence, process-
ing times, and machine eligibility), while jobs are released
online according to one of four exogenous arrival distribu-
tions: pulse train, late-ramp, cyclic, and step-up. These distri-
butions capture periodic bursts, a monotonically increasing
arrival rate, cyclic variation, and a mid-horizon rate increase,
respectively, as illustrated in Appendix A. We control the ar-
rival intensity β to scale the scheduling horizon by a factor of
five relative to the base benchmarks to avoid trivial instances.
Scheduling performance is measured by total weighted tar-
diness, where jobs completed before their due dates incur no
penalty, while unfinished jobs are charged at the end of the
scheduling horizon, as detailed in Appendix C.
Baselines. We compare AWMP with two groups of base-
lines. The first contains eight classical PDRs: WSPT, ATC,
shortest processing time (SPT), earliest due date (EDD),
most work remaining (MWKR), first-in-first-out (FIFO),
minimum slack (MS), and cost over time (COVERT). They
re-rank currently feasible operations at every decision step
whenever the shop state changes or new job arrivals occur.
The second group comprises five optimization-based on-
line schedulers: Myopic, Rolling-horizon, MSA, SAA, and
expected-value optimization (EV). Myopic optimizes over
currently released jobs only; Rolling-horizon periodically re-
optimizes over a moving planning window; MSA selects de-
cisions through multiple-scenario consensus; SAA optimizes
a sample-average objective over sampled arrival scenarios;
and EV solves a deterministic approximation under expected
future arrivals. All five methods use OR-Tools (Perron and
Furnon 2023) to solve their subproblems, and are allocated
computational budgets at least as large as AWMP. We use
Claude Haiku 4 as the frozen LLM for online arrival-belief
prediction and Claude Sonnet 4 as the proposer LLM for
offline meta-evolution.

consequential under weighted tardiness, where an early de-

induced by an inaccurate explicit future model. AWMP com-

anticipation by inferring future arrival pressure from the ob-
served history and incorporating it into short-horizon plan-

4.3
Effect of Residual Self-Calibration
We evaluate the forecast accuracy and the effect of resid-
ual self-calibration on dynamic JSSP instances under a si-
nusoidally drifting arrival intensity. The near-term forecast
correlation increases from r = 0.12 using only the ob-
served arrival history to r = 0.93 after incorporating recent
prediction–outcome residuals (Fig. 3). We compute the latter
from 342 prediction–outcome pairs sampled from the same
dynamic JSSP instances and arrival distribution, after center-
ing the predictions and outcomes within each instance. This
closed-loop calibration enables the arrival belief to more
closely track the evolving arrival dynamics without parame-
ter updates.
The improved forecast also leads to better scheduling per-
formance. Under the same planning strategy, replacing the
raw belief with the calibrated belief reduces the objective
from 6771 to 4011, corresponding to a 41% reduction. The
sampled prediction–outcome pairs are obtained from a sep-
arate diagnostic run and are not included in the main evalu-
ation results.
4.4
LLM Call Efficiency
We compare three querying regimes under the same running
setting: 1) naive per-rollout querying, 2) w/ intra-step belief
caching, and 3) w/ inter-step reuse and surprise-triggered re-
fresh. Naive querying requires 7191 LLM calls per episode.
Intra-step belief caching reduces this count to 393, leading
to an 18× reduction, while incorporating inter-step reuse
and surprise-triggered refresh further reduces it to only 18,
approximately 400× fewer calls than naive querying. The re-
duction comes from sharing the same root belief across can-
didate actions and rollout scenarios within a decision step,
thereby avoiding repeated forecasts for identical observed
histories. Across consecutive decision steps, the cached be-
lief is carried forward and refreshed only when realized ar-
rivals deviate sufficiently from the current belief or when the
prediction horizon is no longer adequate. At the measured
latency of approximately 0.84 s per call, the naive regime
requires well over an hour per episode due to the latency
of LLM calls, whereas AWMP with inter- and intra-step
reuse finishes its queries within seconds. These results show
that belief reuse substantially amortizes the cost of online
LLM forecasting for repeated dispatching decisions, while
preserving the same belief-conditioned planning procedure.

5
Conclusion
We introduced AWMP, an afterstate world-model planning
framework for dynamic scheduling under online arrivals. The
afterstate decomposition separates known deterministic shop
dynamics from the exogenous uncertain arrival stream, form-
ing a structured worldmodel in which a pretrained LLMmod-
els the exogenous component while the scheduling simulator
retains the known endogenous transitions exactly. Residual
self-calibration adapts the belief forecast online under non-
stationary arrivals, while a meta-evolved, belief-conditioned
planner converts it into dispatching decisions through candi-
date screening and short rollouts under a fixed, non-learned
scenario generator. Experiments show that combining a low-
dimensional LLM-based forecast of exogenous uncertainty
with exact scheduling dynamics supports effective anticipa-
tory scheduling without learning the complete shop tran-
sition or training a task-specific neural dispatching policy.
Future work includes enriching the arrival belief with more
job-level information and evaluating AWMP on real-world
and more diverse arrival processes. We will also explore
more efficient forecasting models to further reduce online
inference latency. More broadly, the structured world-model
framework may extend to robust optimization and stochastic
programming settings.
Our study has several limitations. First, AWMP represents
future arrivals through a compact early–late workload belief
rather than detailed job-level predictions. This design keeps
the forecasting problem low-dimensional and supports sta-
ble online calibration, avoiding the difficulty of predicting
the complete future arrival stream. However, richer repre-
sentations that additionally capture job attributes, such as
processing routes, priorities, or machine requirements, may
provide more informative guidance when future workloads
are highly heterogeneous. Second, our experiments construct
dynamic instances from standard JSSP and FJSP benchmarks
under four controlled arrival patterns. These patterns cover
several representative forms of temporal variation and allow
controlled evaluation across different arrival dynamics. How-
ever, real manufacturing systems may exhibit more diverse
arrival behaviors and operational conditions, and evaluation
on real scheduling traces would provide a broader assessment
of the framework. Finally, AWMP uses a pretrained LLM for
online belief updates. Belief reuse and surprise-triggered re-
fresh substantially limit the associated inference overhead.
Nevertheless, the runtime of belief updates is still affected
by the latency of the underlying model, and more efficient
forecasting models could further improve online deployment.
