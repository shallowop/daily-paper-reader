A Semi-Analytical Inference Framework for Structural Causal Models
Tatiana Boura∗, Robert Peharz
Graz University of Technology
Abstract
Structural Causal Models (SCMs) provide a principled frame-
work for observational, interventional, and counterfactual rea-
soning, all of which rely on probabilistic inference. Existing
frameworks either transform SCMs into alternative tractable
representations or rely on approximate inference with expres-
sive generative models. We instead perform inference using a
message passing scheme directly on the SCM by representing
structural mechanisms and probability densities in a com-
mon finite-dimensional function space. Forward propagation
combines a numerical realization of the Law of the Uncon-
scious Statistician, while propagated densities are recovered
by spline projection from LOTUS moments. The same repre-
sentation naturally yields backward message passing for con-
ditioning, providing a unified framework for causal inference.
Using tensor-product B-spline bases, the resulting algorithm
expresses inference through numerical quadrature and linear
algebra without explicitly constructing conditional distribu-
tions. Experiments on randomly generated continuous SCMs
demonstrate accurate approximation of Monte Carlo reference
solutions and systematic improvement with increasing basis
resolution.
1
Introduction
The notion of causality has been central to philosophical
discourse since antiquity. Structural Causal Models (SCMs)
provide one of the most widely used mathematical frame-
works for causal reasoning (Pearl 2009). Under the assump-
tion that the modeled system is causally closed, uncertainty
is confined to exogenous variables and endogenous variables
are related deterministically (Peters, Janzing, and Schölkopf
2017). SCMs support three levels of reasoning, known as
Pearl’s causal hierarchy: association, intervention, and coun-
terfactuals (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). Although these three
query types differ in their semantics, each ultimately requires
evaluating probability distributions induced by the SCM. Ef-
ficient probabilistic inference therefore forms the compu-
tational core of causal reasoning. Observational queries re-
quire probabilistic inference in the observational distribution,
i.e. the pushforward distribution of the exogenous distribu-
tion under the hierarchical causal mechanism, interventional
queries require inference after modifying the structural equa-
tions, and counterfactual reasoning combines conditioning

with hypothetical interventions (Han, Chen, and Darwiche
2023; Pearl 2009). Specifically, counterfactual inference can
be formulated using a twin SCM or, equivalently, an abduct-
intervene-infer procedure (Pearl 2009).
While much of the causal modeling literature focuses
on causal discovery, identification, and effect estimation
from data (Peters, Janzing, and Schölkopf 2017), compar-
atively less attention has been devoted to efficiently evalu-
ating queries in a given SCM (Darwiche 2022; Wang and
Kwiatkowska 2023; Zečević et al. 2021; Pawlowski, Castro,
and Glocker 2020). These directions are complementary:
discovery and identification determine the causal model and
identifiable effects, whereas causal inference assumes a given
SCM and answers causal queries. This setting naturally arises
when the model is provided by expert knowledge or a pre-
ceding learning stage.
Causal inference appears closely related to inference in
Bayesian networks (BNs) (Koller and Friedman 2009), where
algorithms such as variable elimination, belief propagation,
and junction-tree message passing enable efficient exact and
approximate inference (Pearl 1982; Zhang and Poole 1996;
Wainwright and Jordan 2008). Since every acyclic SCM in-
duces a BN, it is natural to ask whether these methods apply
directly. However, BN inference requires explicit conditional
distributions, whereas SCMs define them only implicitly
through deterministic structural mechanisms and exogenous
noise. Thus, inference depends both on the graph structure
and the representation of the structural mechanisms.
Several approaches have addressed this challenge. One
line of work compiles SCMs into alternative representa-
tions that support tractable inference, including tractable cir-
cuits and related representations (Darwiche 2022; Wang and
Kwiatkowska 2023; Zečević et al. 2021). Another employs
expressive structural mechanisms based on deep generative
models (Pawlowski, Castro, and Glocker 2020), where infer-
ence is typically performed approximately. Existing methods
therefore either transform the SCM into a tractable represen-
tation or perform inference approximately using sampling or
learned generative models.
In this work, we instead perform inference directly on
the original SCM representation. We formulate message
passing in a common finite-dimensional function space for
structural mechanisms and probability densities. Forward
propagation evaluates the Law of the Unconscious Statis-

tician (LOTUS) integrals numerically and recovers propa-
gated densities through spline projection, yielding a semi-
analytical inference procedure that avoids SCM compila-
tion and sampling-based inference. The same representation
naturally extends to backward message passing, providing
a unified framework for observational, interventional, and
counterfactual inference.
We instantiate the framework using tensor-product B-
spline bases, whose approximation properties, numerical sta-
bility, and efficient quadrature algorithms (de Boor 2001;
Schumaker 2007; Cottrell, Hughes, and Bazilevs 2009) al-
low inference to be expressed through linear algebra and
numerical quadrature while representing highly nonlinear
mechanisms. Specifically, we model both densities and struc-
tural mechanisms using tensor-product B-splines. Restricting
structural mechanisms is not itself new (Hoyer et al. 2008;
Bühlmann, Peters, and Ernest 2014; Morville and Park 2025;
Zhang and Hyvärinen 2009). Previous work, however, does
so primarily to facilitate causal discovery or parameter esti-
mation. In contrast, we employ a restricted mechanism fam-
ily to perform efficient inference in fully specified continuous
SCMs. The main contributions of this work are as follows:
• We formulate probabilistic inference in continuous SCMs
as message passing in a common finite-dimensional ap-
proximate functional space, avoiding the explicit con-
struction of the distributions induced by nonlinear struc-
tural mechanisms.
• We derive forward and backward message-passing oper-
ators based on LOTUS and spline projection, reducing
inference to linear algebra and numerical quadrature in
this common function space.
• We instantiate the framework using tensor-product B-
spline representations for both structural mechanisms
and probability densities, resulting in a practical semi-
analytical inference algorithm for continuous SCMs.

Preliminaries
We briefly review the concepts required throughout the paper
and establish the notation.
2.1
Structural Causal Models
A Structural Causal Model (SCM) (Pearl 2009) is a tuple
M = (U, V, F, P(U)),
where
• U is the set of exogenous variables;
• V is the set of endogenous variables;
• F = {fX}X∈V is a collection of structural mechanisms,
X = fX(Pa(X), UX),
where each endogenous variable is a deterministic func-
tion of its endogenous parents, Pa(X), and its associated
exogenous variable UX. Throughout this work, we as-
sume mutually independent exogenous variables and one
exogenous variable per endogenous variable.
• P(U) is the joint probability distribution of the exogenous
variables.
The structural mechanisms induce a directed acyclic graph
(DAG) G whose vertices are the endogenous variables and
whose edges encode parent-child relationships. Under the
above assumptions, the SCM satisfies the causal Markov
property (Peters, Janzing, and Schölkopf 2017), implying
the factorization P(V) = Q
X∈V P(X | Pa(X)).
SCMs support three principal classes of inference
queries (Pearl and Mackenzie 2018). Observational queries
compute probabilities conditioned on observed variables. In-
terventional queries evaluate the distribution resulting from
modifying one or more structural mechanisms via the do(·)
operator. Counterfactual queries reason about hypothetical
outcomes by combining observed evidence with interven-
tions on the same SCM instance. Although these queries
differ in their semantics, they all ultimately require efficient
probabilistic inference. For an illustrative representation of
these queries, we refer the reader to the Appendix.
2.2
Message Passing on Graphical Models
Exact probabilistic inference in graphical models is com-
monly performed by message-passing algorithms, which ex-
ploit conditional independence to decompose global infer-
ence into local computations (Cowell et al. 1999; Koller and
Friedman 2009). Rather than manipulating the full joint dis-
tribution directly, neighboring regions exchange messages
that summarize the influence of the remaining variables.
For tree-structured graphical models, exact marginals are
computed by the sum-product algorithm, which propagates
messages along the graph edges (Pearl 1982). For non-tree
models, exact inference first constructs a junction tree whose
nodes correspond to variable cliques. Messages are then ex-
changed between neighboring cliques through their separator
sets using local multiplication, marginalization, and normal-
ization (Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter 1988; Jensen, Olesen,
and Andersen 1990; Shenoy and Shafer 1990).
Let T = (C, E) denote a junction tree, where each node
C ∈C is a clique and each edge (Ci, Cj) ∈E connects
neighboring cliques. The separator associated with an edge
is Sij = Ci ∩Cj. Each clique stores a joint state, while
separator messages ensure consistency between neighboring
cliques. After the collect and distribute passes, calibrated
clique states agree on their separator marginals.
Although every acyclic SCM induces a Bayesian net-
work (Koller and Friedman 2009) satisfying the causal
Markov property (Section 2.1), its local conditional distri-
butions are not represented explicitly. Instead, they are de-
fined implicitly by deterministic structural mechanisms and
exogenous variables. Consequently, the local probability fac-
tors required by standard message-passing algorithms are
unavailable. The proposed framework overcomes this limi-
tation by propagating probability measures directly through
structural mechanisms rather than explicit conditional den-
sities. By combining numerical realizations of the LOTUS
with least-squares recovery in a common functional space,
the resulting algorithm retains the junction-tree message-
passing paradigm while avoiding the explicit construction of
conditional probability distributions.

3
Method
This section presents the message-passing framework for in-
ference in fully specified SCMs. Whereas causal discovery
and identification determine the causal model and identifi-
able causal effects, we address the complementary problem
of efficiently answering causal queries. The algorithm op-
erates directly on structural mechanisms, avoiding the ex-
plicit construction of conditional densities. Inference pro-
ceeds in two stages. The forward pass propagates probabil-
ity measures through the SCM to construct marginals and
clique states for observational and interventional inference.
The backward pass then propagates likelihood information
through these states to incorporate evidence, enabling con-
ditional and counterfactual inference.

3.2
Forward Message Passing
The forward pass processes the SCM in topological order.
For each endogenous variable, it propagates the parent mea-
sure through the structural mechanism to recover the child
marginal. Whenever required to preserve downstream de-
pendencies, it additionally constructs joint spline states over
selected variable scopes. After forward propagation, scopes
that are strict subsets of another stored scope are discarded,
and the remaining maximal scopes become the cliques of the
junction tree used during backward inference.
Rather than explicitly constructing the pushforward den-
sity induced by a structural mechanism, the method charac-
terizes the propagated measure through its moments. By the
LOTUS, these moments can be evaluated for any measur-
able test function φ (Pollard 2002). Thus, for an endogenous
variable X with structural mechanism fX,

Interventions.
Interventional inference requires no ad-
ditional message-passing operator. Under an intervention
do(X = x⋆), the structural mechanism of X is replaced
by the constant assignment X := x⋆, yielding a modified
SCM. The forward pass is then reapplied, reusing the states
of non-descendants and recomputing only those affected by
the intervention. Since a point intervention induces a Dirac
measure, the intervened variable is propagated deterministi-
cally rather than represented as a spline density.
3.3
Backward Message Passing
The backward pass incorporates observations by propagating
likelihoods through the junction tree (Section 2.2). Starting
from the prior clique states computed during the forward
pass, neighboring cliques exchange likelihood-ratio mes-
sages that update these priors into posterior clique states.
Whenever a message is propagated through a structural
mechanism, the corresponding conditionaldensity isunavail-
able explicitly. Instead, likelihoods are propagated through
the structural mechanism via a local backward operator.

Counterfactuals.
Counterfactual inference follows the
abduct-intervene-infer procedure. During the abduction step,
the local backward operator is applied while retaining the rel-
evant exogenous variables in the propagated state, yielding
their posterior distributions conditioned on the observations
(abduct). The intervention is then applied by replacing the
corresponding structural mechanisms (intervene). Finally,
the modified SCM is evaluated using the abducted exogenous
distribution (infer). This preserves the shared exogenous re-
alization between the factual and counterfactual worlds.

coefficients and uses a QM-point quadrature grid. The im-

via tensor contractions and Kronecker-structured operators,
avoiding explicit tensor-product mass matrices.

Results
The main experiments consider tree-structured SCMs and
DAGs where approximately 20% of the nodes have two par-
ents, reflecting the exponential tensor-product scaling of the
proposed representation. We therefore focus on the regime
where exact tensor-product inference remains computation-
ally practical, leaving sparse and low-rank extensions to
future work. Throughout, the number of quadrature points
scales with the basis resolution; eight points per basis func-
tion were found best in preliminary experiments. For each
graph size, we generate five random SCMs.
Forward pass.
Figure 1 reports the percentage of endoge-
nous variables whose domain-normalized expectation error
exceeds 0.5%. As expected, increasing the number of basis
functions consistently improves the approximation. With 32
basis functions, no high-error variables are observed, even for
SCMs with up to 104 endogenous variables. For two-parent
SCMs, computational cost limited experiments to graphs of
at most 500 endogenous variables, and to only 20 variables
when using 128 basis functions. For tree-structured SCMs,
Figure 2 compares the runtime of the proposed method with
the high-accuracy MC reference. Our approach is approxi-
mately one order of magnitude faster while maintaining com-
parable accuracy. Finally, Figure 3 reports the mean Hellinger
distance to the MC reference, together with one standard
deviation across experiments. The error decreases from ap-
proximately 0.2 with 32 basis functions toward zero as the
basis is refined, demonstrating convergence of the inferred
densities.
Backward pass.
For backward evaluation, we consider
smaller SCMs than in the forward experiments, since condi-
tioning evidence at leaf nodes becomes less informative for
distant ancestors. Validating backward inference requires a
different MC reference, as rejection sampling becomes im-
practical under conditioning. Instead, we generate ancestral
samples by forward simulation and approximate condition-
ing using Gaussian kernel weights. Conditional expectations
are estimated via self-normalized importance sampling.

three observations, maximum clique size three, and maxi-

5
Conclusion
This work introduced a message-passing framework for fully
specified SCMs that performs observational, interventional,
and counterfactual inference within a unified B-spline rep-
resentation. By combining forward propagation of probabil-
ity measures with backward propagation of likelihoods, the
method avoids explicit construction of pushforward and con-
ditional densities. Experiments demonstrate accurate proba-
bility densities and expectations while scaling to large SCMs,
with errors decreasing consistently as the spline resolu-
tion increases. Future work will investigate low-rank ten-
sor approximations and adaptive basis refinement for higher-
dimensional models with larger parent sets.
