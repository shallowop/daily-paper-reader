# Accelerated Benders decomposition for bi-objective mixed integer programming

Abstract 

We investigate bi-objective mixed integer programming to which Benders de-composition can be applied, a widely used technique for exploiting problem structures in real-world applications. Our study focuses on a sustainable sup-ply chain network design problem that aims to minimise both economic and environmental objectives. To address the bi-objective nature, we employ the weighted sum method, which requires solving similar problems repeatedly with different weight values. To tackle this, we propose a straightforward acceleration technique that reuses Benders cuts generated in previous it-erations of the weighted sum method. This approach enables the Benders decomposition master problem to be initialised with pre-generated cuts. Our computational experiments demonstrate that reusing these cuts significantly reduces computing time. 

Keywords: Benders decomposition, Bi-objective optimisation, Mixed integer programming 

1. Introduction 

In the diverse fields of engineering and industry, decision-making pro-cesses often involve conflicting objectives, giving rise to multi-objective opti-misation problems. Such problems require finding a set of trade-off solutions,

where the improvement of one objective cannot be achieved without a de-terioration in another objective. Furthermore, real-world applications are often large-scale, and they consequently pose significant computational lim-itations, making them challenging to solve within reasonable computation time. One approach that has demonstrated its effectiveness in the single-objective domain is Benders decomposition (BD) initially proposed by Ben-ders (1962). Based on our current understanding, only a limited number of studies have applied the classical BD framework to the bi-objective setting. In the domain of scalarisation-based methods, Mardan et al. (2019) com-bined weighted-sum scalarisation with Benders decomposition to address a bi-objective green closed-loop supply chain network design problem. Their acceleration strategies focus on warm-starting and problem-specific enhance-ments, while each scalarised problem is solved independently. Parragh et al. (2021) applied Benders’ idea to address the bi-objective stochastic facility location problem. The researchers integrated BD into the branch-and-bound scheme to compute the lower bound set. Their problem setting required only optimality cuts, which affected only one of the two objectives consid-ered. Dowson et al. (2022) studied bi-objective multistage stochastic linear programming problems and combined weighted-sum scalarisation with an L-shaped decomposition method. Their methodology exploits the continuous linear programming structure and solves each scalarised problem indepen-dently. Raith et al. (2025) proposed a theoretical framework for applying Benders decomposition to bi-objective linear programming and integrated it with the bi-objective simplex algorithm to generate a complete set of efficient solutions. One potential concern in employing a scalarisation-based method is the repeated need to solve similar problems with different weight values. There-fore, we propose a simple approach to reuse the generated Benders cuts, thereby accelerating the subsequently solved problems.

(2025) develop a theoretical frame-work for applying Benders decomposition to bi-objective linear programming by integrating it with a simplex algorithm, enabling the reuse of cuts within a single search process. In contrast, our method tackles mixed integer pro-gramming problems, where such integration with the simplex method is not directly applicable. Instead, we iteratively find optimal extreme points within a dichotomic search framework, which enables the reuse of generated cuts in subsequent searches and gives rise to a process resembling a branch-and-cut scheme. Furthermore, the “weighted optimality cuts” in Raith et al. (2025) should be generated from two “extreme” weight cases λ = 0 and λ = 1 by solv-ing the respective Benders subproblems. On the other hand, our approach can generate Benders cuts for a new weight λ from different weights, not nec-essarily either λ = 0 or λ = 1 , because we store the bases Bp that can yield a vaild optimality cut for any given weight. While this dichotomic search systematically explores the objective space, it cannot guarantee finding all efficient solutions of the bi-objective mixed integer programming problems. Instead, it produces a representative subset of efficient solutions correspond-ing to the weights considered. 

3. Computational Study 

We evaluate our accelerated Benders decomposition algorithm (AB) in comparison with the classical Benders decomposition approach (CB). In CB, each weighted sum problem is solved independently from scratch, and no cuts, either optimality or feasibility, are reused across iterations. By contrast, AB reuses optimality cuts generated in previous weighted sum problems. To inte-grate BD (both CB and AB) into the bi-objective dichotomic search scheme, we first obtain two extreme solutions using lexicographic optimisation. The weight is then computed based on the objective values, following the method described in Aneja and Nair (1979). For both CB and AB, optimality and feasibility cuts are generated during each iteration of the search. For AB, the optimal bases obtained from each subproblem are stored throughout the dichotomic search for use in subsequent iterations. From the second iteration onward, the previously generated optimality cuts are re-evaluated under the current weight. A cut is considered violated if its evaluated value exceeds the current master problem value. Among the violated cuts, a fixed number are selected in ascending order of violation and added to the master prob-lem. This strategy was adopted after preliminary experiments comparing 5different approaches, including the most violated optimality cuts and ran-domly selected optimality cuts. The number of pre-generated cuts added at each iteration was determined empirically through preliminary experiments. Cuts were increased in increments of 10, up to 100, and 40 cuts per iteration were selected (with a maximum of 40 cuts checked for violation), as this pro-vided the best computational performance in terms of total solution time. Feasibility cuts were not reused across iterations, since initial experiments indicated that their reuse did not have a noticeable impact on computational performance. 

3.1. Test instances 

We test the proposed scheme on a bi-objective mixed integer sustainable supply chain network design (SCND) problem adapted from Eskandarpour et al. (2021). The original setting is scaled down by reducing the number of facilities and products. The network comprises four layers: suppliers ( I), plants ( J), distribution centres ( K), and customers ( L), defined on a directed graph G = ( V, A ) with 

V = I ∪ J ∪ K ∪ L. Products flow sequentially to meet deterministic demand. Plants and distribution centres (DCs) may use regular or green technolo-gies, and transport options between facilities include conventional and green modes. The objectives are: (1) minimise total logistics costs (facility, pro-duction, and transport) and (2) minimise CO 2 emissions. Decisions involve facility locations, technology levels, transport modes, and product flows. We generate ten small SCND instances, divided into two size classes. An instance is denoted as (|I|, |J|, |K|, |L|)_index , where |I|, |J|, |K|, and |L| are the numbers of candidate suppliers, plants, DCs, and customers, respectively, and index distinguishes instances of the same size. For all instances, |I| = |J|,

|K| = 2 |I|, and |L| = 5 |I|, with two technologies and one product. For conciseness, the complete mathematical formulations are provided in Appendix A. 

3.2. Experimental results 

All experiments were conducted on a machine equipped with an Intel Core i5-8250U CPU @ 1.60 GHz (8 cores), 16 GB of RAM. CPLEX is em-ployed to solve the mathematical model. The dichotomic search algorithm is implemented in the programming language Julia. We observe that in some weighted sum problems CB and AB yield dif-ferent individual objective values, while still producing the same weighted 6sum objective value. When these discrepancies affect the weights calculated during the dichotomic search, resulting in different search paths for CB and AB, we perform additional experiments using the weights generated by AB to enable a fair comparison. The corresponding results are reported in Table B.6 in Appendix B. Table 1 presents the following results: the number of efficient solutions ob-tained and the total number of iterations in the dichotomic search (#ES/Iter), the number of added pre-generated optimality cuts (#Precut) in AB, the to-tal number of feasibility and optimality cuts (#Fcuts, #Ocuts) generated during the dichotomic search, the improvement achieved by AB (denoted as imp, in %), and the average total CPU time over five runs (denoted as CPUT, in seconds) required to complete the search. Table 1 shows that the numbers of feasibility and optimality cuts decrease significantly by adding pre-generated cuts from previous iterations. This nat-urally leads to a substantial reduction in total computing time. For instances such as (2,2,4,10)_2 and (2,2,4,10)_4, where no improvement in CPU time was observed, the time spent storing and retrieving cuts was a contributing factor. Table 2 provides an example illustrating this behaviour. Table 2 compares CB and AB for instance (2,2,4,10)_2 across each itera-tion of the dichotomic search. In addition to the previously defined columns (#Precut, #Fcuts, #Ocuts), the column (Weight) indicates the scalarisation weight used in the current iteration, while (subT and mpT) report the CPU time spent on solving the subproblem and master problem, respectively. While pre-generated cuts reduce the number of cuts and CPU time, AB without them required more time due to the extra computation needed to obtain and store the optimal basis, as reflected in the column mpT. Furthermore, we observe that the type of cut has a greater impact on performance than the number of cuts added, as shown in Table B.7 in Ap-pendix B. In the final iteration with weight 0.9071, adding a single cut drasti-cally reduced both the number of generated cuts and the computation time. Recall that we simply choose, at most, a predetermined number of violated Benders cuts from the pool of bases obtained from previous dichotomic search iterations. Recent studies have demonstrated that adding “stronger” Benders cuts can enhance the performance of the Benders algorithm. For example, Seo et al. (2022) proposed a reformulation technique of the Benders subprob-lem to identify “closer” Benders cuts to the relatively feasible solution. It is straightforward to implement a more “advanced cut selection” scheme with the proposed approach. Nonetheless, we would like to present this line of 7investigation as an interesting future research topic.

4. Conclusion 

This study examined the application of Benders decomposition to bi-objective mixed integer programming problems, focusing on sustainable sup-ply chain network design. To address the bi-objective nature, we employed the weighted-sum method, which involves solving a sequence of problems with varying weights. Recognising the computational burden of this approach, we proposed an accelerated Benders decomposition technique that reuses cuts generated in previous iterations. Computational results demonstrated that 8our method significantly reduces both total solution time and the number of generated cuts compared to the classical approach. Furthermore, our find-ings indicate that the type of reused cut has a greater impact on performance than their quantity. This suggests a promising direction for future research: investigating strategies for selecting cuts or developing adaptive mechanisms to balance preprocessing effort with overall efficiency. Another direction for future work is to test the method on simpler problem instances to better understand its behaviour and performance characteristics.
