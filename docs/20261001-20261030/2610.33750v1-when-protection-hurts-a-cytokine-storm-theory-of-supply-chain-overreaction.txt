When Protection Hurts: A Cytokine Storm Theory of Supply
Chain Overreaction
Gurkirat Wadhwa
Industrial Engineering and Operations Research (IEOR), IIT Bombay

Abstract. Supply chain resilience research has traditionally viewed defensive actions such as safety
stock, backup suppliers, expedited transportation, and emergency sourcing as beneficial. However,
disruptions such as the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2021 semiconductor shortage, and the Suez Canal
blockage highlight how synchronized hoarding, duplicate ordering, and capacity reservation can amplify
congestion and operational costs, sometimes worsening the original disruption. Motivated by this obser-
vation, we develop a decision-theoretic framework in which Protective Response Intensity (PRI) is an
endogenous decision variable. We introduce Response-Induced Loss (RIL) to capture costs generated
by defensive actions and characterize a unique optimal response that balances disruption mitigation
against self-induced losses. We derive a closed-form critical response threshold beyond which RIL ex-
ceeds the original disruption intensity, a phenomenon termed a *Supply Chain Cytokine Storm*. We
further propose the Cytokine Storm Index (CSI), measuring response-induced loss relative to the origi-
nal disruption, and discuss its potential calibration using operational data. Extending the framework to
interconnected supply networks, we derive a spectral stability condition distinguishing bounded decen-
tralized responses from network-wide propagation. Numerical experiments show that optimal responses
remain below the storm threshold, whereas proportional response heuristics can exceed it. Network re-
sponses converge or diverge according to the derived stability condition. Overall, our findings highlight
that supply chain resilience depends not only on mitigating disruptions but also on keeping defensive
responses proportionate to the shocks they are intended to address.
Keywords: Supply chain resilience; behavioral overreaction; response-induced loss; network cascades;
convex optimization.
1
Introduction
Supply chain resilience is traditionally viewed through a simple principle: greater preparedness leads to
lower disruption losses. Consequently, firms invest in safety stock, backup suppliers, flexible capacity, expe-
dited transportation, and emergency sourcing to improve their ability to withstand unexpected disruptions
[1,2,3]. Implicit in this perspective is the assumption that defensive actions are inherently beneficial and that
increasing preparedness can only improve resilience.
Recent large-scale disruptions suggest that this assumption is incomplete. During the COVID-19 pan-
demic, the global semiconductor shortage, and the Suez Canal blockage, firms responded to uncertainty
with synchronized hoarding, duplicate ordering, capacity reservation, and aggressive expediting. Although
these actions were individually rational, their collective effect amplified congestion, prolonged shortages,
and increased operational costs well beyond those caused by the original disruption itself [12,13,14,16,15].
These episodes indicate that supply chains may suffer not only from external shocks, but also from excessive
defensive responses.
This phenomenon closely resembles a cytokine storm in immunology, where the immune system’s response
to a pathogen becomes so aggressive that it causes more damage than the infection itself [8,9]. Motivated
by this analogy, we investigate an analogous question for supply chains: Can protective actions themselves
become the dominant source of operational loss?. This analogy is used for motivating the intuition behind
the proposed framework.
To address this question, we model a firm’s Protective Response Intensity (PRI) as a decision variable
rather than a fixed parameter and explicitly account for the costs generated by defensive actions through
the notion of Response-Induced Loss (RIL). Building on this formulation, we introduce the Cytokine Storm

Index (CSI), which quantifies the proportion of loss attributable to the response itself, derive a closed-

interconnected supply networks by establishing a spectral condition governing whether defensive responses
remain localized or propagate throughout the network.
Our work differs from existing resilience and behavioral supply chain literature in three important re-
spects. First, unlike traditional resilience models that treat preparedness as uniformly beneficial [1,2,3],
we explicitly model protective actions as optimization decisions that balance mitigation benefits against
self-induced operational costs. Second, whereas the bullwhip-effect literature demonstrates how ordering
variability propagates through supply chains [4,5,6], we derive a closed-form analytical condition identifying
when defensive responses themselves become counterproductive. Third, by extending the analysis to inter-
connected supply networks, we show that excessive responses are not solely firm-level phenomena but are
fundamentally shaped by network interactions, linking resilience analysis with spectral methods commonly
used in network science [10,11].
The main contributions of this paper are as follows. We formulate a decision-theoretic framework that
models Protective Response Intensity (PRI) as an endogenous decision variable and introduces Response-
Induced Loss (RIL) to explicitly capture the operational costs generated by defensive actions. Building on this
framework, we establish the existence and uniqueness of the optimal response, derive its comparative statics,
characterize a closed-form threshold separating beneficial protection from excessive defensive behavior, and
propose the Cytokine Storm Index (CSI) as a quantitative measure of self-induced operational loss. Finally, we
extend the analysis to interconnected supply networks by deriving a spectral stability condition governing
the propagation of defensive responses and demonstrate the resulting threshold and cascade phenomena
through numerical experiments.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature. Section 3
presents the proposed model, Section 4 develops the theoretical analysis, Section 5 extends the framework
to interconnected supply networks, Section 6 presents the numerical experiments, and Section 7 concludes
the paper.

Related Work
Resilience research typically models a firm’s or network’s ability to absorb, adapt to, and recover from disrup-
tions, with defensive investment entering as a parameter that improves absorption or shortens recovery time
[1,2,3]. Tang [1] catalogs mitigation strategies—postponement, flexible supply base, economic incentives—as
levers firms should pull as far as budget allows; Christopher and Peck [2] and Sheffi [3] similarly frame re-
silience as a capacity to be built up, with redundancy and flexibility appearing as unambiguously protective.
These models describe a single firm acting against a well-defined risk reasonably well, but they share an im-
plicit assumption that resilience-enhancing investment is weakly beneficial, leaving no room for the response
itself to become a source of loss.
A second stream shows that overreaction can emerge even without any individual decision-maker be-
having irrationally. The bullwhip effect literature shows that order variance amplifies upstream, driven by
demand-signal processing, order batching, price fluctuation, and rationing, even under individually optimal
ordering policies [4]. Laboratory studies using the beer distribution game show that human subjects sys-
tematically over-order relative to the optimal benchmark, especially just after a shock [5,6]. This literature
establishes overreaction as real and measurable, but characterizes it indirectly, through order-variance de-
compositions under a fixed ordering policy, rather than by asking when a chosen level of responsiveness
becomes counterproductive; no closed-form threshold analogous to ours appears in this line of work. Nor
does this literature offer a metric comparable to CSI: standard resilience diagnostics such as fill rate, time-
to-recovery, or bullwhip order-variance ratios summarize how well a system absorbs or reacts to a shock, but
to the best of our knowledge, these studies do not isolate the fraction of loss that the response itself creates.
CSI is intended to fill exactly that gap, and comparing it empirically against these established metrics is a
natural next step we flag in Section 6.
A third stream draws on immune-inspired methods and network cascades. Artificial Immune Systems have
long served as metaheuristics for supplier selection, scheduling, and routing [7], and terms like “organizational
immunity” occasionally appear in the practitioner literature as informal metaphors for adaptive capacity,
stopping short of formalizing an overactive response. Separately, network science and epidemiology use

Taken together, these streams motivate the need for a unified decision-theoretic perspective. While ex-
isting studies have substantially advanced our understanding of disruption propagation, resilience strategies,
and behavioral amplification, relatively less attention has been devoted to characterizing when protective ac-
tions themselves become counterproductive. To the best of our knowledge, we are not aware of an analytical
framework that explicitly identifies a critical threshold between beneficial protection and self-induced oper-
ational loss, while also examining how such behavior propagates over supply networks. Section 3 develops a
framework to address these questions.

Model
3.1
Setup
Consider a firm facing a disruption of intensity D ≥0, representing, for example, a normalized capacity loss,
demand shock, or lead-time increase expressed on a common cost-equivalent scale. To mitigate the disruption,
the firm selects a Protective Response Intensity (PRI), denoted by R ≥0, which represents the aggregate
level of defensive actions such as increasing safety stock, expediting shipments, reserving transportation
capacity, and engaging emergency suppliers. While these actions reduce the losses directly attributable to
the disruption, they also incur operational and coordination costs. Consequently, the firm’s response creates
a trade-off between disruption mitigation and response-induced costs. Figure 1 illustrates the framework
describing the model.

A Supply Chain Cytokine Storm occurs when the response-induced loss exceeds the original disruption

realized once the response is in place; the two coincide only when µ = 0. Moreover, this does not imply that

simultaneously creating even greater operational losses through excessive defensive actions. Thus, the storm
condition captures disproportionate response intensity relative to the size of the original disruption, rather
than inadequate disruption mitigation.
3.2
Optimal response
Given the loss function in (3), a rational firm seeks to determine the level of Protective Response Inten-
sity that minimizes its total expected loss. The resulting optimization balances the mitigation benefits of
defensive actions against the operational costs they generate, thereby identifying the response that achieves
the best trade-off between preparedness and overreaction. To highlight the value of this optimal policy, we
compare it with a simple behavioral benchmark in which firms respond proportionally to the observed dis-
ruption, i.e., R = αD for a fixed responsiveness parameter α > 0. This heuristic represents panic-driven or
overly aggressive ordering behavior frequently associated with the bullwhip effect. Section 4 characterizes
the optimal response analytically and establishes the conditions under which both decision rules enter the
cytokine storm region.

Network Extension
The analysis in the previous sections considers a single decision maker that chooses its protective response
independently. In real supply chains, however, firms rarely operate in isolation. A firm’s defensive actions—
such as inventory hoarding, emergency sourcing, or aggressive capacity reservation can alter the operating
environment faced by its suppliers and customers, often inducing them to increase their own defensive
responses. Consequently, overreaction is not merely an individual optimization problem but also a network
phenomenon in which local decisions may propagate throughout the supply chain.
To capture this mechanism, we extend the proposed framework from a single firm to a network of
interacting firms. Rather than developing a full multi-agent game in which firms jointly optimize their
responses, which will be a part of future work, we isolate the propagation mechanism through which protective
responses spread across supply chain relationships. The network model is therefore a reduced-form behavioral
propagation model rather than a strategic equilibrium. The complete multi-agent game analysis of multiple
firms is an interesting future direction.

When Protection Hurts: A Cytokine Storm Theory of Supply Chain Overreaction
11
In interconnected supply chains, resilience depends not only on how individual firms respond to disrup-
tions but also on how those responses propagate through the network. Even a well-intentioned local reaction
can escalate into a system-wide overreaction when amplified by highly connected partners. Consequently,
managers should promote firm-level resilience initiatives with mechanisms that limit behavioral contagion,
such as information sharing, coordinated decision-making, and greater supply chain visibility.
Taken together, the numerical results are consistent with the theoretical analysis. The optimal response
remains below the cytokine storm threshold across the tested parameter range, whereas simple proportional
response heuristics readily enter the storm region. At the network level, defensive responses either remain
contained or propagate into system-wide overreaction depending on the spectral stability condition of The-
orem 1. These findings suggest that disruption impacts can arise not only from the external shock itself but
also from the collective response of firms. This observation is consistent with events following the Suez Canal
blockage, where widespread inventory building, rerouting, and capacity reservation contributed to congestion
and prolonged recovery across global supply chains [15].
6.4
Managerial Implications
The framework yields four managerial implications: (i) the firms should optimize rather than maximize defen-
sive actions, since marginal response costs eventually dominate mitigation benefits; (ii) monitor Rc =
p
D/θ
and CSI as warning measures of disproportionate response; (iii) evaluate resilience using both disruption-
induced and response-induced losses; and (iv) coordinate responses across supply-network partners, since
behavioral contagion can amplify individually reasonable actions into network-wide overreaction.
6.5
Limitations
There are several limitations. First, the reduced-form parameters θ, µ, and β summarize overreaction, miti-
gation effectiveness, and behavioral contagion but are not estimated from industry-specific data; estimating
them from historical disruption events is an important next step. Second, CSI is normalized using the
original disruption intensity D rather than the mitigated exogenous loss, providing a response-independent
benchmark; alternative normalizations and comparisons with fill rate, recovery time, and bullwhip measures
would further clarify its practical value. Third, the network extension is a reduced-form behavioral propa-
gation model rather than a strategic equilibrium, and its numerical illustration uses a single representative
network realization rather than a systematic sweep across topologies. Finally, the network extension assumes
static topology and exogenous disruption characteristics; dynamic supply networks with jointly optimized
defensive responses is an interesting direction for future research.
7
Conclusion
We develop a decision-theoretic framework for analyzing when protective actions in supply chains become
counterproductive. By introducing the concepts of Protective Response Intensity (PRI), Response-Induced
Loss (RIL), and the Cytokine Storm Index (CSI), the proposed framework explicitly distinguishes losses
caused by external disruptions from those generated by excessive defensive responses. The analysis establishes
a unique optimal response, derives a closed-form threshold separating proportionate and excessive defensive
actions, and extends the framework to interconnected supply networks through a spectral stability condition
governing the propagation of behavioral responses. The numerical experiments illustrate the theoretical
results. The optimal response remains below the cytokine storm threshold, whereas simple proportional
response heuristics readily enter the storm region. The network experiments further illustrate how behavioral
responses either remain localized or propagate into system-wide overreaction depending on the underlying
network interactions. Overall, the proposed framework suggests that effective supply chain resilience depends
not only on mitigating external disruptions but also on regulating the intensity of defensive responses.
