Abstract
Industrial spatial decisions are assembled across systems that share
neither a reference frame nor an owner, and analysts spend substan-
tial effort reconciling those systems rather than reasoning about
space. This paper presents ANASSA, a reference architecture inte-
grating structured reasoning, multi-agent workflow orchestration,
authoritative spatial grounding, and feedback-driven execution, and
reports its instantiation in an operating supply-chain deployment.
The architecture places typed data contracts at agent boundaries,
validates spatial claims against a certified authoritative foundation,
and enforces two blocking human-in-the-loop approvals inside a
six-step cognitive loop. The deployment covers multi-hazard risk as-
sessment on an ocean-and-inland freight lane, including cold-chain
excursion detection over container telemetry. Across a three-month
proof of concept (POC) with three implementation iterations, three
deployment outcomes are reported: 25% improvement in data qual-
ity, 50% reduction in spatial workflow runtime, and 60% lower deci-
sion latency relative to the preceding manual workflow. Absolute
deployment-scale quantities and baseline counts are confidential;
reported percentages are observed relative changes. These are un-
controlled deployment observations, not controlled experiments.
We therefore specify instrumentation and ablation protocols for
mechanism-level evaluation.

Introduction
Modern geospatial operations face persistent coordination chal-
lenges across organizational boundaries. Spatial data is distributed
through disconnected GIS platforms, satellite feeds, IoT sensors,
field devices, cloud databases, and legacy infrastructure, each oper-
ating largely in isolation [14]. Human analysts must extract, trans-
form, route, and reconcile data across fragmented systems, diverting
effort from spatial reasoning and decision support [7, 10]. Agentic
AI increasingly allows coordination tasks to be delegated to systems
capable of planning, memory, and tool orchestration [16, 18].
Recent work combines multi-agent architectures with retrieval-
augmented generation to address the inability of text similarity
alone to represent topology, proximity, and spatial dependency
[6, 9, 10]. Other systems constrain reasoning with explicit workflow
graphs encoding spatial entities and transformation dependencies
[1, 2]. Execution benchmarks show that agents can handle moder-
ately complex workflows but degrade on multi-step tasks requiring
precise parameter inference and tool sequencing [5, 8, 17, 19].
This paper introduces ANASSA (Autonomous Neural Agents for
Spatial Systems Architecture), an autonomous orchestration layer
for geospatial intelligence, and reports its instantiation in an op-
erating supply-chain deployment. Building on prior research [14],
ANASSA extends the GeoBlockchain coordination model [13] from
blockchain-based to AI-agent-based orchestration, distributing rea-
soning, planning, execution, and validation across autonomous
agents under human-in-the-loop governance. The architecture ad-
dresses the limitations above by integrating spatial reasoning with
execution-aware orchestration, embedding feedback-driven adap-
tation through a continuous cognitive loop, and incorporating gov-
ernance for hallucination management.
This paper makes three contributions. First, it specifies ANASSA
as a unified architecture of eleven components across four layers,
in which every boundary between agents is a typed data contract
rather than an ad hoc handoff. Second, it instantiates that architec-
ture in an operating supply-chain deployment—multi-hazard risk
assessment on an ocean-and-inland freight lane, including cold-
chain excursion detection over container telemetry—and reports
three deployment outcomes together with an explicit account of
what those observations do and do not support. Third, it specifies
the instrument set and ablation protocol required to convert un-
controlled deployment observations into attributable, controlled
evidence, and identifies design principles for agentic GIS that follow
from the deployment.

ANASSA Framework
Agentic GIS systems improve automation, workflow generation,
and natural-language interaction [6, 9, 10], but robust spatial rea-
soning, execution reliability, and evaluation remain fragmented.
Multi-agent architectures improve decomposition yet remain sensi-
tive to ambiguous spatial constraints and long dependency chains
[1, 9, 17, 19]; structured reasoning approaches enforce logical con-
sistency but do not fully integrate execution feedback [1, 2].
Classifications are based on properties explicitly documented
in the cited publications; the cited references provide the basis for
external verification.
Figure 1 shows the progression from manual GIS to LLM/LMM-
augmented GIS and then to agentic GIS, where reasoning, multi-
agent execution, feedback, and validation form a closed loop.
2.1
Architecture Components
ANASSA is organized into eleven numbered architectural com-
ponents (C1–C11) structured across four layers (Figure 2). The
Governance and Output layer (C7, C10) validates framework out-
puts and governs all agent and workflow behavior. The Operational
layer (C1–C4, C6) defines participants, industry sectors, deployment
paths, the technical system-of-systems backbone, and multimodal
spatial data. The Cognitive Engine (C5) runs the six-step Geospa-
tial AI Cognitive Loop. The Foundation layer (C8) provides trusted
authoritative data and knowledge that all other components draw
from, while C9 and C11 represent the continuous downward and
upward process flows that close the loop. The layer assignment is
summarized in Table 2.

A third scheme, the agent coordination levels A0–A4, is introduced

synthesizes results into trusted intelligence. After each cycle, the

to the LLM- and LMM-based reasoning agents at C4-L1 and C4-

claim about model internals; the contribution is the coordination,
contract, and governance structure surrounding those models.
2.2
Geospatial AI Cognitive Loop
Component C5 is the core of ANASSA: a continuous six-step rea-
soning and action cycle that transforms spatial data into spatial
intelligence and intelligence into action (Table 3). The six steps
are S1 Sensing, S2 Reasoning, S3 Planning, S4 Decision and Hal-
lucination Validation, S5 Execution, and S6 Learning. All six are
coordinated by the Orchestrator (C4-L0) and grounded in authori-
tative data from C8. The cycle is feedback-driven rather than linear.
Outputs from S5 do not terminate the workflow; they are captured
at S6 and fed back into the system, enabling authorized operational
parameters and agent capabilities to adapt while governance-policy
changes remain subject to explicit approval. This closed-loop design
distinguishes ANASSA from pipeline-based agentic GIS systems,
which typically treat execution as a terminal stage and rely on
offline retraining for adaptation [9, 19].
Two mandatory human-in-the-loop gates appear at S3 (Planning)
and S4 (Decision and Hallucination Validation). At S3, participants
review and approve the generated workflow before any geospatial
operation is executed, preventing inappropriate model or tool se-
lection from propagating downstream. At S4, participants approve
or reject the cross-validated outputs before publication. Both gates
are blocking rather than advisory in the deployment reported here,
and both record approver identity and timestamp. These gates re-
flect the framework’s position that the highest-stakes geospatial

2.3
Governance and Hallucination Management
Component C7 functions as the governance backbone of ANASSA
and is positioned as a framework-wide overlay connecting to every
other component. Its design is informed by the CPMAI method-
ology [3], which structures the AI project lifecycle from problem
definition through production operation. Governance is organized
across seven pillars: business and problem governance (G1), data
governance and sovereignty (G2), AI model and agent governance
with hallucination management (G3), human-in-the-loop gover-
nance (G4), trust and auditability (G5), ethical AI and responsible
use (G6), and operationalization and continuous improvement (G7).
A distinctive feature of the framework is explicit management of
geospatial hallucinations as a primary governance concern. Fab-
ricated coordinates, invented boundaries, and false spatial claims
can produce incorrect maps used for high-stakes decisions, gener-
ate jurisdictional disputes, and in safety-critical contexts endanger
lives. The Claim Validation Agent in C7 tests every spatial claim in
a candidate output against the certified ground truth maintained in
C8. Claims that do not match a certified assertion are not silently
dropped and not silently published: they are delivered with the out-
put on an explicit unverified-claim list. This mechanism is specified
as contract K5 and is the subject of RQ2.
3
Research Questions and Use Case
The central research question motivating this work is: how can
agentic GIS systems integrate spatial reasoning, workflow orchestra-
tion, and execution feedback to enable robust and scalable geospa-
tial analysis in real-world environments? This paper investigates
that question through a single instantiated use case: multi-hazard
supply-chain risk assessment on an operating ocean-and-inland
freight lane. The use case requires no new sensing infrastructure; it
takes existing telemetry, network, and hazard streams as its starting


Papantoniou and Hilton
point and examines what the orchestration and spatial reasoning
layer contributes on top of them. The four research questions below
are scoped to what this deployment can and cannot answer, and
Section 6 reports the evidence obtained against each.
RQ1 Coordination. Do typed contracts replacing analyst me-
diated handoffs reduce coordination effort, measured by analyst
time and systems touched per analysis? RQ2 Grounding. Does
validating spatial claims against certified authoritative data reduce
spatial reference and unsupported claim errors and detect them
earlier? RQ3 Fusion and reproducibility. Does versioned fusion
of telemetry, network state, and asynchronously updating hazards
enable auditable decisions requiring multiple sources? RQ4 Gover-
nance under machine pace. Can blocking HITL gates preserve
accountability without eliminating the decision-latency advantage
of orchestration?
3.1
Use-Case Requirements and Failure Model
A logistics planner evaluating supply-chain vulnerability must com-
bine warehouse locations, transport routes, and environmental risk
such as flood zones, which requires spatial joins, distance anal-
ysis, and risk overlay across multiple steps. Existing LLM-based
systems can generate such workflows but fail when an interme-
diate output is missing or a dependency breaks, and the failure
can be silent: the workflow produces a plausible but unsupported
result rather than an error. Under ANASSA the Orchestrator mon-
itors execution, detects the missing layer, and replans, because
every transition is a typed contract with a declared precondition. A
missing coordinate reference system, unresolved owner, expired re-
striction, ungrounded facility, or trajectory gap therefore becomes
a contract violation at the point of handoff rather than a silent
degradation downstream. The use case exercises execution relia-
bility, adaptability, and alignment between automated reasoning
and human-governed decision making. It does not exercise cross-
domain generality, which Section 7 identifies as future work.
RQ4 deliberately states a tension rather than presuming that
governance is free. Two mandatory approvals inside a machine-pace
loop can reduce the very latency advantage the architecture seeks
to create. Decision latency is consequently defined from objective
to approved decision, not merely query to generated result, so the
cost of human review remains inside the measured interval.
3.2
Supply-Chain Agent Specification
Coordination levels A0–A4 are distinct from C4 infrastructure levels.
A0 is the Orchestrator; A1 the cognitive-loop roles; A2 domain
specialists; A3 governance; and A4 foundation services. In this
deployment, A4 is realized by C8 services rather than a distinct
agent. Table 4 summarizes the eleven active roles and representative
validation conditions.
3.3
Coordination Semantics
The coordination levels define invocation authority as well as role
grouping. A0 decomposes intent and sequences work; A1 performs
the cognitive-loop functions; A2 supplies domain-specialist execu-
tion; A3 governs publication and can block; A4 supplies author-
itative foundation services. Governance agents are intentionally
asymmetric with execution agents: A3 may stop or return work to
the cognitive loop, while A1 and A2 cannot bypass or disable an A3
decision. In the reported instantiation, A4 functions are provided
by C8 services rather than a separately autonomous agent. K8 may
update only pre-authorized operational parameters; changes to
blocking gates, validation requirements, publication authority, or
governance policy require explicit authorized approval.
The validation conditions in Table 4 are part of the architecture
rather than implementation commentary. Each condition names
a checkable failure mode: legal ownership must resolve before
downstream action, facilities and terminals must resolve to certified
geometry or be marked unverified, trajectories must remain gap-
aware, restrictions must carry validity intervals, and reallocation
proposals must cite the observations that triggered them. These
conditions externalize a portion of expert operating knowledge
into machine-checkable boundaries while leaving consequential
judgment at the HITL gates.
4
Implementation
4.1
Deployment Architecture
The implementation is a hybrid multi-cloud deployment integrat-
ing foundation-model reasoning, deterministic spatial services, au-
tomated workflows, and operator dashboards. Provider-specific
model identifiers and infrastructure configuration are deployment-
specific and are not reported; the contribution evaluated here is
the orchestration and governance layer rather than model-specific
performance. Container telemetry is written to versioned storage
through C6→C8. Certification denotes workflow-authorized ev-
idence with known source identity, spatial reference, temporal
validity where applicable, and recorded storage version; it does
not imply that a source is error-free. Spatial/vector indexes are
version-recorded and rebuilt rather than served stale.
The workload is spatial big data rather than merely workflow
automation. Container telemetry is a high-cardinality point stream
keyed by consignment; active-consignment counts, observation
rates, retention windows, and authoritative-layer version counts are
confidential deployment-scale quantities. Tracks require gap-aware
trajectory handling, while hazard polygons update asynchronously
and carry validity intervals. The resulting join is spatio-temporal
and runs against version-recorded spatial indexes. Typed contracts
declare this join once rather than forcing analysts to reconstruct it
per query.

4.3
End-to-End Execution Trace
The supply-chain instantiation can be read as a concrete execution
trace through the contracts and cognitive loop rather than as a
collection of independent agents. Figure 3 anchors the sequence
to the selected cold-chain observation shown later in the operator
view: Location ID 8015, Express Logistics, 93◦F, at 47.56, -26.54.
A cycle begins when C6 receives telemetry or a hazard-layer up-
date. K1 assigns certification state and records the storage version
before the new observation enters C8. K2 then packages the sens-
ing bundle with an explicit coordinate reference system and with
gaps represented as monitoring failures rather than silently interpo-
lated history. S1 can therefore distinguish an observed event from a
missing observation window before the reasoning stage is invoked.
At S2, the Reasoning function establishes spatial and operational
context and identifies the tools and domain roles needed for the ob-
jective. The Orchestrator converts that reasoning into a task graph
under K3. Each node declares its tool, parameters, expected output
type, and dependency. The first blocking gate, K4 at S3, exposes the
candidate plan to an accountable participant before execution; rejec-
tion returns the workflow for revision before geospatial operations
are committed.
Approval identity/timestamp and tool-output metadata are re-
tained in operational logs but omitted from the anonymized trace.
Execution then proceeds through the A2 domain roles. The Sup-
ply Origin Agent resolves the dispatch facility against certified C8
geometry. The Port State Agent maintains the load and discharge
terminal states. The Maritime Leg Agent produces a gap-aware
ocean corridor, and the Inland Leg Agent applies restrictions with
validity intervals to the final-haul route. The Inventory and Al-
location Agent reconciles arriving quantity and, when required,
proposes reallocation while citing the triggering observations. Each

4.5
Cold-Chain Excursion Detection
A second view of the same lane (Figure 6) surfaces a temperature
excursion on the ocean leg, detected by the Cold-Chain Telemetry
Agent. Temperature is reported in degrees Fahrenheit. For the il-
lustrated canned-food POC, 95◦F was configured as the maximum
storage threshold and the selected 93◦F observation was surfaced as
an early-warning condition before that maximum was reached. Sam-
pling cadence is source-determined rather than fixed by ANASSA;
the framework reasons over received observations and treats miss-
ing windows as monitoring gaps rather than interpolating them.
Event extent is derived from the valid trajectory segment associated
with successive excursion observations, not from an inferred path
across missing measurements [11, 12].
4.6
Versioning, Provenance, and Reproducibility
The authoritative foundation is not treated as a single current-
state database. Telemetry writes receive a certification level and
storage version at ingestion, while hazard layers carry independent
validity intervals. Spatial and vector indexes are version-recorded
and rebuilt rather than silently serving stale state. Contract K7
preserves the index version and query with the validated output.
Consequently, the artifact used by a domain agent and the artifact
later examined by Claim Validation can be tied to the same recorded
state.
This design addresses an audit problem specific to spatial deci-
sion systems: the same nominal query can return a different answer
after a geometry, restriction, or hazard layer changes. Here, audit
re-issue means recovering the validated decision artifact together
with the recorded query, spatial-index/source versions, transforma-
tion context, and provenance chain. It does not imply bit-for-bit
regeneration of the complete agentic reasoning process, which
would require additional model and execution-state preservation.

5.1
Observed Outcomes
Three outcomes were recorded during a three-month POC that
progressed through three implementation iterations. The itera-
tions reflect successive operational refinements rather than in-
dependent experimental runs. Individual workflow-run counts,
rejected/deferred-output counts, and workload distributions were
not retained for controlled statistical analysis; absolute deployment
quantities remain confidential. D1–D3 are therefore reported as
normalized relative changes against the preceding manual work-

Δ𝑀= (𝑀manual −𝑀POC)/𝑀manual × 100%.
D1. Data quality. Human reviewers independently reviewed
outputs. Data quality is the unsupported-claim rate: unsupported
published claims divided by total published claims; claims surfaced
as unverified and routed to review are tracked separately. The POC
was associated with a 25% lower unsupported-claim rate against
the manual workflow.
D2. Workflow runtime. Runtime spans initiation of the multi-
hazard analytical workflow through production of the validated
analytical output. The POC combined environmental hazard layers,
transport-network restrictions, and cold-chain telemetry. It was
associated with a 50% reduction in workflow runtime relative to
the preceding manual workflow.
D3. Decision latency. The same deployment was associated
with 60% lower decision latency, measured from objective to ap-
proved decision. The two blocking approvals are inside this interval;
D3 therefore captures the human-governed end-to-end decision

5.2
Threats to Validity
D1–D3 are deployment observations from operating the platform
against the pre-existing manual workflow as baseline. They are not
controlled experiments, and several limits apply. The compared con-
figurations differ in more than one mechanism at a time: ANASSA
introduces typed contracts, authoritative grounding, and gover-
nance gates simultaneously. The observations therefore support
an association between the coordination layer as a whole and ma-
terial operational improvement, but not attribution to any single
mechanism. The three-month POC was not randomized, progressed
through three implementation iterations, and experienced varying
lane conditions; seasonal, volume, and iteration effects cannot be
separated from the observed changes. The deployment covers one
operating context, and behavior under different data-certification
regimes, jurisdictional constraints, or organizational structures is
unobserved. Instrumentation was incomplete during the observa-
tion period; in particular, intervention rate and gate dwell time were
not captured, which leaves the evidence against RQ4 partial. Finally,
the baseline is the organization’s pre-existing manual workflow,
not a competing agentic-GIS system. The comparison therefore
speaks to the value of orchestration over manual coordination and
not to ANASSA’s standing relative to GeoAgentic-RAG, GeoAgent,
or other published agentic-GIS pipelines.
5.3
Instrumentation for Controlled Evaluation
The architecture emits contract, gate, and provenance logs from
which the next evaluation can compute: analyst time and systems
touched (RQ1); contract violations, spatial reference errors, unsup-
ported claims, unverified claims, and failure detection stage mea-
sures (RQ1/RQ2); fusion coverage, re-query determinism, and audit
completeness (RQ3); and intervention rate, gate dwell time, and
objective-to-approved-decision latency (RQ4). Unverified-claim list
length is especially important: if authoritative coverage is too low,
the architecture merely relocates coordination burden to human
review.
5.4
Industrial Impact
The architecture is aimed at organizations that already hold the
data and already have the problem at scale, and whose analysts
spend a large share of their time reconciling systems rather than
reasoning about space. The same contract set applies wherever a
spatial decision is assembled across systems that share neither a
reference frame nor an owner: last-mile delivery planning, where
route feasibility depends on restriction attributes held by a third
party; dynamic routing under disruption, where hazard layers and
network state update on different cadences; and resource allocation
across facilities, which is only as good as the derived locations
it is computed over. What transfers is the typed boundary rather
than the domain model, and coverage at an operator-accepted pre-
cision floor rather than raw throughput. Two conditions govern
whether that transfer pays. The first is foundation coverage: below
the threshold at which the unverified-claim list stays reviewable
at operational volume, the architecture relocates coordination bur-
den rather than reducing it. The second is that typed boundaries
must be declared once by someone who understands the domain,
an up-front cost paid against recurring analyst time rather than a
capability the platform supplies. This is a claim about applicability,
not a demonstrated result; the architecture has been instantiated in
one domain only.

6.1
Design Principles Operationalized
Four design principles are operationalized. First, reasoning and exe-
cution should be coupled rather than run as separate phases; C4-L0
realizes this by sequencing both within one task graph. Second,
transitions should carry declared preconditions rather than merely
structured payloads; K1–K8 make the boundary itself checkable,
and what transfers to another domain is the typed boundary rather
than the logistics model. Third, systems should correct errors dur-
ing execution and record learning updates explicitly; C5-S6 and K8
require a cycle identifier and an enumeration of changed parame-
ters rather than silent adaptation. Fourth, authoritative grounding
should be consulted before publication rather than used only for
post-hoc checking; C8 and K5 force unmatched claims onto an
explicit review list.
These principles are paired with measurements rather than as-
serted as causal outcomes. Analyst time and systems touched mea-
sure reconciliation burden; spatial-reference, unsupported-claim,
and detection-stage metrics test grounding; provenance complete-
ness and re-query determinism test reproducibility; intervention
rate and gate dwell time expose the operational cost of governance.
This pairing is important because the deployment observations are
aggregate outcomes and cannot isolate individual mechanisms.
6.2
Limits and Boundary Conditions
The architecture externalizes some expert judgment as validation
conditions but does not capture tacit competence. Examples in-
clude recognizing when a nominally valid route is operationally
unrealistic, deciding which anomaly warrants escalation in a spe-
cific commercial relationship, or weighing a marginal reallocation
against customer commitments. HITL gates preserve that expertise
in the operating loop rather than claiming to encode it. Systemati-
cally eliciting and representing organizational decision knowledge
remains a prerequisite for safely raising the autonomy level.
The framework also assumes effective integration with opera-
tional GIS execution tools. Robust error detection, interoperability,
and scalability under production loads are engineering problems
that the architecture organizes but does not itself solve. Deployment-
scale utilization and commercial cost figures are confidential. Princi-
pal resource drivers are telemetry/index processing, spatio-temporal
analysis, model inference, and versioned provenance storage; no
general cost or throughput envelope is claimed. A second bound-
ary condition is authoritative-foundation coverage. If C8 cannot
resolve enough claims at the operator-accepted precision floor, the
unverified-claim list grows and human review becomes the bottle-
neck; in that regime the architecture relocates coordination burden
instead of reducing it. Finally, the evidence is from one operating
supply-chain context and does not establish cross-domain general-
ity.
7
Future Research
The immediate priority is attributable evaluation. A grounding ab-
lation disables K5 and Claim Validation while retaining contracts
and gates, measuring spatial-reference errors, unsupported claims,
review load, and failure-detection stage. A governance ablation
converts K4/K6 from blocking to advisory, measuring claim errors,
intervention rate, gate dwell, and decision latency. A contract abla-
tion replaces typed contracts with ad hoc handoffs while retaining
grounding and gates, measuring analyst time, systems touched, and
contract failures.
Beyond ablation, the workflow should be evaluated against GeoA-
gentBench [19] and GeoBenchX [8]; repeated under different data-
certification regimes; extended with checkable representations of
tacit decision knowledge; and studied with logistics planners to test
whether gate and unverified-claim interfaces remain usable at oper-
ational volume. Broader research should also address quantitative
spatial reasoning over multi-step dependencies [4] and additional
modalities such as remote sensing and temporal data [5, 15].
8
Conclusion
ANASSA is a reference architecture for autonomous geospatial
intelligence across distributed systems. Its operative properties are
typed agent boundaries with declared preconditions, authoritative
validation of spatial claims, and blocking human accountability
at consequential decision points. In one operating supply-chain
deployment, the architecture was associated with 25% improved
data quality, 50% lower spatial workflow runtime, and 60% lower
decision latency against a manual baseline. These are uncontrolled
