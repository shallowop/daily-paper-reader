tariff and geopolitical uncertainty

Abstract
Global supply chains are increasingly exposed to trade policy shocks and phys-
ical disruptions arising from common geopolitical events. We develop a robust
optimization framework with two decision stages for strategic supply chain recon-
figuration. Supplier qualification and transportation arc activation decisions are
made before uncertainty is realized, while operational flows adapt afterward.
Geopolitical uncertainty is represented through country level shocks that can
jointly trigger tariff escalation, policy induced restrictions on cross border capac-
ity, facility disruptions, and physical transportation failures. The resulting min
max min problem is reformulated by dualizing the operational recourse problem,
yielding a nonconvex mixed integer bilinear adversarial problem solved within
a column and constraint generation procedure. Comparison with complete enu-
meration verifies the implementation on small instances, while experiments on
60 synthetic supply chain networks of varying sizes assess computational perfor-
mance. The results show that omitting geopolitical transmission channels during
strategic design can lead to substantial deterioration in cost and service under full
geopolitical stress. Broader protection is achieved primarily through changes in
transportation network configuration rather than increased supplier qualification.
Robust performance also depends on the severity and composition of geopolitical
consequences, with restrictions on cross border capacity exerting a stronger effect
than comparable proportional changes in tariff severity over the tested ranges. A
geographically grounded case analysis further shows that the value of individual
uncertainty channels depends on network structure and geographic exposure.
Keywords: robust optimization; supply chain resilience; geopolitical risk; trade policy
uncertainty; network reconfiguration; column and constraint generation

1 Introduction
Global supply chains are increasingly exposed to shifts in international trade pol-
icy that can alter sourcing costs, manufacturing footprints, transportation routes,
and market access. Tariff escalation, retaliatory measures, sanctions, export controls,
and border restrictions can require firms to reconsider strategic sourcing and logistics
decisions made under substantially different trade conditions. At the same time, the
geopolitical events underlying such policy changes may also disrupt facilities or trans-
portation connections. Consequently, strategic supply chain reconfiguration requires
consideration not only of changes in the cost of cross border trade, but also of the mul-
tiple operational consequences through which the same geopolitical event may affect
the network.
Supply chain network design under uncertainty has received extensive attention
in the operations research literature. Existing studies employ stochastic, robust, and
related optimization approaches to account for uncertain demand, supply, capacity,
and disruption risk [1, 2]. Robust optimization has also been applied to integrated
network configuration and operational decisions, including facility location, inventory
management, and transportation routing [3, 4]. Within the resilience literature, facility
failures, supply and demand interruptions, and investments in protection or redun-
dancy have been incorporated into strategic network design models [5–7]. Lotfi et
al. [8] develop a bilevel supply chain network design model incorporating risk, robust-
ness, and resilience strategies. Yildiz et al. [9] examine the tradeoff between network
cost and reliability, while Liu et al. [10] investigate supply chain resilience strategies
under budget constraints. More recently, Yildiz et al. [11] examine the efficiency of
combining multiple risk mitigation strategies under complex disruption patterns. Dol-
gui et al. [12] emphasize the role of supply chain stress testing in assessing resilience
and viability under disruptions. Together, these studies highlight the importance of
network configuration, operational flexibility, and systematic evaluation of resilience
strategies under adverse conditions.
A related literature examines international sourcing decisions and the effects of
trade policy interventions on global supply chains. Kandil et al. [13] review analytical
models for sourcing and location decisions in the context of globalization and changing
international trade conditions. Dong and Kouvelis [14] discuss the implications of tar-
iffs for global supply chain network configuration, while Grossman et al. [15] examine
how unexpected tariffs affect supplier relationships and sourcing decisions. Nagurney
et al. [16] analyze tariff rate quotas in competitive global supply chain networks, while
Feng et al. [17] investigate the effects of trade policies on supply chain network equilib-
rium. Nagurney and Samadi [18] extend the equilibrium perspective by incorporating
ad valorem tariffs and labor constraints.
Recent research also emphasizes the broader consequences of tariff uncertainty and
geopolitical disruptions. Ivanov and Dolgui [19] discuss the ripple effects and deep
uncertainty associated with tariff shocks, while Ivanov [20] develops a decision support
framework for responding to tariff induced uncertainty and disruptions. Cruz [21]
examines tariff uncertainty and risk aversion in a multiperiod supply chain equilibrium
model. Particularly related to the present study, Ahmadi Digehsara et al. [22] develop
a robust optimization framework with two decision stages for geopolitical disruptions
2


in which strategic alliances influence exposure to tariff increases and quota based trade
restrictions.
These streams provide sophisticated treatments of physical disruption risk and
trade policy interventions, but an important modeling issue arises when different con-
sequences originate from a common geopolitical event. Changes in trade costs or
capacity and disruptions to facilities or transportation need not constitute independent
sources of uncertainty. They may instead represent alternative or simultaneous conse-
quences of the same country level shock. Treating these consequences as independent
can admit combinations that do not reflect their geographic origin, whereas represent-
ing only one consequence channel can omit other ways in which the same geopolitical
event affects the network. This motivates an uncertainty architecture that separates
the geographic origin of geopolitical uncertainty from its consequences for the supply
chain and explicitly controls which combinations of consequences may occur jointly.
To address this issue, we develop a robust optimization model with two decision
stages for strategic global supply chain reconfiguration under geopolitical uncertainty
with multiple consequence channels. Before uncertainty is realized, the firm qualifies
suppliers and activates transportation arcs, thereby determining the sourcing and rout-
ing alternatives available to the network. After the geopolitical realization is observed,
operational flows adapt over the resulting network. Country level shocks determine
the geographic exposure of network components, while separate consequence chan-
nels represent their effects on trade conditions, facility availability, and transportation
connectivity. Budgets for individual channels regulate the extent of each type of
consequence, while a joint budget controls their simultaneous occurrence.
The paper makes three main contributions. First, we develop an uncertainty archi-
tecture in which geopolitical shocks originate at the country level and may propagate
through multiple trade policy and physical disruption channels. Restricted versions of
the uncertainty set are obtained by selectively excluding consequence channels. This
common structure allows us to compare strategic designs that consider only tariff esca-
lation, tariff escalation together with restrictions on cross border capacity, or physical
disruptions to facilities and transportation with designs that consider all four conse-
quence channels. It therefore provides a systematic basis for assessing the implications
of omitting particular transmission channels from strategic network design. Second, we
integrate supplier qualification and transportation arc activation with adaptive oper-
ational recourse in a unified robust model for supply chain reconfiguration. Supplier
qualification creates alternative sourcing options, while transportation arc activation
determines the routing alternatives available after uncertainty is realized. The model
therefore captures two complementary mechanisms for strategic resilience and their
interaction with adaptive operational decisions.
Third, we develop a column and constraint generation solution approach for the
resulting robust optimization problem and conduct a systematic computational evalu-
ation of the proposed framework. Comparison with complete enumeration verifies the
implementation on small instances. We then compare strategic configurations devel-
oped under different uncertainty assumptions by exposing each configuration to the
same full range of geopolitical consequences. This analysis quantifies the implications
3


of omitting particular transmission channels during strategic design. Additional exper-
iments examine how the results vary with the structure and severity of geopolitical
uncertainty and with the geographic characteristics of the network.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the deci-
sion setting and develops the structure of geopolitical uncertainty. Section 3 presents
the robust optimization model and defines the restricted uncertainty environments.
Section 4 develops the reformulation and solution procedure. Section 5 presents the
computational study and discusses the resulting insights. Section 6 concludes the
paper.
2 Problem setting and geopolitical uncertainty
We consider a firm that strategically configures a global supply chain in the presence
of geopolitical uncertainty. The supply chain consists of four echelons: suppliers, man-
ufacturing plants, warehouses, and retailers located across multiple countries. Material
flows sequentially from suppliers to plants, from plants to warehouses, and from ware-
houses to retailers. Because facilities and transportation connections span national
boundaries, the network is exposed to changes in trade conditions as well as physical
disruptions associated with geopolitical events.
The firm selects its strategic network configuration before geopolitical uncertainty
is realized, incurring costs for supplier qualification and transportation arc activa-
tion. Once geopolitical conditions are observed, material flows are adjusted within the
available network capacities. Demand that cannot be served because of capacity lim-
itations, trade restrictions, or physical disruptions is represented as unmet demand
and incurs a penalty cost. The objective is to determine a strategic configuration that
minimizes the sum of the initial reconfiguration cost and the worst case operating cost
under admissible geopolitical realizations.
The defining feature of the uncertainty structure is the distinction between the
geographic origin of a geopolitical event and its consequences for the supply chain. A
country level shock represents an adverse geopolitical event, such as a trade dispute,
economic sanction, political instability, or regional conflict, associated with a particular
country. Such a shock creates exposure for facilities located in that country and for
transportation arcs connected to it. It does not require every exposed component
to experience an adverse consequence. Instead, the country shock establishes which
network components are eligible to be affected, while separate consequence variables
and uncertainty budgets determine which adverse effects are realized.
Four consequence channels are considered. The trade cost channel represents tariff
escalation on international transportation arcs. The trade capacity channel represents
reductions in usable international transportation capacity resulting from measures
such as quotas, sanctions, export controls, or border restrictions. The facility channel
represents disruptions to suppliers, manufacturing plants, and warehouses. The trans-
portation channel represents physical failures of transportation arcs. These channels
distinguish changes in the economic and regulatory conditions of international trade
from losses in the physical availability of facilities and transportation connections.
4


Dependence among these consequences arises from their common geographic expo-
sure. A facility consequence can occur only when the country hosting that facility
is shocked, while a consequence affecting a transportation arc can occur only when
at least one of its endpoint countries is shocked. Budgets for individual consequence
channels limit the extent of each type of disruption or trade intervention. A joint
budget additionally limits the composition of consequences that may occur simulta-
neously across channels. The resulting construction retains the flexibility of budgeted
robust optimization while preventing consequences from being assigned to network
components that have no exposure to a selected country shock.

4 Reformulation and solution method
The robust formulation (28) requires evaluation of the worst-case recourse cost over UF
for a given first-stage configuration. Explicit incorporation of all admissible uncertainty
realizations becomes computationally impractical as the dimensions of the network and
uncertainty set increase. We therefore employ the column and constraint generation
(CCG) method (see for instance [23]), which iteratively augments a restricted master
problem (RMP) with uncertainty realizations identified by an adversarial subproblem.
For fixed first-stage decisions, the adversarial subproblem is obtained by dualizing the
linear operational recourse problem defined in Section 3.3.

4.2 Column-and-constraint generation
The adversarial problem (41) evaluates the worst-case recourse cost associated with
a fixed first-stage configuration. To solve the two-stage robust problem (28) without
explicitly incorporating all realizations in UF , we employ a column-and-constraint

followed by solving the adversarial problem for the resulting first-stage configuration.

5 Computational study and model evaluation
This section evaluates the proposed robust optimization framework and the CCG solu-
tion procedure developed in Section 4. The computational study has four objectives.
First, we assess the computational performance and scalability of the CCG procedure
over a collection of synthetically generated four-echelon supply chain networks. Second,
we examine the value of explicitly accounting for multiple geopolitical transmission
channels by comparing strategic configurations obtained under the full uncertainty
set UF and the restricted uncertainty environments defined in Section 3.4. Third, we
investigate the sensitivity of the resulting strategic configurations and worst-case oper-
ating performance to the severity and composition of geopolitical uncertainty. Finally,
a case-based analysis evaluates the framework on a supply chain network constructed
from real-world data.

uncertainty and the cost of operating under geopolitical stress. Restricted uncertainty
environments produce lower design objectives because fewer adverse consequences are
considered. However, these apparent cost advantages do not persist when the resulting
configurations are evaluated under the full uncertainty set. Although the configuration

under common geopolitical stress. This distinction demonstrates why strategic configu-

a common uncertainty environment. The comparison also reveals substantial differ-
ences in the protection provided by individual uncertainty channels. Configurations
designed only against tariff escalation exhibit the greatest cost deterioration. Incor-
porating restrictions on international transportation capacity improves performance,
indicating that accounting for changes in trade costs alone provides limited protection
when geopolitical events also restrict physical flows. Configurations designed against
facility and transportation disruptions perform considerably closer to those obtained
under the full uncertainty set. This suggests that accounting for physical disruptions
provides broader protection against omitted geopolitical consequences than accounting
for trade policy uncertainty alone, although the additional channels remain relevant.
Cost and service consequences of omitted channels: The differences in total
cost arise despite relatively small variations in strategic reconfiguration expenditure.

slightly higher initial costs but achieve substantially lower worst case operating costs
under the common stress test. The reduction in operating costs more than compen-
sates for the additional strategic expenditure. This indicates that relatively modest
investments in network configuration can provide substantial protection against the
operational consequences of omitted geopolitical uncertainty channels. The service
results reveal a similar pattern. Configurations designed under restricted uncertainty
environments exhibit lower average service rates under the common stress test, with
the greatest deterioration observed for designs considering only tariff escalation. Thus,
the consequences of omitting uncertainty channels extend beyond additional operat-
ing costs to reduced demand fulfillment. As defined in Section 5.1, the reported service
rates correspond to the operational responses under the selected worst case cost real-
izations. The differences between restricted uncertainty environments also suggest an
asymmetry in the protection provided by different channels. Configurations designed
against physical disruptions retain considerably greater protection under full geopo-

explanation is that protection against facility and transportation failures creates alter-
native network connections that can also accommodate changes in trade costs and
usable international transportation capacity. Conversely, configurations designed only
against trade policy uncertainty remain more exposed when geopolitical shocks also
affect the physical availability of the network. These findings suggest that protec-
tion against physical disruptions provides partial protection against other geopolitical
consequences, although explicit consideration of trade policy uncertainty remains valu-
able. The strategic configurations provide further insight into this pattern. Across the

on an inbound international transportation corridor. The affected corridor is Egypt–
Oman for configurations developed under UT and UT P , and Hong Kong–Oman for
those developed under UD and UF . These consequences are consistent with the coun-
try dependence constraints in (7)–(10), which permit multiple adverse consequences
to originate from the same country level shock. The results identify the Oman ware-
house as a critical location under the selected worst case realizations, while the affected
inbound transportation corridor depends on the strategic configuration. This illus-
trates how country level geopolitical shocks can propagate through different network
components and how strategic decisions influence the resulting geographic exposure.

6 Conclusions
This study develops a robust optimization framework for strategic supply chain recon-
figuration that captures the dependence among multiple geopolitical consequences
arising from common country level shocks. The proposed uncertainty structure inte-
grates physical disruptions and trade policy interventions through channel specific and
joint uncertainty budgets, while its nested restricted sets permit systematic evaluation
of individual transmission channels. The resulting two stage robust optimization prob-
lem is addressed through recourse dualization and a column and constraint generation
procedure. The computational results show that omitting geopolitical consequence
channels during strategic design can lead to substantial increases in cost and reduc-
tions in service under common full uncertainty stress. Configurations designed against
physical disruptions generally provide greater protection against omitted channels
than those considering only trade policy uncertainty. Broader protection is associated
primarily with changes in transportation network configuration rather than increased
supplier qualification. The sensitivity experiments demonstrate that both the com-
position and severity of geopolitical consequences influence robust performance, with
restrictions on international transportation capacity having a stronger effect than
comparable proportional changes in tariff increments over the tested ranges. The geo-
graphically grounded case study further shows that the incremental value of individual
channels depends on network characteristics.
From a managerial perspective, the findings highlight the importance of evaluat-
ing strategic configurations against the combined consequences of geopolitical shocks.
In particular, alternative transportation connections can provide operational flexi-
bility without necessarily requiring additional supplier qualification. The framework
also enables decision makers to identify vulnerabilities that may be overlooked when
geopolitical exposure is evaluated through isolated trade policy or physical disruption
scenarios. The analysis considers binary geopolitical consequences with exogenously
specified uncertainty budgets and channel weights, fixed demand, and linear operat-
ing costs. Future research could develop empirical calibration methods for geopolitical
uncertainty, incorporate continuous or multiple disruption states, and extend the
framework to multistage settings with evolving geopolitical conditions and strategic
reconfiguration decisions.
