Foundation Models Meet Power Grid Topology for
Cascade Prediction
Soumav Biswas and Mia Naeini
Electrical Engineering Department, University of South Florida, Tampa, Florida, USA
soumavbiswas@usf.edu, mnaeini@usf.edu
Abstract—Accurate early-stage prediction of cascading failure
severity is essential for assessing the vulnerability of power
transmission networks. However, existing data-driven approaches
often rely on network-specific representations, limiting their
generalizability across power system topologies. This work inves-
tigates the complementary capabilities of Large Language Models
(LLMs) and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) for cascading
failure severity prediction. First, standalone LLM predictors are
developed using a LoRA-fine-tuned LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct model
with varying degrees of topological information. A hybrid Graph-
LLM fusion framework is then proposed, integrating a Topology-
Aware Graph Attention Transformer (TAGAT) with the LLM
through confidence-gated fusion. TAGAT captures structural
dependencies among transmission lines, while the LLM extracts
representations from early-stage power-flow observations and
aggregated cascade descriptors. The frameworks are evaluated
on 30,000 simulated cascading failure scenarios for each of the
IEEE 118-bus and IEEE 300-bus systems. Using only the first four
cascade steps, the hybrid framework achieves weighted F1 scores
of 0.94 and 0.92, respectively. In cross-system transfer from IEEE
118-bus to IEEE 300-bus, it achieves 0.72 zero-shot accuracy,
improving to 0.83 with limited target-system calibration, out-
performing standalone models and conventional baselines. These
results show the potential of combining graph-based structural
learning with foundation models to improve cascading failure
severity prediction and cross-system transferability.
Index Terms—Large Language Model, Graph Neural Net-
works, Graph Signal Processing, Cascade Size Prediction.
I. INTRODUCTION
Cascading failures are complex, high-impact events that
arise as interdependent component failures triggered by initial
outages and intensified by shortcomings in control, computa-
tion and human error. For decades, these phenomena have been
examined from various perspectives to better understand their
behavior and develop strategies for prevention, mitigation, and
recovery. The techniques used to analyze these phenomena
include simulation-based [1], physics-driven [2] and machine
learning (ML)-based [3] models.
In particular, ML–based models have recently gained signif-
icant attention for cascading failure analysis in power systems
to supplement the traditional techniques [4]. From classical
ML models, including support vector machines (SVMs) and
random forests (RFs) [5], to deep neural networks (DNNs) and
graph-aware models, such as Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)
[6], a range of data-driven approaches has demonstrated strong
potential for cascade analyses and prediction [7]. Among these
approaches, GNN-based models can utilize network topology
and component interactions to capture the complex depen-
dencies underlying cascading failures. However, conventional
GNN architectures rely on local neighborhood aggregation and
are often coupled to specific network topologies, limiting their
system-level reasoning and generalizability across heteroge-
neous power systems [8].
Conversely, Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged
as powerful sequence modeling and reasoning tools for het-
erogeneous numerical and textual information. For power
system applications, LLMs provide a flexible mechanism
for representing structured measurements, event descriptors,
and system-level properties and indicators as natural-language
prompts [9]. For cascading failure prediction, LLMs can learn
latent relationships between early-stage indicators and eventual
cascade severity, providing representations less dependent on
fixed graph structures than conventional graph-based models.
To utilize the complementary strengths of two powerful
classes of learning models, namely graph-based architectures
and LLMs, this paper adopts a hybrid learning paradigm. The
main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
• A Graph-LLM Fusion framework is proposed for cascad-
ing failure severity prediction, integrating topology-aware
structural representations learned by Topology-Aware
Graph Attention Transformer (TAGAT) with the reason-
ing and representation capabilities of LLMs through a
confidence-gated fusion mechanism.
• A comprehensive taxonomy and comparison of cascade
severity prediction models is developed across three
distinct categories: (i) topology-agnostic, (ii) topology-
aware, and (iii) Graph-LLM fusion models.
• An extensive set of topology-agnostic baseline models
is investigated, including SVM, RF, DNN, Feature To-
kenizer Transformer (FT-Transformer), and a standalone
LLM variant.
• Multiple topology-aware standalone prediction frame-
works are evaluated, including TAGAT and LLM-based
models enriched with either aggregated topological de-
scriptors or explicit network topology information.
• Topological features based on Graph Signal Process-
ing (GSP) are considered to capture global power-flow
smoothness and its relationship to uncontrolled islanding.
Additional descriptors, including betweenness centrality
and bridge properties of initially failed lines, are used to
characterize structural vulnerability.
• Cross-system transferability study evaluates graph-based,
LLM-based, and Graph-LLM fusion models trained on
the IEEE 118-bus system and tested on the IEEE 300-

II. RELATED WORKS
Existing data-driven research has focused on classical ML
methods and graph-based deep learning frameworks, partic-
ularly GNNs. Examples of such studies include cascade size
prediction formulated as a classification problem [5], [7], [10],
component status prediction [11], [12], and risk assessment
based on contingency precursors [13]. Other applications of
data-driven techniques include predicting cascading failure
trajectories [14], [15] and identifying critical components
involved in the cascade process [16]–[18]. Moreover, physics-
informed graph-based models, with domain constraints or
attention mechanisms [8], [19] have also been proposed for
cascade analyses in power systems. GSP methods have also
been employed to capture topological relationships and power-
flow variations for identifying early indicators of cascading
failures [20]–[22].
The use of LLMs in power systems analytics is a recent
development. Domain-tuned assistants and agent-based sys-
tems like GAIA [23] and GridMind [24], utilize LLMs for
operational guidance and contingency reasoning. LLM has
also been introduced for time-series analysis [25], [26] and
event forecasting [27] of real-time data in power systems.
This approach also has demonstrated notable gains in load
forecasting [28]–[30], fault diagnosis [31], [32], and event
classification [33], showing its potential for predictive ana-
lytics in power systems.
Recent advances in multimodal learning have demonstrated
that combining graph-based models with foundation models
enables complementary learning of structural and semantic
representations in specific domains [34]–[36]. Although graph-
language hybrid frameworks have shown promising results in
molecular modeling, knowledge graphs, and recommendation
systems, their application to power systems, particularly cas-
cading failure prediction, remains largely unexplored. Moti-
vated by these advances, this work integrates topology-aware
graph representation learning with LLM reasoning capabilities
for early cascade severity prediction.
Moreover, cross-system transferability remains a key chal-
lenge for many ML models, particularly those developed for
power systems with different network topologies. Power net-
works may differ significantly in bus count, line count, graph
density, and operating conditions. Recent studies on GNNs for
power system applications have shown that topology changes
and distribution shifts can strongly affect model generalization,
motivating topology-aware but transferable learning strategies
[8], [37], [38]. Domain adaptation techniques, such as corre-
lation alignment, can reduce distribution mismatch between
source and target systems by aligning second-order feature
statistics [39]. In addition, few-shot calibration and source-to-
target adaptation methods provide practical mechanisms for
adapting a trained model using limited target-domain informa-
tion [40], [41]. These ideas are especially relevant for hybrid
graph-language models, where topology-aware representations
may improve in-distribution prediction but may also require
adaptation when deployed on unseen network topologies.
To the best of our knowledge, existing studies have not
systematically compared topology-aware graph representation
learning, topology-agnostic LLMs, and their hybrid fusion for
cascading failure analysis in power systems. This paper ad-
dresses these shortcomings by systematically evaluating these
formulations in terms of in-distribution predictive accuracy,
zero-shot cross-system transferability, and few-shot adaptation
under a limited target-label budget.
III. CASCADE SIMULATION, DATA GENERATION, AND
FEATURE DEFINITION
This section describes the cascading failure model used in
the simulation and the methodology used for generating data
description from the simulated cascading failure events.
1) Cascading Failures Model: The cascading failure events
are modeled using a line-overloading mechanism implemented
in MATPOWER, where DC power flow is recomputed after
each outage to determine post-contingency line loading [42].
The cascade continues until no additional overloaded lines are
detected. Two systems, specifically, the IEEE-118 and IEEE-
300 test systems have been used for generating the cascade
events and for the performance evaluation of the models. The
IEEE 118-bus (118 buses, 186 lines, 54 generators, 91 loads)
and IEEE 300-bus (300 buses, 411 lines, 69 generators, 200
loads) systems serve as standard benchmark models.
It is assumed that two initial failures trigger the cascading
process, after which subsequent steps involve a single failure
at a time caused by line overloading. This simplification states
that time can be divided into sufficiently small intervals,
allowing multiple near-simultaneous failures to be treated as
sequential events occurring in rapid succession.
The cascade scenarios are grouped by the severity of the
outage: Small (1 to 10%), Medium (10 to 30%), large (30
to 60%), and Very large (>60%) number of transmission
lines failed. Each dataset contains 30,000 cascading failure
scenarios, with 3,466 small, 13,303 medium, 10,108 large, and
3,123 very large cascades for IEEE-118, and 2,964, 12,443,
13,206, and 1,387, respectively, for IEEE-300. The objective
is to predict the severity label y ∈{0, 1, 2, 3}, representing
small, medium, large, and very large cascades, respectively.
2) System Model as a Graph: The power transmission
network is represented as a line graph G = (V, E), where
each node vi ∈V represents transmission line i, and each
edge (vi, vj) ∈E indicates that the two transmission lines
share a common bus. Unlike the conventional bus graph,
the line graph captures the connectivity among transmission
lines through which cascading failures propagate. For each
benchmark system, the line graph is constructed once and
remains fixed across cascading failure scenarios, while node
attributes evolve with the cascade.

IV. CASCADE PREDICTION FRAMEWORK AND MODEL
ARCHITECTURE
This section reviews and presents the class-based cascade
size prediction frameworks considered in this study. The
models are organized into three categories according to the
extent to which they incorporate structural information about
the system’s graph:
(A) Topology-agnostic baseline models, which operate
solely on tabular or sequential feature representations without
explicitly incorporating the network structure. The models in
this category include SVM, RF, DNN, FT-Transformer, and a
standalone LLM model. (B) Topology-aware standalone mod-
els, which incorporate explicit line-graph structure or structural
descriptors as part of their input. This category includes the
TAGAT and two standalone LLM models with topological
information. (C) Graph–LLM fusion model, which combines
both representations through a confidence-gated fusion mod-
ule.
A. Topology-Agnostic Baseline Models
The following five models serve as topology agnostic
baselines. Each is trained and evaluated using the same
train/validation/test splits as the other presented models, with-
out access to the line-graph adjacency or any structural de-
scriptor of the transmission network.


4
1) Support Vector Machine (SVM): An SVM classifier
with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel is used as a non-
parametric baseline. The RBF kernel enables the model to
learn nonlinear decision boundaries between cascade severity
classes.
2) Random Forest (RF): An RF classifier is used as
an ensemble tree-based baseline. By aggregating predictions
across a large number of decorrelated decision trees trained
on bootstrap-resampled subsets of the data, the RF model
provides a robust, low-variance baseline that does not require
explicit topological information.
3) Deep Neural Network (DNN):
The DNN baseline
consists of an input layer whose size equals the dimension of
the feature vector, depending on the size of the system and the
feature set, followed by four fully connected hidden layers of
widths 128, 128, 256, and 256, using ReLU activations and a
dropout rate of 20% for regularization. The network is trained
with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 1×10−4, using
categorical cross-entropy as the loss function, for 120 epochs
with a batch size of 64 and a validation split of 20%.
4) Feature Tokenizer Transformer (FT-Transformer): As
a more advanced topology-agnostic baseline for tabular data,
an FT-Transformer is also implemented. Unlike recurrent or
graph-based architectures, the FT-Transformer employs multi-
head self-attention directly on feature tokens, allowing global
interactions among input features without relying on explicit
graph topology modeling.
5)

of the fine-tuning is the standard causal language modeling
(CLM) loss.
Given a scenario prompt s and its associated label token y,
the loss is L = −log Pθ(y | s), which is computed only over
the final label token.

2) Aggregated Topology-Informed LLM (ATI-LLM):
While the LLM formulation described in Section IV.A operates
only on per-line power flow data, this topology-aware variant
of the standalone LLM classifier incorporates some aggregated
topological information in the form of natural language for
the classification. This model shares the same LLaMA-3-8B-
Instruct backbone and LoRA fine-tuning procedure detailed
in Section IV.A; the only distinction from the topology-
agnostic formulation lies in two additional information blocks
appended to the prompt after information block B1 in Fig. 1.
These information blocks include (B2) the GSP-based features
including GF1 to GF4, and (B3) the non-GSP-based global
features including GF5 to GF8, as explained in Section III.
To prevent information leakage beyond the first T observed
steps, GSP features in block (B2) are conditionally included
based on event occurrence. If both the first local maximum and
uncontrolled islanding occur, GF1–GF4 are included; if only
the local maximum occurs, GF1 and GF4 are included; and if
only islanding occurs, GF2 is included. If neither occurs, no
GSP feature values are provided. Unavailable indicators are
explicitly identified in the prompt.
3) Explicit Topology-Informed LLM (ETI-LLM): This
topology-aware variant of the standalone LLM classifier in-
corporates explicit knowledge of the network topology in the
form of adjacency matrix information in natural language
classification. This model shares the same LLaMA-3-8B-
Instruct backbone and LoRA fine-tuning procedure detailed in
the LLM presented previously; the only distinction from the
ATI-LLM lies in a fourth information block (B4), which is a
natural-language description of the line-graph adjacency that
lists, for each line, the lines adjacent to it (see Fig. 1). The
information blocks B1–B3 and the conditional disclosure of
the GSP features in (B2) are identical to those of the previous
variants, so the same reduced (B2) block, as discussed earlier,
is used whenever an event is not observed within the window.
C. Graph-LLM Fusion Network
The hybrid Graph-LLM Fusion framework includes two
branches: a TAGAT branch and an LLM branch with aggre-
gated topological features and unifies the two within a single
end-to-end classifier, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Since the TAGAT
branch uses explicit topological information, the chosen LLM
is the ATI-LLM. Note that the LLM branch receives the
reduced (B2) information block shown in Fig. 1 whenever a
GSP event is not observed within the window of size T, while
the TAGAT branch uses no GSP features and is unaffected.
The framework employs decision-level fusion, where class
logits from both branches are combined through a learned
confidence gate, which enables scenario-specific weighting of
graph-based and language-based predictions. The remainder
of this subsection first presents the confidence-gated fusion
module, which turns the graph and language embeddings into
a single prediction, and then the gate regularization terms and
the composite training objective used to train it.

cross-system performance in both transfer settings.

early-stage cascading failure severity prediction in power
transmission networks. The proposed confidence-gated fusion
framework combines topology-aware TAGAT representations
with the contextual learning capabilities of a LoRA-fine-tuned
LLaMA-3-8B model, leveraging complementary structural and
system-level information. Evaluations on the IEEE 118-bus
