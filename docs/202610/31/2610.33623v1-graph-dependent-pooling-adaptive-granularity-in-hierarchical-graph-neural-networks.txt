# ABSTRACT 

Graph Neural Networks are widely used to learn structural representations of rela-tional data. Hierarchical graph pooling learns multi-level representations by pro-gressively coarsening graphs; however, prior methods rely on predefined clus-ter numbers or fixed pooling ratios, limiting adaptability to individual graphs. We propose Graph-Dependent Pooling (GDPool), which dynamically determines pooling granularity for each graph. We analyze optimization conflicts among layer-wise objectives through gradient alignment, showing that layer-wise objec-tives at different pooling levels can send conflicting gradients to shared param-eters. GDPool uses self-attention to select graph-specific anchors representing structurally important nodes and constructs coarse graphs through soft node as-signment. Experiments on metal-organic framework and molecular graph bench-marks show that GDPool consistently achieves the best predictive performance across all benchmarks. Evaluations using modularity and the effective number of clusters show that GDPool achieves consistently higher modularity than existing methods while the number of active clusters decreases monotonically with depth. Sensitivity analyses confirm stable behavior across hyperparameter and architec-tural choices. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have become a widely used approach for learning from relational data, including molecules, crystal structures, and social networks (Gilmer et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2019). In molecular and materials property prediction, graph-based models represent atoms and bonds as graphs and directly predict graph-level properties (Xie & Grossman, 2018; Chen et al., 2019). Effective graph-level prediction requires aggregating node-level information into a represen-tation of the entire graph. While standard sum or mean readout aggregates all node representations in a single step, graph pooling progressively coarsens the graph to learn structural representations at multiple levels (Ying et al., 2018; Grattarola et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022). Hierarchical graph pooling has therefore emerged as a key approach for compressing complex graph structures while preserving salient information, with recent work also theoretically analyzing how pooling operations affect the expressive power of GNNs (Bianchi & Lachi, 2023). Hierarchical graph pooling involves two key design challenges. The first is determining how much to coarsen a graph. Clustering-based methods such as DiffPool, MinCutPool (Bianchi et al., 2020), and MemPool (Khasahmadi et al., 2020) typically require a predefined number of clusters, whereas selection-based methods such as Graph U-Nets (Gao & Ji, 2019), SAGPool (Lee et al., 2019), and ASAPool (Ranjan et al., 2020) rely on a predefined pooling ratio. To relax this fixed granularity, methods such as GMPool (Ko et al., 2023) and BN-Pool (Castellana & Bianchi, 2026) adapt the number of clusters to each input graph, but still require a hyperparameter specifying the maximum number of clusters. Moreover, learning graph-dependent coarsening consistently across multiple pooling levels remains an open challenge. The second challenge is how to learn these levels as a coherent hierarchy. Existing soft-assignment methods apply auxiliary objectives at individual levels (Ying et al., 2018; Bianchi et al., 2020); however, because each higher-level graph is constructed from the output of the preceding pooling operation, its supervision target recursively depends on 1arXiv:2610.33623v1 [cs.AI] 24 Oct 2026 Preprint earlier assignments. Consequently, supervising each level only through its own local objective does not necessarily ensure structural consistency across the hierarchy. To address these challenges, we propose Graph-Dependent Pooling (GDPool). GDPool identifies important nodes as graph-specific anchors and dynamically determines pooling granularity without a predefined number of clusters or pooling ratio. Self-attention estimates node importance, selects anchors, and softly assigns nodes to the selected anchors to construct a coarse graph. We further propose hierarchical supervision, which applies pooling objectives to the cumulative assignments so that every level is anchored to the original graph. GDPool thus learns graph-dependent hierar-chical representations. We evaluate predictive performance and hierarchy quality using modularity (Newman & Girvan, 2004) for structural cohesion, the effective number of clusters Keff (Zheng & Vedaldi, 2023) for cluster utilization, and gradient alignment (Sener & Koltun, 2018; Yu et al., 2020) among hierarchical objectives. The evaluation quantifies whether GDPool adapts to each graph while maintaining a consistent hierarchy. Our main contributions are as follows. • Graph-dependent pooling. We propose GDPool, which selects important nodes as an-chors via self-attention and thereby determines pooling granularity without a predefined number of clusters or pooling ratio. Each cluster is anchored at an actual node, which makes the hierarchy directly interpretable. • Gradient conflict analysis and hierarchical supervision. Measuring the gradient align-ment between the auxiliary objectives of different pooling layers, we identify two sources of conflict: layer-wise supervision and a pooling budget that does not fit the graph. We mit-igate the first by supervising the cumulative assignment of every level against the original graph, and the second by graph-dependent anchor selection. • Empirical validation across domains. GDPool achieves the best MAE on all five targets of four MOF and molecular benchmarks against nine existing pooling methods, with a monotonically decreasing number of active clusters and consistently higher modularity. GDPool thus forms coherent hierarchies rather than merely reducing the number of nodes. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 HIERARCHICAL GRAPH POOLING 

Graph pooling can be grouped into three categories: clustering, node selection, and edge contrac-tion (Grattarola et al., 2022). Clustering-based methods learn soft node-to-supernode assignments, including DiffPool, MinCutPool, and MemPool. TopKPool (Gao & Ji, 2019; Cangea et al., 2018; Knyazev et al., 2019), SAGPool (Lee et al., 2019), ASAPool, and MaxCutPool (Abate & Bianchi, 2025) select important nodes or local clusters, while EdgePool (Diehl, 2019) iteratively merges adja-cent nodes. Clustering-based pooling retains explicit assignments of original nodes, enabling direct analysis of hierarchical structures and cluster-level properties. In clustering-based pooling, selecting representative nodes and determining cluster numbers are key design choices. GAT (Velickovic et al., 2018) and graph Transformers (Dwivedi & Bresson, 2020; Ying et al., 2021; Kreuzer et al., 2021; Ramp´ aˇ sek et al., 2022) learn local and global node interac-tions through attention. While SAGPool and ASAPool rely on local scores and a fixed pooling ratio, GDPool scores nodes with graph-wide attention and lets the number of anchors follow each graph’s attention distribution. Unlike ASAPool, which further restricts cluster membership to h-hop neigh-borhoods, GDPool assigns every node to all anchors without locality constraints or additional learn-able parameters. More generally, prior pooling methods fix the number of clusters or the pooling ratio in advance, whereas clustering methods determine cluster numbers from data using eigengaps (Von Luxburg, 2007), nonparametric clustering (Kulis & Jordan, 2011), or modularity maximization (Newman, 2006; Blondel et al., 2008; Traag et al., 2019). In graph pooling, HGP-SL (Zhang et al., 2019) and SEP (Wu et al., 2022) consider adaptive structures, and GMPool (Ko et al., 2023) and BN-Pool also adapt cluster numbers to each graph. However, both must set a maximum number of clusters, and GMPool additionally requires O(N 3) eigendecomposition at each level. After pooling, the coarse graph should preserve the original structure while supporting stable learning across mul-tiple levels. Graph coarsening studies have explored multiresolution construction (Defferrard et al., 2016), spectral and cut preservation (Loukas, 2019; Loukas & Vandergheynst, 2018), and learning-based coarsening and scalability (Cai et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2021; Bacciu et al., 2023). These 2Preprint coarsening methods maintain the composed mapping from the original graph to each level, whereas learned pooling methods define their clustering objectives on each coarsened graph separately; to our knowledge, applying these objectives to the composed assignment at every level has not been studied. Hierarchical GNNs such as Graph U-Net alternate message passing and pooling, while GDPool uses a pre-norm architecture (Xiong et al., 2020) to stabilize these levels. 2.2 DEEP SUPERVISION FOR HIERARCHICAL LEARNING 

Hierarchical models derive effectiveness from a sequence of intermediate representations, making the supervision of intermediate levels an important design consideration. Deep supervision was introduced to improve the optimization and representation learning of deep networks by imposing auxiliary objectives directly on intermediate representations. Deeply-Supervised Nets (Lee et al., 2015) attach auxiliary classifiers to hidden layers, and GoogLeNet (Szegedy et al., 2015), HED (Xie & Tu, 2015), and UNet++ (Zhou et al., 2018) extend deep supervision to deeper and multi-scale architectures. Hierarchical graph pooling similarly employs layer-wise auxiliary objectives: DiffPool applies link prediction and entropy regularization to the adjacency and assignment at each level, and MinCutPool uses min-cut and orthogonality objectives. In both methods, each objective is defined with respect to the graph at the corresponding level, so the auxiliary objectives are defined locally at each level while sharing parameters. When multiple auxiliary objectives jointly optimize shared parameters, the relationship among the gradients of the objectives becomes critical. Multi-task learning addresses gradient interactions by formulating training as multi-objective optimization (Sener & Koltun, 2018), adjusting gradi-ent magnitudes or loss weights with GradNorm (Chen et al., 2018), applying uncertainty weighting (Kendall et al., 2018), or directly modifying conflicting gradients with PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020) and CAGrad (Liu et al., 2021). In particular, Du et al. (2018) weight auxiliary losses by the co-sine similarity between auxiliary and main-task gradients, showing that auxiliary supervision is not always aligned with the task objective. However, gradient interactions among layer-wise auxiliary objectives in hierarchical graph pooling have received limited attention. 

# 3 GRADIENT INTERACTIONS IN HIERARCHICAL LEARNING 

Hierarchical graph learning sequentially constructs graph representations at different levels from a single input graph. Layer-wise supervision assigns a separate objective to the representation at each pooling level, providing a direct learning signal for capturing meaningful information at each level. However, when objectives at different levels share parameters, the gradients of the objectives may point in different directions, so that an update improving the objective at one level degrades the ob-jective at another level. We define the interference between layer-wise objectives as gradient conflict, quantified by the directional alignment of the gradients that the objectives send to shared parame-ters. Measuring gradient conflict during training, we examine whether layer-wise objectives, each defined on its own coarsened graph, are compatible with learning a structurally coherent hierarchy. 3.1 GRADIENT CONFLICT IN HIERARCHICAL LEARNING 

Gradient conflict occurs when layer-wise objectives send gradients in opposing directions to shared parameters, so that improving one objective hinders another. To quantify gradient conflict, let L(l)aux 

denote the auxiliary objective at the l-th pooling level. For two levels l < L , we take the gra-dients of both objectives with respect to the lower-level assignment S(l), gl = ∂L(l)aux /∂S (l) and 

gL = ∂L(L)aux /∂S (l) (l = 1 in our experiments). Similar gradient directions at levels i and j indicate cooperative optimization, whereas opposing directions indicate that an update for one objective hin-ders the other. We quantify the directional relationship between two levels by gradient alignment. Positive alignment indicates that the two objectives favor similar update directions, whereas negative alignment signals likely gradient conflict; alignment near zero implies nearly orthogonal gradients with little mutual influence. Figure 1a illustrates the measurement procedure, in which the gradi-ents of the auxiliary objectives at each pooling level are computed and compared at S(l). Unlike a comparison of loss magnitudes, gradient alignment directly reveals the direction in which each layer-wise objective drives the shared parameters. Figure 1b shows the gradient alignment between 3Preprint the auxiliary objectives of DiffPool, MinCutPool, MemPool, and BN-Pool, measured during train-ing. The alignment of every baseline remains below 0.1 throughout training, either staying close to zero or falling below zero for extended periods. Layer-wise objectives therefore fail to provide cooperative learning signals, either conflicting with or barely influencing one another.

The observed gradient conflict suggests that applying a separate local objective at each level does not guarantee that the hierarchy is learned coherently. When layer-wise objectives send divergent gradients to shared parameters, learning at one level may remain incompatible with representation learning at another level, even if each objective is optimized individually. Satisfying the layer-wise objectives and preserving the structural relationships across the hierarchy can thus become distinct goals. Rather than assuming that layer-wise supervision alone yields a structurally coherent hierar-chy, supervision should explicitly reflect the relationships across levels, since the representations at all levels are derived from a single original graph through successive coarsening. Building on the analysis, we propose hierarchical supervision, which applies existing pooling objectives to the cu-mulative assignments so that the supervision at each level reflects the structural relationships across levels. Section 5.4 analyzes the effect of hierarchical supervision on GDPool, further examining how gradient conflict across levels changes when a fixed pooling granularity is not enforced. 

# 4 GRAPH -DEPENDENT HIERARCHICAL POOLING 

We propose GDPool, which adaptively determines pooling granularity according to the input graph. GDPool computes context-aware node importance by self-attention over the node representations and selects the most important nodes as graph-specific anchors. A coarse graph is then constructed through soft-assignment of each node to the selected anchors. GDPool thereby learns hierarchical representations whose granularity differs across graphs, without a predefined number of clusters or pooling ratio. 4.1 CONTEXT -AWARE ANCHOR SELECTION

As shown in Figure 2, GDPool is integrated into a hierarchical architecture that stacks GDformer blocks on a graph encoder, each block consisting of a Graph Feed-Forward Network (GFFN) fol-lowed by GDPool. Given an input graph G(l), the GFFN updates the node representations of G(l),after which GDPool constructs the coarse graph G(l+1) as the input to the next level. Repeated appli-cation of GDformer blocks yields hierarchical graph representations with graph-dependent pooling granularity. Because graphs within a mini-batch differ in size, the graphs at each level are padded to a common size, with a mask excluding padded nodes from attention, assignment, and readout. Masked readouts from all levels are aggregated for graph-level prediction. Appendix B.1 illustrates the GDPool layer. To preserve the structural information of the original graph at each level, we adopt the auxiliary link prediction objective LLink and the entropy regularization LEnt of DiffPool. Under layer-wise super-vision, existing pooling methods learn structural consistency between the input and pooled graphs using the assignment matrix of the current level. In hierarchical pooling, however, the original nodes are connected to the coarse nodes at higher levels through a chain of assignment matrices. Hierar-chical supervision therefore defines the objective at each level on the assignment accumulated along the hierarchy rather than on the assignment of the corresponding level alone. Based on the accumulated assignment, we apply link prediction and entropy regularization at each level so that the hierarchical representations maintain the structural relationships of the original graph. # 5 EXPERIMENTS 

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation to assess the effectiveness of GDPool in graph property pre-diction and hierarchical graph representation learning. For a fair comparison, all pooling methods within each dataset share the same encoder, GFFN, and training settings, so that only the pooling op-erator differs. On the MOF benchmarks, graphs constructed from 3D crystal structures are encoded by MMAGNN (Teng et al., 2025), with SAGEConv (Hamilton et al., 2017) used in each GFFN. On the molecular benchmarks, the graph encoder consists of four GINEConv (Hu et al., 2020) layers to exploit the discrete bond-type edge features provided by the datasets, with GINEConv also used in each GFFN. We set the GFFN expansion ratio to 2 and the entropy weight λ to 0.05, whose effects are further analyzed in Section 5.5. The details of the architectures used in each experiment and the hyperparameter selection procedure are described in Appendix B.2. 

Datasets. We evaluate GDPool on four graph regression datasets spanning MOFs and molecular graphs: ARC-MOF (Burner et al., 2023), hMOF (Wilmer et al., 2012), ZINC (Dwivedi et al., 2023), and QM9 (Ramakrishnan et al., 2014). ARC-MOF and hMOF contain relatively large material graphs and are used for gas adsorption prediction, while ZINC and QM9 contain smaller molecular graphs and are used for solubility and dipole moment prediction, respectively. 5.1 COMPARISON WITH EXISTING POOLING METHODS 

To validate the performance of GDPool, we use the four benchmark datasets. The targets are the CO 2 uptake at 0.15 bar on ARC-MOF, the CO 2 uptake at 0.1 bar and the CH 4 uptake at 0.9 bar on hMOF, the constrained solubility on ZINC, and the dipole moment on QM9. Predictive performance is evaluated with Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), and R2, where lower MAE and RMSE and higher R2 indicate better prediction. As shown in Table 1, GDPool achieves the best MAE on every prediction task across the datasets compared. GDPool therefore improves consistently on MOF and molecular graphs with different characteristics, rather than on a particular dataset or property. These results indicate that the anchor-based pooling of GDPool is effective for graph representation learning across graph types. The same trend holds for RMSE and 

R2 (Tables 5 and 6). ASAPool and MaxCutPool did not train stably in our setting; we report their results as obtained and discuss this in Appendix E. 5.2 HIERARCHICAL CLUSTERING BEHAVIOR 

To analyze hierarchical graph representations, we evaluate clustering behavior according to the pool-ing process. We compare GDPool with DiffPool, MinCutPool, MemPool, and BN-Pool, which directly use soft assignment. We measure the structural cohesion of the assignment produced at each pooling level with modularity. As shown in Table 2, the modularity of the baselines collapses once pooling levels are stacked, whereas GDPool maintains high modularity at every level. A near-uniform soft assignment gives Q ≈ 0 by construction, so these values indicate assignments that do not follow the graph structure; such degenerate assignments have been noted previously (Bianchi et al., 2020). GDPool therefore aggregates nodes according to the structural relationships of the input graph rather than merely reducing the number of nodes. We further compare the nominal number of clusters with Keff , the number of clusters actually used during pooling. Natural hierar-chical coarsening activates fewer clusters at higher levels as pooling proceeds. The baselines show no clear reduction: Keff at the first level is smaller than at later levels, or most clusters remain active at every level, leaving little difference from the nominal number. Stacking pooling levels there-fore does not necessarily reduce the granularity of the representations. In contrast, Keff of GDPool decreases with depth, using more clusters at early levels to represent fine-grained structure while activating fewer clusters at later levels to form coarse-grained representations. GDPool thus forms an appropriate pooling granularity by selecting anchors from the node representations of each graph instead of enforcing a fixed number of clusters. Section 3 analyzed how objectives at different pooling levels can produce optimization conflicts in hierarchical graph learning. We measure the gradient similarity of GDPool during training to analyze the effect of hierarchical supervision on gradient interactions. As shown in Figure 1b, GDPool with hierarchical supervision maintains an alignment of approximately 0.6 to 0.8 through-out training, whereas conventional pooling methods with layer-wise supervision remain below 0.1 or become intermittently negative. We further compare the same GDPool architecture under hier-archical and layer-wise supervision. Figure 6a in Appendix H.1 shows the alignment over training epochs, where hierarchical supervision keeps the alignment both high and stable. Alignment de-pends not only on the supervision scheme but also on the anchor selection rule. Under the same hierarchical supervision, the default fixed rules lose gradient alignment and the matched ratio de-cays throughout training, whereas GDPool remains at about 0.6 in Figure 6b. Hierarchical super-vision and graph-dependent anchor selection thus mitigate the conflict from layer-wise supervision and ill-fitting budgets, respectively. The advantage of GDPool does not hinge on the supervision scheme: supervising only the final cumulative assignment matches hierarchical supervision in accu-racy (MAE 0.152 vs. 0.151), whereas hierarchical supervision yields a markedly more compact and coherent hierarchy. Layer-wise supervision degrades both accuracy and structure (Appendix H.2). Under identical hierarchical supervision, GDPool achieves lower MAE than DiffPool at either λ on hMOF (Appendix H.3). 5.5 SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS 

We analyze the sensitivity of GDPool to the entropy loss weight λ, the number of hierarchical pool-ing levels, and the GFFN expansion ratio. As shown in Table 12, MAE is insensitive to λ, whereas modularity increases overall as λ grows. Keff shows no clear trend but rises slightly overall. We adopt λ = 0 .05 , which improves modularity without loss of predictive performance. To analyze the effect of pooling depth, we compare models trained with one and two pooling levels. As shown in Table 13, GDPool achieves lower MAE than existing pooling methods in both settings, together with the best modularity at the first pooling level. The initial clustering behavior of GDPool therefore re-mains stable under changes in pooling depth. In Table 14, varying the GFFN expansion ratio makes little difference in performance. The analyses cover a GINEConv-based model and a SAGEConv-based model, with GDPool maintaining stable performance under hyperparameter and architectural changes in both cases. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

We proposed GDPool, which adaptively determines pooling granularity according to the structural characteristics of the input graph. GDPool selects a different number of anchors for each graph using the contextual importance computed by self-attention, constructing a coarse graph through soft-assignment of each node to the selected anchors. Hierarchical graph representations are thereby learned without fixing the number of clusters or the pooling ratio in advance. Our gradient alignment analysis reveals that auxiliary objectives at different pooling levels can send conflicting gradients to shared parameters. Supervising each level only through a local objective, as in existing practice, therefore does not guarantee coherent learning of the hierarchy. On the four benchmarks, ARC-MOF, hMOF, ZINC, and QM9, GDPool consistently outperformed all compared pooling methods. In the layer-wise analysis of modularity and Keff , existing methods either retain the nominal number of clusters at every level or vary Keff non-monotonically across levels, whereas Keff of GDPool decreases monotonically with depth while maintaining high modu-larity. GDPool thus learns a granularity suited to the structure of the input graph rather than merely reducing the number of nodes. Our study has several limitations. The number of anchors is determined by the mean attention score, so the selection criterion itself is not learned, and hierarchical supervision reuses existing clustering objectives rather than ones designed for cumulative assignments. Future work could explore learned granularity criteria and conflict-aware optimization, and evaluate GDPool on larger and more diverse graph-level tasks. Given the stable number of nodes per anchor across graph sizes and the high 9Preprint activation rates of the resulting clusters, GDPool can also be extended to assign predicted properties to cluster-level substructures. # B IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

B.1 GDP OOL LAYER 

A schematic depiction of the GDPool layer is illustrated in Figure 4. GDPool first uses the normal-ized node representations as queries and keys to score node importance, and selects anchor nodes for each graph based on these scores. It then derives a soft assignment of each node to the anchors and aggregates the values of the original nodes according to this assignment to produce the coarsened graph. B.2 MODEL CONFIGURATIONS 

The embedding dimension of the node representations was set to 64, and the batch size was fixed at 64. All models were trained with the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) with a weight decay of 10 −4. The learning rate followed a cosine annealing schedule (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017), with the maximum and minimum learning rates set to 10 −3 and 10 −5, respectively. On the ARC-MOF and hMOF datasets, all models were trained for 100 epochs with three hierarchical pooling levels; for pooling methods that require the number of clusters to be specified in advance, the number of clusters at each level was set to 32, 16, and 8, respectively. On the ZINC and QM9 datasets, all models were trained for 300 epochs with two hierarchical pooling levels, and the number of clusters at each level was set to 12 and 6, respectively, for such methods. # C COMPLEXITY AND COMPUTATIONAL COST 

Table 3 reports the training time per epoch and the peak GPU memory measured on the hMOF dataset. In terms of training time, GDPool takes 236 .21 seconds per epoch, which is faster than MemPool, comparable to MaxCutPool, and about 1.8× that of SAGPool, the fastest method. It is also more than 8× faster than EdgePool, which performs iterative edge contraction. In contrast, its peak memory usage of 10 .56 GB is higher than that of all other methods. This is because GDPool must retain both the attention logits and the softmax outputs, and additionally constructs the as-signment to the anchors on top of them. Moreover, since the graphs in a mini-batch are padded to 

Cmax = max b Cb, where Cb is the number of anchors in the b-th graph, a single graph with many anchors forces the memory of the entire batch to be allocated accordingly. As shown in Figure 3, the number of anchors scales with graph size and the size distribution of hMOF graphs is right-skewed. The distribution of anchor counts has a long right tail, so the actual memory usage is governed by the maximum within a batch rather than by the average number of anchors. The relatively large stan-dard deviation of the training time is likewise attributable to the variation of Cmax across batches. Since the number of anchors is determined during the forward pass, batches cannot be formed by exact anchor count at the data loading stage. Because the number of anchors scales with graph size, however, bucketing graphs by size would largely reduce this overhead; we leave this to future work. # D DATASETS DETAILS 

The statistics of the benchmark datasets used in this paper, ARC-MOF, hMOF, ZINC, and QM9, are reported in Table 4. The ARC-MOF dataset is split with the same ratio by a coverage-aware ran-dom procedure: for each element, from rarest to most common, one unassigned structure containing it is placed in each split so that every element appears in all three splits, and the remaining struc-tures are assigned at random. ARC-MOF contains several variants of the same parent structure (e.g., different solvent-removal treatments); because a single split is shared by all methods, the methods are compared under identical conditions, but absolute errors may be optimistic. The 3D atomic coordinates available in QM9 are not used. All targets are used in their native units, without further normalization or rescal-ing. For hard assignments this reduces to the standard modularity, and near-uniform soft assign-ments yield Q ≈ 0. At level l > 1, A denotes the coarse adjacency produced by the preceding level, so modularity values at deeper levels are computed on progressively smaller graphs and are not directly comparable across methods that coarsen at different rates. The effective number of clusters Keff measures how many clusters are actually used (Zheng & Vedaldi, 2023). For each graph, the total assignment mass received by each cluster is normalized into a distribution over clusters, and Keff is the perplexity of this distribution, i.e., the exponential of its entropy. It equals the number of clusters when all clusters receive equal mass and approaches one when the assignment collapses onto a single cluster. Both metrics are computed per graph and averaged over the test set. 

# G ANCHOR SELECTION ANALYSES 

G.1 ADAPTIVE POOLING GRANULARITY 

We discuss in detail the visualization of pooling granularity presented in Section 5.3. For each graph in the hMOF test set, we recorded the number of original nodes N together with the number of anchors C selected at the first pooling level, averaged over three seeds. Note that, unlike Keff , C

refers to the number of anchors actually selected by the anchor selection step. As shown in Figure 3a, 

C grows approximately linearly with N . A linear fit through the origin gives C ≈ 0.15 N (Pearson 17 Preprint 

r = 0 .96 ), and C/N lies between 0.10 and 0.17 for 80% of the graphs (10th–90th percentiles, median 0.13). GDPool thus behaves like a ratio-based pooling method whose ratio is not set in advance but emerges from the anchor criterion pj > ¯p, and the emergent ratio differs from both a fixed number of clusters ( K = 32 ) and the default pooling ratio of 0.5. Around this trend, graphs of similar size receive different numbers of anchors. Within a 50-node size range, C varies by a factor of about 1.6 between the 10th and 90th percentiles, so the granularity reflects not only the size of each graph but also its structure. Figure 3b shows the same data as nodes per anchor, N/C .GDPool keeps this quantity within a narrow range, whereas a fixed K = 32 would let it grow from 1 to 40 across the graph sizes in the test set. On average, GDPool selects 19.60 anchors per graph, of which Keff = 17 .35 are active, corresponding to an activation rate of approximately 89%. In contrast, existing pooling methods either fail to form proper clusters or activate fewer than 20% of their clusters. This shows that GDPool not only allocates anchors in proportion to graph size but also actually utilizes most of the clusters it forms. The motivation for graph-dependent granularity is interpretability. Properties of both materials and molecules are governed by local substructures, such as metal nodes and linkers in MOFs or func-tional groups in molecules, and we ultimately aim to attribute predicted properties to such substruc-tures at the cluster level, which we leave to future work. This requires cluster sizes set by local structure rather than by graph size. A fixed number of clusters cannot provide this. A fixed pooling ratio keeps the cluster size constant but assigns the same budget to every graph of the same size regardless of its structure, whereas GDPool varies the budget among graphs of the same size. G.2 SEED ROBUSTNESS OF ANCHOR SELECTION 

To assess the robustness of anchor selection to the choice of random seed, we measured the consis-tency of both the number of selected anchors and their node identities across three different seeds. To quantify the consistency of the number of selected anchors, we used the one-way random-effects ICC, i.e., ICC(1,1) in the notation of Shrout & Fleiss (1979). Treating each graph as the unit of measurement and each random seed as a repeated measurement, we compute the ICC as follows: 

ICC = MS B − MS W

MS B + ( s − 1)MS W

, (6) where MS B is the between-graph mean square, which captures the variation across graphs, MS W

is the within-graph mean square, which captures the variation across seeds within the same graph, and s is the number of random seeds. An ICC close to 1 therefore indicates that the variation arising from differences between graphs dominates the variation induced by the random seed, i.e., that the number of anchors is stable with respect to the seed. This experiment was conducted on the first pooling layer of the model trained on the CH 4@0.9 of the hMOF dataset. The within-graph standard deviation across seeds ( √MS W ) was 3.02 , whereas the standard deviation of the per-graph mean anchor count was 17 .54 , yielding an ICC of 0.971 . This demonstrates that the number of anchors selected for the same graph remains consistent even when the random seed is changed. Because C scales with N , part of this agreement reflects graph size. The ICC of the size-normalized count C/N is 0.67, so the graph-specific deviations from the size trend are also largely reproducible across seeds. To assess the consistency of the selected nodes as well as their number, we additionally measured the Jaccard similarity between anchor sets. For each pair of seeds, we computed the Jaccard similarity per graph and averaged it over all graphs; the final score is the mean over the three seed pairs. Table 7 reports this score together with a random baseline that selects the same number of nodes uniformly at random for each graph. The anchors selected under different seeds overlap far more than expected from random selection. These results show that the anchor selection of GDPool is stable under seed changes in terms of both the number of anchors and the identity of the selected nodes. Hierarchical supervision differs from layer-wise supervision only in the arguments of the objec-tives: at level l, the former evaluates LLink and LEnt on the accumulated assignment S(l)Hier against the original adjacency A(0) , the latter on the local assignment S(l) against the coarsened adjacency 

A(l−1) . The two schemes share the first-level term, and the substitution above it changes the op-timization in two ways. First, the reference graph becomes fixed. Under layer-wise supervision, 

A(l−1) is produced by the lower levels and moves during training, and each objective requires only that the pooled graph and the assignment agree with each other: a near-uniform assignment yields an almost constant A(l) that another near-uniform assignment matches up to scale, so no level is required to follow the input structure, consistent with the near-uniform assignments under layer-wise supervision in Table 10. Under hierarchical supervision, every level is compared with the same data-defined graph A(0) . Second, the objectives become coupled: since S(l)Hier = S(1) · · · S(l), each assignment matrix receives gradients from every objective at or above its level, all measured against the same graph, which is reflected in the higher and more stable alignment in Figure 6a. Final-level supervision keeps only the l = L term of Eq. 4. Table 10 compares the three schemes. Final-level su-pervision matches hierarchical supervision in accuracy while still outperforming all baselines, so the predictive advantage of GDPool stems from the operator rather than the supervision. The interme-diate terms of Eq. 4 instead shape the hierarchy: without them, only the final partition must preserve the input structure, and the first level retains more anchors with lower modularity. Layer-wise su-pervision degrades both accuracy and structure. Under layer-wise supervision, whose alignment is positive but unstable in Figure 6a, the first-level Keff of GDPool nearly equals the number of an-chors (65.3 of 65.8), yet its modularity collapses to zero in Table 10. This indicates a near-uniform assignment that trivially maximizes Keff rather than clusters that follow the graph structure. Across supervision schemes and anchor rules, reduced or unstable alignment thus co-occurs with a failure in one of the two properties required for downstream analysis (Appendix G.3): fixed rules leave first-level anchors inactive, whereas layer-wise supervision yields assignments that do not follow the graph structure. Only GDPool with hierarchical supervision attains both high activation and high modularity, together with high and stable alignment.
