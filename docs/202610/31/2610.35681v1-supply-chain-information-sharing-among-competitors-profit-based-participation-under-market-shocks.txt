Abstract 

Sharing information can help coordinate supply chain decisions in response to mar-ket shocks. Yet, firms must weigh these benefits against the risk of strengthening competitors. We study when competing firms voluntarily join an information-sharing platform to exchange information about market shocks while retaining operational au-tonomy. Firms differ in information capabilities and decide whether to participate be-fore private signals are realized, creating stochastic information asymmetry. We model participation and quantity decisions as a two-stage dynamic game with incomplete information. For two horizontal competitors, we derive closed-form equilibrium partic-ipation strategies that reveal how information power and production efficiency shape participation. Numerical analysis examines more complex supply chain networks. We find that supply chain characteristics impact participation and the level of information asymmetry is a key factor in the equilibrium platform profiles. Firms with similar information power may benefit from joining forces to reduce their competitive disad-vantage against higher information power competitors. Cost-inefficient firms may be less willing to participate because their competitors can exploit shared information, and uncertainty can serve as a competitive shield by restricting the actions of more efficient competitors. However, when several competitors join the platform, a cost-inefficient firm may also choose to participate to avoid an informational disadvantage. 

Keywords: information-sharing platform, quantity competition, dynamic game, stochastic information asymmetry. 

# 1 Introduction 

Supply chain firms’ ability to adapt their operations in response to changes in market conditions is key to improving supply chain performance, especially when disruptions or market fluctuations cause demand shocks (Eckstein et al., 2015). Timely access to supply chain information determines this adaptability (Topan et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2019). However, in most supply chains, information flows indirectly through orders or shipments, requiring firms to infer market conditions retrospectively rather than observe them directly

Information asymmetry is further amplified in competitive environments, where some firms invest in more powerful forecasting or other sensing efforts, resulting in better prediction capabilities of market disruption than other firms (Shin and Tunca, 2010). When firms rely on private information and respond to market shocks asynchronously, coordination across the supply chain becomes more difficult. For example, asymmetric information creates challenges for designing effective coordination mechanisms for replen-ishment and inventory planning policies (Ha, 2001; Sahin and Robinson, 2002). This is because staggered information flow causes undersupply during the actual demand phase and oversupply after demand dissipates. This was observed during the COVID-19 pan-demic, which disrupted both supply and demand, with demand for some products surging while demand for others fell sharply. Delayed and decentralized responses led firms to join markets or expand production based on incomplete information, resulting in excess in-ventories and falling prices, as observed in China’s mask market (Sixth Tone, 2020). Such situations illustrate how supply chains may have the collective capacity to respond to shocks but fail to do so in a timely or efficient manner because of information asymmetry. Numerous studies have shown that sharing operational information, such as observed shocks and changing demand or market parameters, across supply chain partners allows firms to improve responsiveness, reduce uncertainty, and enhance overall supply chain per-formance (Cachon and Fisher (2000), Lee et al. (2000), Chen et al. (2000), Ivanov (2021), Li et al. (2017), Wong et al. (2021), Costantino et al. (2014), Gu et al. (2021),Huo et al. (2014)). Beyond overall system improvements, firms with more information may also choose to share it for their own benefit. For example, Walmart shares real-time sales and inven-tory data with suppliers through Retail Link, improving suppliers’ demand forecasting and production decisions and strengthening Walmart’s competitive advantage. These well-established benefits of information sharing in supply chains have been stud-ied extensively, yet this literature commonly assumes firms willingly share information or considers information sharing only through bilateral relationships in supply chain contracts (Shen et al., 2019). However, in practice, competing firms rarely participate in information exchange platforms. Consequently, there exists a gap between the proposed benefits of 2information sharing and the practical reluctance of competing firms to do so. Given the limited understanding of what drives competing firms to share information (Guan et al., 2020), we study competing firms’ decisions to participate in information-sharing platforms. We consider a set of horizontally competing firms that retain full au-tonomy over whether to join an information-sharing platform and over their operational decisions. Firms decide whether to join a centralized information-sharing platform, and this decision is made by firms before knowing whether they or their competitors will acquire pri-vate information (what is referred to as stochastic information asymmetry). If a firm joins the platform, it commits to sharing information it detects about market conditions, and the platform distributes this information to all participating firms. Following the participation decisions, firms make quantity decisions to maximize their own expected profits based on their available information, either their private information, if they do not participate in the platform, or, if they do participate, the shared information through the platform. The key question for each firm is whether the benefits of information sharing outweigh the loss of competitive advantage from sharing their private information. We explore equilibrium participation decisions under stochastic information asymmetry and study how firms’ information capabilities, competitive interactions, and network struc-ture affect their incentives to participate in information-sharing. In doing so, this work makes the following key contributions. First, we analyze competing firms’ information-sharing decisions as strategic decisions made before they know which firms will acquire private information, and thus before they know whether they will act as an information provider or an information recipient. Second, we characterize equilibrium participation strategies for two horizontally competing firms and show how differences in information power and production efficiency affect participation. When both firms have equal infor-mation power, the higher-cost firm is the participation bottleneck. When production costs are equal, the firm with greater information power is the bottleneck. We also show that market sensitivity affects participation differently under these two asymmetries, motivating participation under cost asymmetry while discouraging it under information power asym-metry. Third, we examine information sharing among multiple firms within a networked competition structure, and numerically observe that denser networks reduce participation incentives. In sparse networks, participation is easier among firms with no common markets, 3while it becomes more difficult when firms compete in overlapping markets. This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related literature on information sharing in supply chains. In Section 3, we describe the problem setting and present a formulation. Section 4 presents an analysis of a small supply chain and explores the key factors in firms’ participation decisions. In Section 5, we provide numerical results to explore larger supply chains to further investigate platform participation. Section 6 provides conclusions and directions for future research. 

# 2 Literature Review 

We review related literature in three areas: studies evaluating supply chain information sharing, studies considering information sharing as a strategic decision in bilateral settings, and studies examining information sharing among multiple firms facilitated by a platform. 

# 2.1 Value of Supply Chain Information Sharing 

In this stream of literature, information sharing is not treated as a decision; instead, this literature evaluates the system-wide benefits of different information sharing policies. By exchanging key information such as supplier capacity (Choi et al., 2008; Yuan and Qiong, 2008), demand estimates (Lee et al., 1997; Chen et al., 2000; Lee et al., 2000; Lin et al., 2002; Zhao et al., 2002; Cho and Lee, 2013), or both (Yuan and Qiong, 2008), these studies show that information sharing in supply chains reduces uncertainty, leading to more effective material flow and reduced costs. When analyzing information sharing, these studies do not consider firms’ individual objectives. Firms are assumed to participate in information sharing regardless of whether they benefit individually. When firms maximize individual profits, incentives to share may diverge from system-wide objectives, particularly when private information provides a competitive advantage. For example, Zhang (2006) shows that inventory information exchange can reduce system-wide inefficiencies; yet it does not necessarily benefit all firms. Therefore, although related studies show the value of system-wide information sharing, firms’ individual objectives and the value of private information must be considered. 42.2 Information Sharing as a Decision Problem in Bilateral Set-tings 

This stream models information sharing as a strategic decision in bilateral settings, where firms exchange information through one-to-one relationships. A common setting focuses on interactions in which one firm is the sharer and the other firm is the receiver, generally in vertical interactions where downstream firms hold private demand information that is beneficial to upstream firms. Because the informed firm lacks incentives to share its information voluntarily, a large group of studies examines how contracts can induce information sharing. Shen et al. (2019) provides a comprehensive review. For example, manufacturers may purchase demand forecasts from retailers (Li and Zhang, 2002) or offer supply chain contracts that incentivize information sharing (Ha and Tong, 2008; Zhou et al., 2017; Chen and Sun, 2025). Beyond contracts, different mechanisms can also influence information sharing. For example, reducing production costs may motivate retailers to share demand information (Ha et al., 2017). Other studies find that explicit and tacit collusion between retailers may affect sharing incentives (Wu et al., 2021). Even when information is shared only on a one-to-one basis, other firms (not part of the one-to-one sharing agreement) introduce additional strategic dynamics into information sharing decisions. For example, when a retailer interacts with multiple upstream firms, the retailer may choose to share with none, one, or both firms, depending on contract timing and operational dynamics, such as nonlinear costs or competition intensity (Shang et al., 2016). Uninformed competitors may infer information indirectly, weakening incentives for voluntary information sharing (Zhang, 2002; Li, 2002). Furthermore, inference effects can even harm the intended information receiver (Cao and Chen, 2021). Similarly, shared information can be leaked to competitors, although contracts may prevent such leakage (Chen and ¨Ozer, 2019). In competitive multi-chain environments, retailers’ incentive for sharing private information bilaterally with their respective manufacturers depends explic-itly on rivals’ actions. Although mutual information sharing maximizes joint profits, each retailer can prefer to withhold information when the rival shares, leading to a prisoner’s dilemma–type outcome (Guan et al., 2020). Another group of studies considers settings in which both firms possess private informa-5tion but the firms decide whether to share information after private information is observed. In such environments, sharing incentives depend on both strategic interactions and signal characteristics. For example, when a supplier and a retailer have signals with different precision, sharing incentives depend on the variance and correlation of their signals (Zhang and Chen, 2013). In a coopetitive supply chain where one of the firms serves both as the supplier and the competitor of the other firm, sharing information may benefit one firm while harming the other (Wang et al., 2020). Raweewan and Ferrell (2018) develops a static game-theoretic model of bilateral information sharing among competing firms, where firms simultaneously decide whether to reveal private signals in a static bilateral information sharing game with exogenously determined payoffs. While this literature extensively examines strategic bilateral information sharing under asymmetric information and competition, all known work assume two key features. First, firms decide whether to share information after private information is observed, allowing them to make sharing decisions conditioned on realized informational advantage. Second, the interaction structure is predefined: the set of firms and the direction of information flows are given. Hence, these studies consider the environments in which the firms already have their private information when making information sharing decisions. In many supply chain environments, however, informational advantage varies across realizations, and firms need to make participation decisions in information sharing before observing the information. In these cases, participation decisions differ significantly from information sharing decisions based on realized signals. 

# 2.3 Platform-Based Information Sharing Among Multiple Firms 

Because information sharing through bilateral relationships is often difficult to manage when multiple firms operate in the supply chain, a stream of studies examines multi-agent settings, in which a platform facilitates information exchange among multiple firms simultaneously. Several studies analyze settings where a platform possesses superior information and selectively reveals it to competing firms (Hou et al., 2026; Li et al., 2021; Zhong et al., 2023; Zha et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021). Online retail platforms are a common example in this setting. In these models, the platform determines the level of information to reveal to each participant, while firm participation is assumed to be given. The focus is on 6how such a platform affects competition and operational outcomes, rather than on firms’ incentives to join the platform. Massari and Giannoccaro (2021) studies information sharing among competing firms using agent-based simulation, and finds that information sharing can improve performance even under competitive environments. However, participation is assumed rather than derived from equilibrium behavior, so firm-level incentives are not explicitly modeled. These studies highlight the role of platforms in facilitating information sharing among multiple competing firms. However, they assume firms will participate and do not con-sider their private incentives. Two notable exceptions are Wu et al. (2022) and Liu and Rahimian (2026), both of which study participation decisions in an information-sharing platform. In both settings, each participant has private demand information. Therefore, each competitor knows that, conditioned on participation, it will both share its private in-formation and receive information from other participants. Specifically, Liu and Rahimian (2026) study privacy-preserving information sharing among firms competing in a single Cournot market. Their platform aggregates participants’ private demand signals, consid-ering a privacy preserving mechanism, with its own external information to provide back to participants an aggregate demand estimate. Wu et al. (2022) studies participation de-cisions in an information-sharing platform among competing garages with interdependent demands. In their setting, each garage has complete information about its own demand shock, and platform participation allows it to access competitors’ private demand informa-tion. In contrast, our work is interested in settings where firms do not yet know whether they will share or receive information when they decide participation. Although voluntary platform participation has been considered in these two papers, the conditions that shape participation and platform formation under stochastic information asymmetry, where the firms are uncertain about who will have private information, has not been studied. 

# 3 Problem Definition and Formulation 

We study firms’ participation decisions in an information-sharing platform within a quantity-competition setting in a perishable-product supply chain prone to shocks. Firms decide whether to participate before observing the market state and without knowledge of other 7firms’ private information. Therefore, platform participation is considered a strategic de-cision. A firm joins the platform only if participation yields a higher expected profit. Since one firm’s participation affects the information available to others and their quan-tity decisions, we model the participation decision in the information-sharing platform as a two-stage dynamic game. In the first stage, firms decide whether to participate, and in the second stage, they make their quantity decisions given the realized platform configura-tion. Our goal is to analyze the information sharing participation decisions under different supply chain settings. In the following part, we first present the underlying supply chain environment. We then introduce the two-stage game structure and the information-sharing platform, and formulate the second-stage quantity decision and the first-stage participation decision.

# 3.2 Information-sharing Platform 

Market shocks create challenges for efficient quantity decisions, leading to under- or over-supply. The information-sharing platform aims to improve supply chain performance by reducing information asymmetry among participating firms through the sharing of private signals. Firms’ participation decisions can be driven by two motives: improving their own access to market information to support better decision-making and reducing uncertainty regarding other firms’ information state. If every firm observes the market shock but is unaware of other firms’ observations, they will produce more than what they would under complete information. This creates oversupply in the market, which can be avoided by information sharing. The process that governs the firms’ platform participation decision can be modeled as a two-stage dynamic game. In the first stage, firms simultaneously decide whether to participate in the information-sharing platform before observing the realized market state or other firms’ private information. Each set of participating firms represents a unique 10 platform participation configuration (hereafter referred to as configuration ), denoted by a binary vector ⃗ V ∈ { 0, 1}|F| , where Vi = 1 indicates that Firm i participates in the platform. Once the platform is formed, in the second stage, a market shock may occur, and firms choose their quantities based on the observed information conditional on the re-alized platform configuration ⃗ V in a networked Cournot competition under uncertainty. Nonparticipating firms rely on their own private information, while participants share the platform’s accumulated information power, denoted by pplatform (⃗ V ) for a platform configu-ration ⃗ V , defined as the probability that at least one participant detects an existing market shock. (ii) High information power (Figure 3b): When both firms have high information power, sharing information does not lead to a significant informational advantage, since the competitor is likely to observe the shock as well. Instead, the platform helps firms resolve uncertainty about each other’s types, enabling them to choose production quantities based on more accurate beliefs. (iii) High shock probability (Figure 3c): When the shock is a low-probability event, the signals become strategically valuable, and firms prefer to keep it private to maintain in-formational advantage. On the other hand, as the probability of a shock increases, the value of private information decreases, while the benefit of eliminating information asymmetry remains, making firms more willing to share information through the platform. In summary, under conditions (ii) and (iii), the private signal has limited value, whereas under condition (i), the adverse economic consequences of information disclosure are limited for homogeneous firms. 16 4.2 Heterogeneous Firms

# 5 Numerical Study: Networked Competition Structure Effects on Participation 

The two-firm analysis examines settings in which firms compete directly and explores how firm and market characteristics affect platform formation. When more than two firms interact across multiple markets, firms are subject to both direct and indirect competition, and participation decisions depend on the broader network competition structure, which is determined by the topology of the network connecting firms to markets. Thus, in this section, we conduct a numerical study of supply chains comprising three firms and four markets, varying the network topology in addition to the other factors captured in Section 4. Our goal is to examine whether observations in the direct-competition environment extend to more complex supply chain network competition structures, and to identify how changes in supply chain competition density and firms’ exposure to local competition affect firms’ participation decisions in the presence of varying information power and shock magnitude. In the numerical study, we set the baseline market size to aj = a = 1000 for each Market j and the shock probability to ω = 0 .1. We assume a uniform distribution of shock locations across markets, with rj = r = 0 .25 for all j. Other parameter variants are created by modifying this baseline setting and are described in the subsequent sections.

# 6 Summary 

In this paper, we study the participation of horizontally competing supply chain firms in a centralized, multi-firm information-sharing platform to share market shock signals that affect demand. We consider firms with different information capabilities and the participation problem prior to the realization of private signals, thereby creating a stochastic information-asymmetry environment. The key question for each firm is whether the benefits of information sharing outweigh the loss of competitive advantage from sharing their private information. We develop a game-theoretic model to identify the supply chain characteristics that shape firms’ incentives to participate in the platform. We first analyze the equilibrium participation strategies when there are two competing firms, providing closed-form results for equilibrium participation behavior that highlight the effect of information power and production efficiency on firms’ participation decisions. Numerical results, capturing more complex competition network structures, show that both network structure and the position of each firm in the network critically influence participation incentives. Our findings reveal how supply chain characteristics impact participation in informa-tion sharing. First, the level of information asymmetry is a key factor in the equilibrium platform profile. Firms with similar information power may benefit from joining forces to reduce their competitive disadvantage against higher information power competitors. 25 Second, cost-inefficient firms may be less willing to participate because their competitors can exploit shared information, and uncertainty can serve as a competitive shield by re-stricting the actions of more efficient competitors. However, when several competitors join the platform, a cost-inefficient firm may also choose to participate to avoid an informa-tional disadvantage. Third, market sensitivity affects the competitive role of information and uncertainty. In highly sensitive markets, where firms have less flexibility to adjust their production quantities, information becomes more valuable. At the same time, uncer-tainty becomes more challenging for cost-inefficient firms. Consequently, greater market sensitivity increases the reluctance of high-information power firms to participate, while cost-inefficient firms are more willing to participate. Lastly, we analyze how supply chain network topology shapes information sharing incentives among competing firms. Although competing firms are generally considered to be reluctant to share information, our results show that they may do so when information sharing creates an opportunity to redistribute their efforts between the markets they serve. Specifically, strategic information sharing may reduce the intensity in shared markets by enabling firms to focus on the markets where they have stronger positions. There are a number of directions open for future research. First, this work assumes the signals being shared are truthful, and future research could explore mechanisms that penal-ize untruthful reporting. Further, different disruption settings and types of information to share could be considered. In addition, future research could extend the single-period set-ting to multiple operational periods with a defined participation horizon that captures how updates in beliefs affect participation. Another direction worth exploring is how to design platform mechanisms to increase participation. For example, firms could be compensated to participate by creating reward allocation schemes funded by participation fees collected from firms benefiting more from the platform. Finally, in this work, we assume that firms make their decisions based on financial gains alone, omitting privacy concerns, to isolate the impact of supply chain conditions on platform benefits, but future work could explore privacy-preserving methods to encourage information sharing in competing supply chains.
