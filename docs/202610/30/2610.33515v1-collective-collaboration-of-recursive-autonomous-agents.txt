Collective Collaboration of Recursive Autonomous Agents
Ziqing Zhou†, Linqiang Hu, Yuning Chen, Hongda Zhang,
Zhao Huang, Ding Zhou, Haining Xu*
Abstract. Smart factories must translate order-level objectives into production tasks at multiple scales and combine the
capabilities of individual units into an overall delivery capability. To address repeated adaptation of organizational mechanisms
across equipment, workshops, and factories, as well as coordination across levels under changing production conditions, this
paper develops a framework for the collective collaboration of recursive autonomous agents (RAAs). A common task and
capability interface supports the recursive decomposition of production objectives from the top down and the aggregation
of conditional capabilities through peer collaboration from the bottom up. Composite units can therefore participate in
higher-level organizations as agents of the same kind. We formulate organizational models for leaf and composite units, specify
interface closure under composition and conditions for finite task expansion, and develop operational mechanisms linking task
decomposition, resource commitments, result aggregation, and coordination within the scope affected by change. A production
network integrating machining, inspection, and logistics across multiple workshops illustrates how a common organizational
kernel is instantiated at the equipment-service, workshop, and factory scales. The framework connects recursive organization
with explicit task responsibilities, capability conditions, and execution interfaces for manufacturing systems.
Keywords: recursive autonomous agents; collective collaboration; cross-level isomorphism; recursive task decomposition;
capability aggregation; dynamic reconfiguration
1 Introduction
Traditional factories typically organize production around
process requirements and managerial responsibilities. Pro-
duction management systems develop order and resource
plans, workshops dispatch work to stations and equipment,
and local control systems execute the required operations.
This organization provides clear responsibilities and supports
continuous production when products, processes, and re-
source configurations remain relatively stable. As batch sizes
decrease, product mixes change, and delivery requirements
become more demanding, production schedules must respond
more frequently to equipment failures, fluctuations in pro-
cessing rates, and material delays. Revising an existing plan
therefore involves not only individual equipment operations
but also interactions among process handovers, shared re-
sources, and order due dates. Manufacturing control research
has consequently expanded from plan execution within fixed
structures to distributed decision-making and production-
system reconfiguration in response to change [1, 2].
Smart factories strengthen the connection between pro-
duction operations and management through equipment con-
nectivity, state sensing, cyber-physical systems, and data-
driven decisions. The cyber-physical production systems
discussed by Monostori et al. integrate computation, com-
munication, and physical processes, providing a basis for
synchronized production-state information and decisions at
multiple scales [3]. The 5C architecture of Lee et al. orga-
nizes the progression from data connection and information
conversion to cognition and configuration [4]. These de-
velopments allow manufacturing resources to participate in
task allocation according to their current states and extend
production management from periodic planning to rolling op-
timization and event-driven rescheduling [5]. Smart factories
thus provide the technical foundations for connecting opera-
tional information with production decisions. Their further
development requires a coherent organizational process that

This coherence depends on how production units are orga-
nized as well as on decision quality. Local optimization by
individual machines does not by itself establish a deliverable
workshop capability. Similarly, information sharing among
workshops still requires coordination of process handovers,
transport windows, and shared resources. If equipment, work-
shops, and factories use separate task representations and
coordination procedures, adding production units or chang-
ing management boundaries can require repeated adaptation
of interfaces and organizational logic across levels. The re-
sulting research question is how to reuse production-task
organization as units are composed, while preserving differ-
ences in domain activities and communicating local changes
to the appropriate coordination scope.
Recursive autonomous agents (RAAs) organize produc-
tion through individual autonomy and collective collabora-
tion. Here, engineering autonomy denotes a unit’s ability to
select and revise its execution strategy within specified objec-
tives, constraints, and authorization. Collective collaboration
connects the tasks, resources, and handover conditions of
multiple units so that their local execution jointly satisfies an
overall objective. An RAA factory is therefore a recursively
composed production organization: equipment or operational
units collaborate with peers to establish workshop-level ca-
pabilities, and workshops further establish factory-level ca-
pabilities. The composite entity continues to participate in
higher-level collaboration as an agent with a defined identity,
capability, and responsibility boundary. Figure 1 outlines
the relationship among traditional manufacturing, connected
smart factories, and recursive RAA organization.
The defining feature of recursion is that composition pre-
serves the same kind of organizational capability. From the
top down, order objectives are decomposed into workshop
and equipment tasks, with subtasks retaining the same con-
tract until they map to executable skills. From the bottom
up, members establish joint arrangements subject to process,

Holonic and fractal manufacturing have already investi-
gated the aggregation, self-similarity, and dynamic recon-
figuration of autonomous entities. PROSA explicitly sep-

algorithms; fractal manufacturing builds self-similar organi-
zations from recurring functional modules; and ADACOR
examines the dynamic relationship between overall coordi-
nation and local self-organization [6–9]. Building on these
foundations, this paper specifies RAA recursion through a
common task contract, conditional capability aggregation, a
reusable organizational kernel, and collaborative reconfigu-
ration within the scope affected by change. The focus is on
how recursion connects production-task expansion with the
formation of composite capabilities.
The main contributions are: (1) a unified organizational
model for leaf and composite agents, with conditions for
cross-level interface consistency, closure under composi-
tion, and finite task expansion; (2) a recursive collaboration
mechanism connecting task decomposition, peer negotiation,
resource commitments, and result aggregation, including
rules for determining coordination scope after task or capa-
bility changes; and (3) a cross-scale task mapping in a multi-
workshop machining–inspection–logistics network, together
with an implementation architecture based on a common
organizational kernel and configurable domain components.
2 Related Work
2.1 Manufacturing Reconfiguration and Cyber-Physical
Production Systems
Adapting manufacturing systems to changing demand re-
quires coordinated adjustments to physical resources, control
functions, and production organization. Koren et al. in-
troduced reconfigurable manufacturing systems, in which
adaptable machines, controllers, and system-design meth-
ods allow production capacity and functionality to change
with demand [2]. This work connects reconfigurability with
system structure: adaptation encompasses both runtime task
rescheduling and changes in resource configurations and con-
trol structures. For collaboration across equipment and work-
shops, resource modularity provides a basis for composition,
while capability descriptions, task delegation, and handover
constraints must still be established among modules.
Cyber-physical production systems further connect manu-
facturing entities with their digital representations. Monos-
tori et al. discuss advances in sensing, connectivity, and col-
laboration through the integration of manufacturing science,
computation, and communication [3]. The 5C architecture of
Lee et al. organizes data connection, conversion, networked
analysis, cognition, and configuration to bring operational
data into decision-making [4]. Reconfiguration and cyber-
physical integration thus provide complementary resource
and information foundations for dynamic production organi-
zation. This paper examines how task and capability inter-
faces remain consistent as organizational scale changes and
how composite units continue to participate in higher-level
collaboration.

2.2 Holonic Manufacturing and Fractal Organization
Holonic manufacturing treats production entities as au-
tonomous, cooperative units that are simultaneously wholes
and parts of larger systems. PROSA, proposed by Van Brus-
sel et al., comprises product, order, and resource holons,
with staff holons providing specialized decision support [6].
Aggregation allows entities at different scales to retain dis-
tinct external identities, while specialization permits different
manufacturing functions to use different internal implementa-
tions. PROSA also explicitly separates organizational struc-
ture from control algorithms, using self-similarity to support
component integration and system reconfiguration. These
principles are directly relevant to the encapsulation of com-
posite units and reuse of mechanisms across levels considered
here.
Fractal manufacturing likewise emphasizes self-similar
functional organization. Ryu et al. describe basic units using
five functional modules—observer, analyzer, organizer, re-
solver, and reporter—that handle production tasks through
inter-agent collaboration and negotiation [7]. Repeating a
common functional structure across scales enables units to
adjust internal behavior and interactions as conditions change.
These studies establish a direct foundation for cross-scale
self-similarity and functional reuse. This paper further de-
scribes their operational connections through task contracts,
conditional composite capabilities, and commitment lifecy-
cles.
ADACOR addresses the relationship between global coor-
dination and local responsiveness. Its supervisor, task, prod-
uct, and operational holons organize production and adjust co-
ordination under normal and disturbed conditions to balance
overall planning with local adaptation [8]. The evolution-
ary architecture of Barbosa et al. extends self-organization
to behavioral and structural adaptation, allowing both unit
strategies and organizational relationships to change with pro-
duction conditions [9]. Drawing on these ideas, the present
work focuses on cross-level consistency in task decompo-
sition, composite capabilities, resource commitments, and
result feedback, and describes their operational integration
through a common kernel.
2.3 Hierarchical Task Planning and Capability-Driven
Allocation
Hierarchical task network (HTN) planning progressively ex-
pands compound tasks into primitive tasks using domain
methods. SHOP2 selects decomposition methods according
to the planning state and supports partially ordered tasks and
their dependencies [10]. HTN provides a representation from
production objectives to operation sequences, but task hier-
archies and organizational hierarchies are distinct modeling
constructs. A single planner may expand the entire task hi-
erarchy centrally, or delegate tasks to other units for further
planning. RAA adopts the latter organization: a compos-
ite assignee organizes its members under the same contract
while retaining responsibility for fulfilling the parent task.
The Contract Net Protocol establishes distributed dele-
gation through task announcements, bids, and awards; as-
signees can also organize further subcontracting [11]. Re-
cursive delegation is therefore not unique to RAA. Its appli-
cation to manufacturing depends on a shared interpretation
of task information, capability conditions, commitment up-
dates, and handovers. The capability and skill reference
model of Köcher et al. provides a common information
basis for describing resource functions and callable imple-
mentations [12]. Building on this distinction, the present
work separates available functions from the tasks a unit can
currently accept, incorporating resource calendars, quality
applicability, and handovers among members into composite
capability descriptions.
Task structures can also constrain learning-based decisions.
Mu et al. combine HTN with multi-agent reinforcement
learning, using task decomposition and subgoals to orga-
nize cooperative policy learning [13]. Such approaches show
how planning knowledge can connect to lower-level policies,
while cross-level reuse of an organizational kernel requires a
separate definition. RAA permits rules, HTN, mathematical
optimization, and learned policies as internal solution meth-
ods, connected through common task, resource, and result
contracts.
2.4 Production–Logistics Coordination and Dynamic
Scheduling
Dynamic scheduling revises existing arrangements when pro-
duction states change. Vieira et al. organize rescheduling
research in terms of strategies, triggering policies, and meth-
ods, and discuss the relationship between schedule revision
and stability [5]. Event-driven scheduling, periodic rolling
planning, and local schedule repair can be used within dif-
ferent organizational architectures. Dynamic responsiveness
therefore depends jointly on scheduling policies, triggering
mechanisms, and organizational structures. Architectural dif-
ferences chiefly concern the distribution of information and
decisions, which constraints are handled locally, and when
coordination must be expanded.
Integrated production and logistics scheduling requires
joint treatment of processing and transport resources.
Gehlhoff and Fay investigate agent-based decentralized in-
tegrated scheduling, coordinating production and transport
arrangements to address temporal and resource coupling in
dynamic manufacturing [14]. Their work demonstrates that
task allocation and logistics services must share feasibility
conditions. In a recursive organization, aggregating process-
ing capabilities without transport and handover conditions
can produce an external capability description that exceeds
what is executable. Capability aggregation must therefore
retain external resource dependencies relevant to higher-level
decisions.
Multi-agent reinforcement learning combines local poli-
cies with global guidance for large-scale scheduling. Jang
et al. investigate agent coordination under directed acyclic
graph (DAG) constraints, linking dependent subtasks through
leader–follower roles and reward mechanisms [15]. Their
semiconductor-factory scheduling study further combines
abstract global objectives with local equipment scheduling in
a leader–follower structure [16]. These approaches provide
algorithmic context for hybrid production decisions, while
scheduling performance and recursive reuse across levels
remain distinct properties. This paper models organizational
structure separately from local solvers, making coordina-
tion scope, capability encapsulation, and mechanism reuse

Industrial agent deployment must connect operational inter-
actions with heterogeneous automation equipment. Seitz et
al. develop an automation-platform-independent multi-agent
system, connecting production resources through common in-
formation representations and OPC UA communication, and
evaluate it in order-driven production and adaptable-factory
scenarios [17]. This work shows how common communi-
cation, resource self-description, and modular integration
support heterogeneous production systems. Cross-level task
responsibilities, composite capabilities, and lifecycles addi-
tionally require explicit organizational rules.
Large models offer new ways to connect unstructured pro-
duction requirements with manufacturing capabilities. Lim
et al. introduce large language models into multi-agent man-
ufacturing, enabling product and resource agents to interpret
user requirements and exchange task information [18]; their
subsequent work investigates model-assisted adaptive task
planning and coordination [19]. PaLM-E jointly uses visual,
state, and language information for embodied tasks, provid-
ing a methodological reference for incorporating multimodal
operational information into planning [20]. These approaches
primarily extend task interpretation, knowledge use, and can-
didate generation. Organizational recursion concerns how
task delegation and composite-unit identity remain consistent
as scale increases.
Here, large models generate candidates within the organi-
zational kernel, while explicit task models, process rules, and
resource constraints determine execution conditions. Model-
generated candidates undergo the same checks as those pro-
duced by rules or optimizers, and confirmed plans connect to
equipment through skill interfaces. Semantic reasoning can
therefore be configured according to task requirements, while
recursive organization rests on common contracts, closure
under composition, and lifecycle reuse across levels.
3 Recursive Organizational Model of RAA
Figure 2 presents the recursive organizational structure and
instantiation of the common kernel. Equipment-service units
form workshops, while workshops and shared services form
a factory. Each composite unit maintains the same exter-
nal interface and organizes collaboration among its internal
members. Downward objective delegation and upward capa-
bility and result feedback connect scales; peer interactions
coordinate task handovers and resources among members.
3.1 Leaf Units, Composite Units, and Organizational
Relationships
A production-service unit with a defined identity, capability,
responsibility boundary, and execution feedback is repre-
sented as agent Av. Equipment participates as a leaf agent
only when it supports a closed operational loop encompassing
objective acceptance, state assessment, execution supervision,
and result feedback. Sensors, actuators, and communication
gateways participate through resource interfaces. The bound-
ary of an autonomous unit is determined by an operational
scope within which tasks can be independently accepted and
their results verified. A leaf may correspond to a single ma-
chine or a group of devices required for a service, depending
on that scope. Members form a composite agent under a
coordinator:

and may serve different organizations; serving multiple tasks
does not duplicate it into independent resources.
3.2 Cross-Level Interface Consistency and the Organiza-
tional Kernel
Cross-level isomorphism is defined here as consistency in
interface semantics, organizational operations, and state tran-
sitions among units at different scales. It denotes a functional
and protocol-level correspondence, allowing physical struc-
tures and workflow topologies to differ. Every unit uses the
common interface schema

and versioning rules of the fields remain consistent. From a
higher-level perspective, delegating an integrated machining-
and-inspection service to a workshop follows the same kind
of delegation, confirmation, and feedback rules as delegating
a machining service to an equipment unit.

reception, capability queries, candidate decomposition, ne-

3.3 Cross-Level Isomorphism in Workflow Networks
Workflow networks provide an intuitive illustration of cross-
level isomorphism. Figure 3 shows the operational structure
of a level-1 agent: low-temperature treatment, impact test-
ing, buffers, loading/unloading points, robots, and conveyors
jointly provide a service through material input, output, han-
dover, and transport relationships. The level-1 agent is not
identical to any single machine shown. It assumes overall re-
sponsibility for objective acceptance, resource organization,
and result feedback within this collaborative scope. Exter-
nally, it provides a complete service capability; internally, it
coordinates processing, storage, and transport resources.
Figure 4 shows the operational structure of a level-2 agent.
Service nodes for impact testing to Chinese and US stan-
dards, automated tensile testing, sorting and measurement,
and drop-weight testing form a broader collaborative system
with incoming-sample buffers, residual-sample collection,
transfer points, and a shared automated guided vehicle (AGV)
network. A service organized within the level-1 scope can
be encapsulated as a processing node in the level-2 network.
The level-2 agent again organizes input, service processing,
buffering, handovers, and transport, and publishes an inte-
grated service externally. Encapsulation requires registering
the lower-level unit’s capabilities and handover conditions
with the corresponding service node. The different test nodes
are not assumed to have identical internal equipment config-
urations.
The isomorphism illustrated by these networks lies in
corresponding operational roles and organizational rules.
Equipment-level processing can be aggregated into a service-
level processing node; local buffering and handovers can be
aggregated into inter-unit input/output conditions; and inter-
nal transport can be exposed as a queryable logistics service.
Once the whole is encapsulated, the next level continues to
organize tasks through the same roles of processing, stor-
age, handover, and transport. The networks need not have
the same number of nodes or the same topology. Purple
paths and teal loading/unloading associations describe opera-
tional resource relationships rather than task delegation itself.
Common contracts and the organizational kernel connect
delegation to these resources and their service capabilities.
These role correspondences provide a structural basis for
recursive encapsulation. A lower-level unit publishes its ca-
pabilities as a whole; a higher-level unit delegates a service
through the common contract; and the assignee organizes exe-
cution through its internal workflow network before returning
results to the parent task. Interface consistency, capability en-
capsulation, and the task lifecycle jointly turn this structural
correspondence into an operational recursive process.

3.4 Recursive Expansion, Upward Aggregation, and Ter-
mination
Organizational recursion requires interface closure under
composition: members satisfying the composition conditions
form a whole through Eq. (1) that continues to use Eq. (2)
in higher-level composition. Task recursion requires decom-
posed subtasks to retain the same task contract, allowing
each assignee to choose between direct execution and further
decomposition. The first requirement establishes the compos-
ability of composite units; the second connects production
objectives to primitive operations.
Peer collaboration links these two directions. The pres-
ence of individual functions does not guarantee a deliverable
composite capability. Machining and inspection equipment
must also have compatible product scopes, material han-
dovers, and transport conditions before jointly committing
to a machining-and-inspection service. Members first estab-
lish executable internal arrangements through collaboration.
The coordinator then abstracts these arrangements into an
integrated service and reports its applicability, timing, and
external resource dependencies. Cross-level isomorphism
allows higher levels to organize this service in turn.
Recursion requires finite termination conditions. Orga-
nizational expansion can visit only members of the finite
organizational tree. Within a unit, task methods must strictly
decrease the rank of remaining compound tasks along every
expansion branch until an approved skill with valid precondi-
tions and permissions is matched. If no applicable method
or leaf capability can be matched, the process reports infea-
sibility or insufficient conditions. Limits on decomposition
depth, query time, and negotiation rounds bound computa-
tion; reaching a limit returns an incomplete status and its
reason.
Equipment, workstations, lines, workshops, and factories
are not mandatory fixed levels. Tightly coupled equipment
may first form an operational module, with several modules
forming a workshop, or a workshop may directly organize
equipment with complete service capabilities. Levels depend
on service responsibilities, resource coupling, and encapsula-
ble capabilities. An additional composition level retains the
existing interface and kernel while configuring the required
domain knowledge, membership, and verification conditions.
3.5 Structural Conditions for Recursive Organization
Hierarchical organization assigns decision responsibilities
at different scales; hybrid organization combines vertical
coordination with local decisions; recursive organization ad-
ditionally specifies interface closure of composite units and
reuse of organizational processes across levels. RAA adopts
a hybrid organization with recursive properties. Levels inter-
act through common contracts and run the common kernel
within their responsibility boundaries. Hierarchical depth can
vary with operational scale, resource coupling, encapsulable
capabilities, and fulfillment responsibilities.

4 Capability-Driven Recursive Collaboration
Figure 5 presents the organizational lifecycle shared across
scales: task acceptance, capability matching and decomposi-
tion, cross-level and peer negotiation, joint checks and com-

updates. Equipment-service units, workshops, and factories
handle different domain objects while retaining the same
stage semantics and feedback relationships. The following
subsections specify this lifecycle through task contracts, com-
posite capabilities, resource constraints, and recursive calls.

4.2 Top-Down Capability-Driven Task Decomposition
After accepting a task, a unit checks its objects, objectives,
and constraints, then queries member capabilities and ex-
isting commitments. Domain methods provide candidate
decompositions, while current capabilities determine which
methods and participants remain applicable. Planning pro-
duces a DAG Gτ = (Vτ, Eτ) whose edges describe process
precedence or material handovers. Subtasks retain Eq. (4).
A composite assignee invokes the same kernel for further
expansion, while a leaf selects approved skills, completing
the mapping from task objectives to equipment execution.
Capability feedback can change the decomposition itself
rather than only the assignee. Suppose machining and final
inspection were originally assigned to one workshop. If its
inspection resource becomes unavailable, the higher-level
unit can reformulate the objective as machining, inter-area
transport, and independent inspection instead of simply sub-
stituting a machine within the original task structure. This
change is valid only if alternative inspection satisfies the orig-
inal quality requirements, the workpiece can be transported,
and transport resources are available. Domain methods and
currently composable capabilities jointly determine task gran-
ularity, while mandatory quality steps and acceptance require-
ments remain in force.
Decomposition must also preserve applicable constraints.
An overall due date can be allocated to local time windows
where process relationships permit; quality requirements map
to step-specific acceptance conditions; and authority can only
be refined within higher-level authorization. The coordinator
continues to maintain constraints that cannot be delegated
independently to a single member, such as a shared resource
limit across workshops. Each level records who checks a con-
straint, when it is confirmed, and which higher-level commit-
ments would be affected by its violation, preventing global
requirements from being lost during refinement.

4.4 Bottom-Up Capability and Result Aggregation
Capability aggregation translates internal collaboration into
a service description usable by higher levels. Member capa-
bilities, interface compatibility, resource conditions, and task
methods jointly determine the objectives a composite unit
can accept. The aggregate description retains at least service
type, applicable products, quality scope, service windows, ex-
pected completion conditions, and external shared-resource
dependencies. A composite unit may hide specific internal
equipment choices, but it must expose shared constraints af-
fecting other units’ feasibility. Otherwise, higher levels may
mistake conflicting local commitments for simultaneously
executable arrangements.
Aggregation must retain the conditional nature of capabil-
ity. Two machining units may each provide processing ca-
pacity, yet their joint service rate may be limited by a shared
transport device. Likewise, idle machining and inspection
equipment do not guarantee a feasible handover window
before a given due date. Overall capacity and completion
times depend on serial and parallel relationships, waiting, and
shared-resource occupancy. When necessary, a composite
unit returns a conditional quotation or feasible time window
for a specific objective rather than a misleading scalar capac-
ity.
Results return along task dependencies. Members submit
evidence associated with objects, task versions, and accep-
tance conditions. The coordinator checks whether subtask
results and handover records jointly satisfy the parent task.

5.1 A Multi-Workshop Machining–Inspection–Logistics
Network
An abstract production network comprising flexible work-
shops, shared transport units, and material buffers illustrates
cross-level isomorphism and response to change. Orders con-
tain batches with machining, inspection, and delivery require-
ments. Products may follow different process routes, and
equivalent operations may run on multiple capable resources.
Machining and inspection equipment have availability win-
dows and processing rates. Transport resources are limited
by load capacity, service duration, and concurrent occupancy.
Tasks must jointly satisfy precedence, quality applicability,
material handovers, resource exclusion, and due dates.
The scenario captures constraints common to multi-
operation production, heterogeneous resources, and logistics
across areas. Product routes, resource capabilities, and orga-
nizational configurations describe particular production in-
stances. Logistics is represented through conditional service
windows and transport times; path conflicts can be included
as additional constraints. The network connects the recursive
organizational model with concrete production tasks.
Equipment-service agents form leaf units. Machining, in-
spection, and internal transport units form workshop compos-
ite agents, while workshops and inter-area transport services
are organized by a factory agent. Additional workstation or
operational-module levels can be incorporated through the
same composition operation. Internal and inter-area transport
may have different managers or share one provider, provided
that resource identities and allocation responsibilities remain
unique and joint calendars prevent double counting.

5.3 Changing Production Conditions and Organizational
Adjustment
The first type of change is a task update. New orders, revised
quantities, and changed due dates alter root or batch con-
tracts. If a workshop can absorb the change within its exist-
ing commitments, it updates only internal subtasks. Changes
affecting other batches, inter-area transport, or factory de-
livery require reassignment by the appropriate higher-level
unit. Frequent updates require a balance between continual
revision and execution stability.
The second type is a reduction in processing rate. When
processing time increases, the equipment-service unit first
revises its expected completion conditions. A workshop may
reassign work to an internal alternative while maintaining
external commitments. If it can no longer meet the batch
due date, it reports the capability change to the factory. The
factory checks whether other workshops and inter-area trans-
port provide a feasible alternative. Equipment, workshops,
and the factory invoke the same organizational operations
within their respective scopes, with the validity of external
commitments determining how far coordination extends.
The third type is a change in resource conditions. After
an inspection device is withdrawn, an internal machining-
and-inspection task may be replaced, where feasible, by local
machining, inter-area transport, and external inspection. Ad-
ditional equipment or transport resources can expand the
available compositions. Such changes may alter both the task
structure and the resulting composite capability.
The fourth type is organizational expansion. An existing
equipment network may be composed into a new workshop,
or an intermediate coordination module may be added among
workshops. The resulting composite unit retains the common
interface and kernel. Expansion still requires capability regis-
tration, membership configuration, and verification; recursive
composition does not remove these domain and integration
responsibilities.

6.2 Multimodal-Assisted Planning and Local Execution
Figure 7 presents a multimodal-assisted implementation of re-
cursive collaboration. Task descriptions, process knowledge,
operational images, and resource states are first associated
with a common task context. A multimodal model com-
bines with rules or planning methods to generate candidate
decompositions. Candidates become structured tasks with ob-
jectives, constraints, participants, and dependencies, then pro-
ceed through capability matching, resource checks, and nego-
tiated confirmation. Semantic processing connects unstruc-
tured inputs to the organizational kernel, and specific models
can be replaced according to operational needs [18, 19].
Rules, HTN, or optimizers can directly handle structured
orders and known processes. Multimodal models are use-
ful where text, images, or heterogeneous knowledge require
interpretation. Their scope is configured independently of
organizational recursion. All candidates pass through com-
mon task objects and constraint checks before commitment
management, so different generation methods do not change
task responsibility or acceptance rules. Cross-level reuse
resides in the organizational kernel, while semantic models
provide revisable candidates.
Local computation can use dedicated edge computers or
existing industrial computing platforms. Domain plugins
hold methods and models; industrial gateways map PLC,
robot, instrument, and logistics interfaces; and existing con-
trollers execute approved skills. Before execution, the system
checks task versions, resource permissions, and physical pre-
conditions. Operational coordination, equipment control, and
independent safety functions operate at their respective time
scales. Research on learning-based robotic control likewise
emphasizes coordination between learning mechanisms, con-
straints, and safety-control layers [21]. The implementation
follows this separation of responsibilities: higher-level se-
mantic reasoning does not replace local control or safety
functions.
6.3 Version Management and Execution Consistency
Tasks, capabilities, domain methods, and organizational con-
figurations retain version links. Resource changes can update
current availability, while modifications to skills or capability
boundaries require verification before publication. Com-
posite capability versions record the member and method
versions on which they depend, allowing higher levels to
determine whether existing commitments remain valid. State
updates that do not affect current tasks need not immediately
trigger global revision. Changes to commitment conditions
are handled using the scope rules in Section 4.
After communication timeouts or node recovery, work-
piece states, execution receipts, task versions, and resource
occupancy are reconciled before continuation or cancellation.
Requests carry unique invocation identifiers so that message
retries do not trigger duplicate physical operations. If a co-
ordinator fails, the scope in which members can continue
is determined by valid commitments and locally verifiable
conditions. New cross-unit commitments require restored
coordination or an explicit takeover satisfying the specified
takeover and consistency conditions.

7 Discussion
7.1 Organizational Reuse and Engineering Adaptation
Recursive design extracts objective acceptance, task delega-
tion, commitment confirmation, and result aggregation from
scale-specific implementations into a common organizational
process. When a composition level is added, the compos-
ite unit retains existing contracts and represents its internal
structure through membership and domain configurations.
This reuse provides a basis for reducing cross-level interface
adaptation. Its practical value depends on the operational
scope covered by the kernel and the modularity of existing
systems.
Domain processes, physical interfaces, and operational
constraints still require modeling and verification.
New
products may require additional decomposition methods,
new equipment needs state and skill mappings, and learning
policies may require retraining after resource distributions
change. Interface adaptation, domain modeling, equipment
integration, and algorithm adjustment therefore remain dis-
tinct engineering responsibilities. Additional levels also in-
troduce query, negotiation, and confirmation overhead.
7.2 Dynamic Responsiveness and Schedule Quality
When external commitments remain valid, local reconfigura-
tion can preserve other units’ arrangements and limit change
propagation. Restricted coordination may, however, miss
better solutions requiring resource exchanges across units.
Capability aggregation reduces details exposed to higher-
level solvers but can introduce abstraction error. Faster re-
sponse therefore does not by itself establish lower tardiness,
higher throughput, better resource utilization, or more stable
schedules.
Coordination within the affected scope is most applicable
when dependency information is sufficient, resource responsi-
13


bilities are explicit, and disturbances can be absorbed locally.
Strong coupling through shared resources or simultaneous in-
validation of multiple delivery commitments requires broader
coordination at a higher level. For small problems with cen-
tralized information, centralized solution methods may incur
less runtime overhead. Frozen windows, adjustment thresh-
olds, and decision deadlines can suppress repeated reschedul-
ing; their effects are distinct from the recursive structure
itself.
7.3 Applicability and Limitations
The model uses a finite rooted tree for primary contain-
ment relationships and a unique responsible manager for
resources shared across organizations. This representation
simplifies task responsibility and coordination-scope determi-
nation. Resource coalitions belonging simultaneously to sev-
eral organizations require extensions to containment, author-
ity inheritance, and commitment management. Capability
composition also depends on domain methods and handover
conditions. Unknown processes, measurement errors, and
unmodeled coupling can affect feasibility judgments.
PROSA, fractal manufacturing, and ADACOR provide
direct foundations for the framework. RAA is specified here
through the connection among common contracts, condi-
tional composite capabilities, and a common organizational
kernel. The present contribution comprises an organizational
model, operational mechanisms, production-task mappings,
and an implementation architecture. These establish how re-
cursive collaboration is organized without establishing quan-
titative advantages in development effort or dynamic schedul-
ing performance.
8 Conclusion
This paper develops a recursive organizational model and
operational mechanisms for collective RAA collaboration
across production scales in smart factories. Common con-
tracts expand production objectives into executable tasks
from the top down. Peer collaboration establishes conditional
capabilities, and composite units participate in higher-level
organization as agents of the same kind. Consistent cross-
level interfaces and lifecycles connect task decomposition,
resource commitments, execution supervision, and result ag-
gregation, allowing equipment-service units, workshops, and
factories to reuse a common organizational kernel.
The framework specifies conditions for composite capa-
bilities, finite expansion, shared-resource allocation, and co-
ordination within the scope affected by change. A multi-
workshop machining–inspection–logistics network illustrates
the corresponding production-task mapping. Multimodal-
assisted planning, domain solvers, and local execution inter-
faces provide configurable implementation support. Together,
these elements connect recursive composition with explicit
operational responsibilities and execution conditions in man-
ufacturing organizations.
