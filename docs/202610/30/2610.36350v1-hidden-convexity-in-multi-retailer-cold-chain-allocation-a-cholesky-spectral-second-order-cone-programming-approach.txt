# Hidden Convexity in Multi-Retailer Cold-Chain Allocation: A Cholesky-Spectral Second-Order Cone Programming Approach

Abstract 

Several dairy retailers share a refrigerated cold-chain network. Each must allocate products across heat-sensitivity subgroups and planning periods, maximising a quadratic-fractional efficiency in the spirit of data envelopment analysis (DEA, Charnes, Cooper and Rhodes, 1978); the fractional form captures spoilage risk per unit of logistics cost and is therefore strictly more informative than a linear additive efficiency, which would conflate scale with intensity. This aligns with the DEA convention of separating technical from scale efficiency (Banker, Charnes and Cooper, 1984): a linear score cannot distinguish a large-volume retailer allocating uniformly from a small-volume retailer concentrated on high-risk items, whereas the ratio isolates per-unit risk intensity, which is the actionable dimension for cold-chain investment (Cook and Seiford, 2009). The individual problem is a non-convex fractional quadratically-constrained quadratic program (QCQP); the joint problem is a generalised Nash equilibrium problem (GNEP) with a shared risk-budget constraint, a class that is generally NP-hard. The Arrhenius spoilage law with coefficient Q10 (van Boekel, 2022) forces the thermal-risk covariance to inherit a rank-one-plus-diagonal structure. This physical structure unlocks hidden convexity, which our algorithm HSDI (Hidden SOCP Dinkelbach Iteration) exploits via a joint Cholesky-spectral transform. Our contributions are fourfold. We establish a simultaneous diagonalisation of (Σ i, H i) without commutativity. We prove an explicit sufficient condition for the second-order cone programming (SOCP) relaxation to be exact, with a closed-form suboptimality bound when it is not. We provide a complete convergence analysis of HSDI: the outer Dinkelbach loop converges globally and Q-superlinearly when the relaxation is exact and at least R-linearly under inexact relaxation, while the coupled variational-equilibrium (VE) iteration that enforces the shared risk budget converges R-linearly under strong monotonicity, yielding an overall O(nd 3.5 log 3(1 /ε )) complexity; we

# 1 Introduction 

This paper is devoted to applications of variational analysis to practical modelling, the area where fundamental contributions of Professor Aussel are difficult to overstate. Cold supply chains for fluid milk are among the highest fixed-cost infrastructures in food retail, with global cold-storage capacity exceeding 719 million m 3 across 51 countries in the most recent survey of GCCA (2020), and the GCCA member network alone reaching ∼ 209 million m 3 by 2024 (GCCA, 2024). In emerging markets such as Vietnam, where logistics performance has been shown to directly affect trade and distribution outcomes (Vu and Doan, 2024), cold-chain infrastructure constraints are particularly binding for domestic food retailers. Three structural features make the allocation problem non-trivial. First, every product in the network shares a single temperature profile, so any thermal shock (power outage, customs delay, insulation failure) becomes a strongly positive externality across retailers. Second, spoilage kinetics depend non-linearly on temperature through an Arrhenius coefficient Q10 ∈ [2 , 3] (van Boekel, 2022), amplifying small temperature deviations into large value-at-risk. Third, when several retailers liquidate inventory after a shock, market impact (Almgren and Chriss, 2001; Iancu and Trichakis, 2014) makes each agent’s problem a component of an equilibrium with strategic interactions. 

From Arrhenius physics to hidden convexity. The Arrhenius temperature-dependence law states that spoilage rates multiply by Q10 ,k for every 10 ◦C of warming, with the coefficient Q10 ,k 

depending only on the heat-sensitivity subgroup k. Linearising this exponential law around the nominal cold-chain temperature with a thermal noise of standard deviation σ produces subgroup loadings bk ∝ ln Q10 ,k · σ that share the same scalar shock σ. The thermal-risk covariance matrix of any retailer therefore inherits a rank-one-plus-diagonal structure Σ i = bib⊤ 

> i

+ Di,with the rank-one factor encoding the shared thermal shock and Di capturing subgroup-specific idiosyncratic risk. We exploit precisely this algebraic structure to expose hidden convexity :through a joint Cholesky-spectral transform, the non-convex fractional QCQP admits a tractable SOCP reformulation that is exact under an explicit, verifiable condition—recovering the global optimum in polynomial time on that regime—and carries a closed-form optimality-gap certificate otherwise. This Arrhenius-driven structural identification is the central methodological insight 2of the paper. These features motivate a quadratic-fractional efficiency function with a physically anchored risk structure, embedded in the variational equilibrium (VE) framework of Rosen (1965). We address two questions. (M) How can we quantify efficiency that captures both economic value and physical risk in the DEA language of Charnes, Cooper and Rhodes (1978), while remaining compatible with shared constraints? (C) The proposed efficiency is a non-convex fractional QCQP. Does the one-factor risk structure allow its reduction to a family of convex problems solvable in polynomial time? 

Positioning in the literature. We situate the paper at the intersection of four streams: (1) hidden convexity in non-convex optimisation (Ben-Tal and Nemirovski, 2001; Xia, Wang and Sheu, 2015; Beck and Pan, 2017; Ramachandran et al., 2024; Pataki, 2013); (2) generalised Nash equilibrium and variational inequalities (Facchinei and Pang, 2003; Facchinei and Kanzow, 2010; Kulkarni and Shanbhag, 2012; Nagurney et al., 2017); (3) multi-portfolio optimisation with market impact (Almgren and Chriss, 2001; Iancu and Trichakis, 2014); and (4) spoilage kinetics and willingness-to-pay for freshness (Tsiros and Heilman, 2005; van Boekel, 2022; Endara et al., 2023). The closest reference is Akkaya and Pınar (2025), who study hidden conic quadratic represen-tations of generalised Nash equilibrium problems motivated by multi-investor Markowitz models. In particular, their Section 3 analyses a ratio-of-quadratics objective with a Frobenius-ball con-straint, using Dinkelbach’s algorithm and a second-order cone relaxation under the assumption that the numerator and denominator matrices commute (so that a single orthogonal rotation di-agonalises both). Our work extends the same algorithmic template - Dinkelbach plus SOC lifting - to vector decision variables and, more importantly, removes the commuting-pair assumption by replacing the joint orthogonal rotation with a Cholesky-spectral transform (Theorem 4.2). In addition, Theorem 4.4(b) gives an explicit closed-form suboptimality bound when the relaxation is not exact, which to our knowledge does not appear in Akkaya and Pınar (2025). 

Contributions. Our contributions are fourfold. (i) Modelling. We propose a quadratic-fractional efficiency function with risk matrix Σ i derived from Arrhenius physics, and we embed it in a GNEP with a shared budget under the VE solution concept. (ii) Structural theory. We establish in Theorem 4.2 a simultaneous diagonalisation of the pair (Σ i, H i) via Cholesky-spectral steps without commutativity . We show in Theorem 4.4 a sufficient exactness condition and a closed-form error bound for the SOCP relaxation. (iii) Convergence theory. In Section 5 we prove that the outer Dinkelbach iteration of HSDI converges globally, at a Q-superlinear rate when the relaxation is exact (Theorem 5.2) and at least R-linearly otherwise, and that the coupled VE iteration enforcing the shared budget converges R-linearly under strong monotonicity (Theorem 5.4); we combine these into an explicit polynomial complexity bound and establish Lipschitz stability of the primal–dual pair via the second-order variational-analysis toolbox of Mordukhovich (2024). (iv) Experiments. We calibrate the model on two independent regimes and run six numerical experiments that verify existence of solutions, spectral structure, calibration robustness, polynomial-time scaling, and—newly—the theoretical convergence rates and shadow-price stability, across problem sizes from n · d = 45 to 180. 3Organisation. Section 2 fixes notation and recalls technical tools. Section 3 builds the model and the GNEP. Section 4 contains the Cholesky-spectral transform, the SOCP exactness theorem, and Algorithm 1. Section 5 develops the convergence analysis of HSDI and of the coupled VE iteration. Section 6 discusses extensions. Section 7 reports the six numerical experiments. Section 8 concludes. Appendices A and B contain detailed proofs and calibration tables.

Remark 4.3 (Comparison with the literature) . Akkaya and Pınar (2025) use a single orthogonal rotation that requires [Σ i, H i] = 0. The measured commutator magnitudes provide direct empirical evidence that the commuting-pair assumption fails for both the Vietnamese and the United States calibrations. The rank-one-plus-diagonal structure of Σ i and the additive structure of Hi do not, in general, commute once calibrated against real-world dairy data. Remark 4.7 (Stability of the primal-dual pair) . The primal-dual solution ( x⋆i , μ ) produced by Algorithm 1 inherits stability guarantees from the second-order variational-analysis theory of parameterised conic equilibria. Under a metric-subregularity constraint qualification, the graphical-derivative calculus of Mordukhovich et al. (2015a) yields the isolated-calmness property of the solution map to the generalised equation associated with (SOCP( ρ)), which specialises to SOCPs in Mordukhovich et al. (2015b) and is further refined for Lorentz-cone constraints in Nguyen (2020). Concretely, small perturbations of the risk budget R, the loadings bk and the co-allocation cost matrix Hi translate into Lipschitz-bounded perturbations of ( x⋆i , μ ): exactly the regularity that the operational reading of μ as a shadow price in Section 8 tacitly presumes.

For the multi-agent problem with n independent agents ( β = 0 ), the total cost is O(nd 3.5 log 2(1 /ε )) , polynomial in n and d.

# 8 Conclusion 

We establish a quadratic-fractional efficiency function for cold-chain allocation, embed it in a GNEP with a shared risk-budget constraint, and adopt the VE as the solution concept. Theorem 4.2 shows that the one-factor structure - quantitatively anchored to the Arrhenius coefficient Q10 - enables a simultaneous diagonalisation of (Σ i, H i) without commutativity. Theorem 4.4 reduces the Dinkelbach inner problem to an SOCP with an explicit sufficient exactness condition and a closed-form error bound. Section 5 then shows that HSDI converges globally— Q-superlinearly on the exact region and R-linearly otherwise for the Dinkelbach loop, and R-linearly for the coupled VE iteration—in polynomial time, and that its primal–dual output is Lipschitz-stable. Together these give a deterministic, convergence-certified allocation with a shadow price that, on the exact region, is provably globally optimal—properties that no multi-start non-linear baseline can offer, however many restarts it uses. The economic interpretation is concentrated in the Lagrange multiplier μ on the shared budget. A larger μ in less-developed markets flags a higher shadow price per unit of additional cold-chain capacity, identifying μ as a quantitative indicator for infrastructure investment. 

Managerial and policy implications. Building on this economic reading, the calibrated comparison delivers actionable guidance for operators and policymakers. In the Vietnam calibration the multiplier μVN is larger than its United States counterpart μUS , signalling a higher marginal value of cold-chain capacity expansion in the emerging-market setting. This finding is consistent with broader evidence that logistics performance gaps in Vietnam carry measurable economic costs (Vu and Doan, 2024), and points to cold-chain capacity as a high-return investment target in the Vietnamese retail food sector. Operators and policymakers active 20 in markets with high μ should prioritise capacity expansion. The SOCP dual solution converts 

μ into a quantitative threshold above which additional investment becomes self-financing under the calibrated risk budget. Because HSDI is deterministic, convergence-certified and—on the exact region—globally optimal, and because μ is Lipschitz-stable in the budget (Theorem 5.6), the shadow price is a reliable decision input for infrastructure planning rather than an artefact of a randomly restarted local solver. Extending the calibration with IoT lane-level data and lifting the model to a multi-period horizon, both discussed below as limitations, would allow μ

to be tracked and updated dynamically as the network evolves. 

Methodological implication. The same pattern applies more broadly: a physical principle yields an algebraic structure, which in turn enables an optimisation reformulation. In our case, the Arrhenius law produces a rank-one-plus-diagonal covariance, which the Cholesky-spectral transform exploits without commutativity. Analogous patterns include power-grid management with Kirchhoff structure, heat-routing problems with Laplacian-spectrum structure, and queueing optimisation with Toeplitz-plus-diagonal structure. 

Limitations. (i) σVN uses a proxy in the absence of IoT lane-level data; (ii) the one-factor model is an approximation; (iii) the single-period framework abstracts away multi-period dynamics; (iv) experiments are run at β = 0 to isolate the technical kernel; (v) the scaling experiment of Section 7.5 covers n · d ∈ [45 , 180] with ninst = 3 LHS perturbations per cell, which is sufficient to separate the HSDI and SLSQP scaling exponents at the 5% level (nearly disjoint 95% intervals), though the predicted wall-time cross-over has not been observed in the tested range; a larger sweep would sharpen both the exponent estimate and the cross-over prediction. (vi) The number of heat-sensitivity subgroups K is fixed exogenously at the values reported in Tables 5 and 6; no sensitivity analysis over K has been conducted. Because K determines the dimension d of the allocation vector and the sparsity pattern of Σ i, the rank-one-plus-diagonal structure exploited by HSDI may degrade as K grows or as subgroups are merged at coarser aggregation levels. A systematic sweep over K would clarify how the exactness condition of Theorem 4.4 tightens or weakens with the granularity of the product taxonomy. (vii) The empirical evaluation rests on a small number of instances: two market calibrations (Vietnam and the United States) and a scaling grid with at most three Latin-hypercube perturbations per cell. While this is adequate to verify correctness and to estimate scaling slopes, it falls short of the sample sizes required to draw statistically robust conclusions about generalisation across supply-chain configurations, retailer asymmetries, or geographic markets beyond those considered. Out-of-sample performance on held-out calibrations remains uncharacterised. (viii) All parameter inputs - retail prices, spoilage coefficients, thermal-noise standard deviations, and the shared risk budget R - are static 

quantities drawn from annual reports, government surveys, and industry benchmarks compiled at a single point in time. The model therefore cannot capture intra-day or seasonal price variation, demand shocks, or evolving cold-chain conditions that practitioners monitor through real-time telemetry. In particular, the Arrhenius coefficient Q10 and the thermal noise σ are treated as constants, whereas in live operations both fluctuate continuously with ambient temperature, equipment age, and lane-level traffic. The absence of real-time, high-frequency inputs means that the calibrated shadow price μ reflects a long-run average rather than an actionable real-time 21 signal. (ix) The precise dimensional threshold at which the outer Dinkelbach loop transitions from the Q-superlinear regime of Theorem 5.2(b) to the merely R-linear regime of Theorem 5.2(c) has not been empirically quantified. This transition is governed by the emergence of a non-empty index set J−(ρ) = {j : λi,j < ρ } along the iterates, whose cardinality is expected to grow with the decision dimension d = KT and hence with the subgroup count K. Because no systematic sweep in K has been conducted (see (vi)), the rate diagnostics of Section 7.6 verify the predicted regimes only at the calibrated values of K and cannot locate the operational boundary beyond which the superlinear guarantee ceases to hold. A dedicated K-sweep, paired with per-iterate monitoring of |J −(ρk)|, would map this boundary and clarify how sensitive the observed rate is to the granularity of the product taxonomy. (x) The convergence guarantees of Sections 5.1 and 5.2 are established for a single planning period. Extending HSDI to the rolling-horizon or IoT-driven multi-period setting sketched in the future-research directions below would naturally warm-start each period’s outer loops from the terminal iterate of the preceding period, and this introduces a cumulative-convergence risk that the present single-period analysis does not cover. Numerical residual carried over from period t may push the initial iterate at period t + 1 outside the local basin of Q-superlinear attraction identified in Theorem 5.2(b), and repeated compounding across many periods may erode the asymptotic R-linear rate of Theorem 5.4, producing progressive solution drift and, in the worst case, loss of asymptotic stability of the equilibrium trajectory. Sufficient conditions on the inter-period residual under which global convergence to the true sequence of variational equilibria is preserved, together with an empirical assessment of drift on synthetic multi-period instances, lie outside the scope of the present paper. Each item above is a natural direction for follow-up work. 

Future research directions. Several extensions follow directly from the limitations above. First, replacing the static parameter inputs with a real-time data pipeline - drawing on IoT sensors, electronic shelf labels, and lane-level telematics - would transform HSDI from a planning-horizon tool into an operational decision engine. This requires integrating the SOCP solver with a rolling-horizon or model-predictive control framework so that the shadow price μ is recomputed as new temperature readings arrive. Second, a rigorous sensitivity analysis over the subgroup count K would map out the boundary of applicability of the rank-one-plus-diagonal assumption and guide practitioners on how finely to partition the product taxonomy before the structural guarantee breaks down. Third, expanding the empirical evaluation to a larger and more diverse set of markets - including cold chains in Southeast Asian emerging economies beyond Vietnam - would provide the out-of-sample evidence needed to assess the transferability of the calibrated parameters and the tightness of the SOCP relaxation across heterogeneous network topologies. Finally, lifting the model to a multi-period or stochastic setting, where Q10 

and σ are themselves random processes, would bring the theoretical framework closer to the operational reality of cold-chain management under climate variability. In parallel, a formal sensitivity analysis of the primal-dual pair ( x⋆, μ ) under joint perturbation of R, bk and Hi

can be carried out through the second-order variational-analysis toolbox for parameterised conic programs developed in Mordukhovich et al. (2015a,b); Hang et al. (2019); Benko et al. (2019); Nguyen (2020). Delivering quantitative Lipschitz moduli for the shadow price μ would 22 rigorously certify the operational-reliability claims made in the present paper and connect the HSDI framework to the mainstream stability theory of nonpolyhedral conic programs.
