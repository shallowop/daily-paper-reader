# Abstract 

Designing optimal auctions for allocating public infrastructure—such as airport slots and energy resources—is challenging due to interdependent resources and complex side-constraints. While the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) auction guar-antees maximum welfare, it cannot maximize revenue or accommodate the multi-objective trade-offs inherent in public infrastructure systems. We propose a dif-ferentiable, constraint-aware framework for combinatorial auction design that enables end-to-end learning of fractionally feasible, approximately strategy-proof mechanisms. Our primary contribution is a combinatorial constraint enforcement technique that ensures fractional feasibility by construction, bypassing the need for penalty-based approximations. We introduce CAGraph , a novel graph-based architecture designed to capture dependencies between items and bidders, alongside CANet 

and CAFormer as deep learning baselines, for combinatorial settings. We also provide a Matching Pursuit-inspired decomposition that maps fractional allocations to feasible discrete allocations and empirically evaluates the resulting discretization gap. In synthetic benchmarks and an airport slot-divestiture case study, respectively, the learned mechanisms achieve up to 73.5% and 180% higher expected revenue than VCG, under small empirical regret. Finally, we visualize the Pareto frontiers for revenue, welfare, and fairness, providing regulators with a decision-support tool to design infrastructure auctions that balance profitability, efficiency and equity. 

# 1 Introduction 

Designing optimal auctions has significant real-world implications for the allocation of public infras-tructure—such as airport slots, energy markets, and transportation networks—as well as digital assets like online advertising. While mechanism design provides a theoretical framework for achieving de-sirable outcomes like efficiency and revenue maximization, analytically deriving optimal mechanisms is often intractable in high-dimensional strategic environments. Traditional approaches, such as the

Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism, offer theoretical guarantees for welfare maximization but are unable to maximize revenue or handle complex side-constraints. Recent advances in differentiable economics offer a promising data-driven path forward, utilizing deep neural networks to flexibly approximate optimal auction rules. However, extending these methods to combinatorial auctions—where bidders value bundles of items—remains a formidable challenge due to the complex, non-convex nature of allocation constraints. This paper introduces a differentiable, constraint-aware framework for combinatorial mechanism design that enables the end-to-end learning of fractionally feasible, approximately strategy-proof, and multi-objective mechanisms that balance revenue, efficiency, and fairness. 

1.1 Background 1.1.1 The Allocative Challenge 

Combinatorial auctions play an important role in allocating scarce public resources of high economic value, such as spectrum licenses and airport slots. However, designing effective mechanisms for these domains presents two fundamental challenges. First, bidders often exhibit complex valuation structures characterized by strong complementarities and substitutabilities—for instance, an airline values a landing slot only if paired with a corresponding takeoff slot. This necessitates the use of bundle-based valuations, where the package value differs from the sum of its parts. Second, the allocation of public goods is inherently multi-objective. Governments often prioritize allocative efficiency (welfare) to ensure that resources are assigned to their most productive users. However, this focus can lead to significant shortcomings in raising public funds or compensating divesting parties. Furthermore, traditional auction mechanisms often fail to uphold fairness, which is essential to protect disadvantaged agents and prevent monopolistic dominance. A critical gap exists in the literature: traditional mechanisms prioritize single objectives in isolation—VCG for welfare [Vickrey, 1961, Clarke, 1971, Groves, 1973], Myerson for revenue in single-item settings [Myerson, 1981], or Random Serial Dictatorship (RSD) for fairness [Abdulkadiroglu and Sönmez, 1998, Bogomolnaia and Moulin, 2001]. Extending these frameworks to multi-objective settings is analytically intractable and computationally prohibitive, as even the single-objective winner determination problem is NP-hard [Sandholm, 2003]. 

1.1.2 Application to Airport Slot Allocation 

We focus on airport slot allocation, a domain characterized by extreme scarcity and high economic value, with slot portfolios often valued in the millions of dollars. The current administrative regime, governed by the International Air Transport Association (IATA) guidelines and “Grandfather Rights,” perpetuates inefficiencies by allowing incumbents to hold slots in perpetuity, often leading to hoarding and suboptimal utilization. While theoretical proposals such as congestion pricing [Carlin and Park, 1970, Daniel, 1995, Vaze and Barnhart, 2012] and primary auctions [Rassenti et al., 1982, Ball et al., 2020] have been suggested, they have failed to gain widespread adoption due to their complexity and the lack of fairness guarantees [Jacquillat and Vaze, 2018, Zografos and Jiang, 2019], which are essential for political and regulatory acceptability. 

1.1.3 Methodological Gap 

A significant methodological gap persists in addressing these multi-faceted challenges. Traditional mechanism design [e.g., Vickrey, 1961, Myerson, 1981] excels at optimizing single objectives but struggles to incorporate complex side-constraints like fairness. Conversely, computational approaches for combinatorial auctions must explicitly handle winner-determination and feasibility constraints, which are known to be computationally challenging in high-dimensional bundle spaces [Rothkopf et al., 1998, Sandholm, 2002, de Vries and Vohra, 2003]. While deep learning, specifically Differentiable Economics [Duetting et al., 2019], offers a promising avenue due to its adaptability to data, standard neural network architectures do not automatically encode the combinatorial structures. This motivates architectures with problem-specific inductive biases, i.e., the architectural assumptions that help the model respect the structures of the problem that are necessary to satisfy the constraints [Battaglia et al., 2018, Cappart et al., 2023a]. This paper addresses these challenges by examining and quantifying the trade-offs between three key objectives—Revenue, Welfare, and Fairness. Our work contributes to multi-objective auction 2design, with real-world case studies focusing on airport resource allocation as an exemplar application domain, by leveraging deep neural networks for constrained resource allocation. 

1.2 Contributions 

1. Data-driven Multi-Objective Optimization. We provide a data-driven framework to discover high-performing, approximately strategy-proof mechanisms for combinatorial auctions where analytical results are unavailable. This framework models and quantifies the trade-offs between Revenue, Welfare, and Fairness, enabling policymakers to quantify the Price of Fairness and calibrate trade-offs between protecting disadvantaged agents and maintaining efficiency. 2. Constraint-Aware Neural Architectures. We introduce differentiable constraint enforce-ment techniques for the fractional relaxation of combinatorial allocation constraints. This enables us to extend existing architectures to the combinatorial setting (CANet, CAFormer) and propose CAGraph , a novel graph-based architecture that generalizes the winner deter-mination problem to a learned, differentiable set-packing task. 3. Resolution-Aware Implementation. We identify the implementability gap created by fractional combinatorial allocations and propose a Matching Pursuit-inspired decomposi-tion that constructs an implementable lottery over feasible deterministic schedules. The method is used as an approximation procedure, with ex-post discretization effects evaluated empirically. 4. Empirical Evaluation on a Real-World Case Study. We evaluate CAGraph on an airport slot-allocation case study using real-world datasets, showing tunable rev-enue–welfare–fairness trade-offs relative to VCG and Random Serial Dictatorship. In the Washington National Airport (DCA) slot-divestiture setting, the conditional full-assignment implementation variant reaches mean realized revenue of $536.3k, a 284.2% increase relative to VCG.

# 2 Related Literature 

Our research relates to multi-objective auction theory, differentiable economics, and airport resource allocation. In each subsection, we summarize the relevant literature in one of these streams. 

2.1 Multi-Objective Auction Theory 

Classical auction theory has primarily focused on single-objective optimization. The Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism serves as the standard solution for guaranteeing dominant-strategy incentive compatibility (DSIC) and exact welfare maximization [Vickrey, 1961, Clarke, 1971, Groves, 1973]. In contrast, Myerson’s optimal auction provides the theoretical foundation for revenue maximization in single-item settings based on virtual valuations [Myerson, 1981]. However, even in bilateral trade, efficiency can conflict with incentive and participation require-ments. The Myerson-Satterthwaite Theorem establishes that no mechanism can simultaneously achieve ex-post efficiency, Bayesian incentive compatibility, interim individual rationality, and bud-get balance [Myerson and Satterthwaite, 1983]. This inherent tension is further exemplified by the Revenue-Welfare Non-Coincidence: even in symmetric settings with uniform valuations, the revenue-maximizing allocation rule (which may withhold items to drive up competition) differs from the welfare-maximizing rule (which always allocates to the highest value) [Myerson, 1981]. Beyond efficiency and revenue, fairness is increasingly recognized as a critical requirement in public resource allocation. Unlike private markets, public domains often require an equitable distribution of 3utility to ensure political and regulatory acceptability. Fairness can be quantified by metrics such as the Jain Index, Gini Coefficient, and Coefficient of Variation. Recent works have explored fairness-welfare trade-offs [e.g., Zografos and Jiang, 2019] and welfare-revenue trade-offs via Automated Mechanism Design [Sandholm, 2003]. However, most existing multi-objective approaches are restricted to single-item or simple unit-demand settings and do not extend to the combinatorial constraints inherent in resource allocation domains, such as airport slot allocation. In contrast to this literature, we do not seek an analytical characterization of a single optimal mechanism. Instead, we provide a data-driven framework for empirically tracing revenue–welfare– fairness frontiers in combinatorial auctions with side constraints. This allows the designer to evaluate policy-relevant trade-offs even when closed-form optimal mechanisms are unavailable. 

2.2 Airport Resource Allocation 

The allocation of airport slots is currently governed by administrative procedures such as the IATA Worldwide Slot Guidelines (WSG). A central feature of this regime is “Grandfather Rights,” which allow incumbent airlines to retain slots in perpetuity, provided they utilize them sufficiently. While providing operational stability, this system creates significant barriers to entry and encourages slot hoarding, impeding allocative efficiency [Ball et al., 2020]. Theoretical alternatives, including congestion pricing [Carlin and Park, 1970, Daniel, 1995, Vaze and Barnhart, 2012] and combinatorial auctions [Rassenti et al., 1982, Ball et al., 2018], have been proposed to address these rigidities but have struggled to gain political traction due to implementation complexities and equity concerns. In practice, regulatory interventions must balance conflicting objectives that pure market mechanisms often ignore. Regulators aim to maximize allocative efficiency (welfare) while frequently needing to generate public funds (revenue) or ensure competitive equity (fairness) [Jacquillat and Vaze, 2018, Zografos and Jiang, 2019]. This multi-objective complexity was exemplified by the 2011 slot transfers at Washington National Airport ( DCA ) and LaGuardia Airport ( LGA ), where regulators sought to foster competition [U.S. Department of Transportation, 2011, Federal Aviation Administration, 2011] by requiring divestitures that expanded access for new-entrants and limited-incumbent carriers. More recently, Spirit Airlines’ 2026 bankruptcy led to a competitive auction of 22 slots at LGA, with JetBlue named the successful bidder at $58.5 million[Federal Aviation Administration, 2026, JetBlue Airways Corporation, 2026]. Consequently, acceptable auction mechanisms must balance revenue generation against allocative efficiency and bidder surplus so that the resulting allocation remains politically feasible and commercially viable for participating airlines. We model airport slot divestiture as a constrained combinatorial auction and use real-world airline data to quantify how alternative learned mechanisms trade off revenue, allocative efficiency, and fairness. Rather than prescribing a single regulatory rule, the framework provides a decision-support tool for comparing policies. 

2.3 Deep Neural Networks for Mechanism Design 

Differentiable Economics has emerged as a promising paradigm for automated mechanism design, leveraging deep learning to learn optimal mechanism rules directly from data. This approach, exemplified by RegretNet [Duetting et al., 2019], parameterizes the mechanism as a neural network and optimizes an objective function—such as revenue maximization—via gradient descent, subject to regret penalties that encourage approximate dominant-strategy incentive compatibility (DSIC). Concurrently, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have shown great promise in solving combinatorial optimization problems [Cappart et al., 2023b] and winner determination problems in energy markets [Ahmed et al., 2023]. The differentiable mechanism-design framework pioneered by RegretNet has demonstrated remarkable success in recovering known optimal auctions in single-item settings and discovering new mechanisms where analytical solutions are unknown. However, extending this paradigm to high-stakes domains like airport slot allocation presents signifi-cant combinatorial challenges. Bidders value specific combinations of items (e.g., paired landing and takeoff slots), creating an exponential valuation space. Crucially, allocations must satisfy strict integrality and feasibility constraints: each item can be allocated at most once, and agents often demand valid bundles rather than fractional fragments. Standard feed-forward neural networks or standard transformer architectures often fail to produce outputs that strictly adhere to these complex feasibility constraints, frequently outputting impractical fractional assignments. 4This creates a critical need for specialized neural architectures that enforce fractional feasibility by design, and for a separate discrete implementation step. Birkhoff’s theorem provides exact lottery implementability for simple assignment structures, whereas overlapping combinatorial constraints need not admit such a decomposition [Budish et al., 2013], we therefore measure and report the residual of a greedy discrete approximation. Our work bridges this gap by introducing graph-based architectures and differentiable resolution layers that inherently satisfy these combinatorial feasibility constraints.

CANet is a deep feed-forward multilayer perceptron (MLP) that maps the flattened valuation profile 

b ∈ Rnk to allocation and payment decisions. The network consists of an input linear projection to a hidden dimension H, followed by L fully connected layers with LeakyReLU activations. To support multi-objective optimization, the architecture splits into two task-specific heads: (i) an Allocation Head that outputs unnormalized logits, which are processed by the constraint factorization layer (Section 4.1) to ensure feasibility; and (ii) a Payment Head that projects the hidden representation to a fractional vector ˜p ∈ [0 , 1] n via Sigmoid activation. 

4.2.2 CAFormer Architecture 

To enhance expressivity and ensure permutation-equivariance, we introduce CAFormer, aTransformer-based architecture that processes the auction state as two parallel streams of exchange-able embeddings: an Item Stream Xitem ∈ Rq×m×H and an Agent Stream Xagent ∈ Rq×n×H , where 

q is the batch size and H is the hidden embedding dimension. These streams pass through L layers of Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) and a specialized Cross-Attention mechanism. Unlike Regret-Former [Ivanov et al., 2022], our architecture uses agent representations to query item representations, allowing the model to learn complex demand structures before producing agent–bundle logits for the constraint enforcement layer. The final output is processed via an invariant attention head to ensure the mechanism’s results are independent of bidder ordering.

4.5 Implementability and Discrete Decomposition 

The neural architectures described above output fractional allocation matrices Z ∈ [0 , 1] n×k, whereas airport slots are indivisible and must ultimately be implemented as deterministic schedules. Thus, fractional feasibility is not sufficient; the learned allocation must either be representable as a lottery over feasible integer allocations or approximated by such a lottery. In simple assignment settings, this implementation gap is closed by the Birkhoff–von Neumann theorem. After augmenting the problem with dummy agents or items so that a doubly sub-stochastic assignment can be completed to a doubly-stochastic matrix, every fractional doubly-stochastic matrix can be written as a convex combination of permutation matrices and is therefore implementable as a lottery over deterministic assignments. Combinatorial slot allocation is more difficult because overlapping bundles induce constraints that are not captured by the standard assignment polytope. Budish et al. [2013] show that universal implementation depends on the structure of the constraints: roughly, constraints must be nested or disjoint rather than partially overlapping. They call such constraint systems bihierarchies . When this structure fails, some feasible fractional allocations may not be implementable exactly as lotteries over deterministic feasible allocations. In particular, their necessity result implies the following. 

Lemma 3 (Budish et al. 2013) . If a canonical two-sided constraint structure is not a bihierarchy, then it is not universally implementable. 

We formalize the connection between this implementability limitation and our slot-allocation setting in Appendix F. This limitation is relevant here because overlapping slot bundles can induce non-bihierarchical constraint structures. # 6 Practical Applications: Airport Slot Allocation 

Capacity-constrained "Level 3" airports (IATA) [International Air Transport Association, 2019] currently rely on administrative "Grandfather Rights," where incumbents retain perpetual tenure via the "80/20 rule." This system incentivizes incumbents to "babysit" underutilized slots to block competition, stifling allocative efficiency. A key regulatory response was the 2011 DOJ-mandated slot divestiture at New York LaGuardia (LGA) and Washington National (DCA) [U.S. Department of Justice, 2011]. Anchoring on this scenario, we model a combinatorial auction among four carriers— JetBlue (B6), Frontier (F9), Spirit (NK), and Sun Country (SY), four candidate slot times (08:00, 11 10:00, 17:00, and 22:00), which generate six two-slot bundles and one four-slot bundle, at DCA, a realistic regulatory approach targeted at facilitating market entry.

Routes and fares are derived from the Airline Origin and Destination Survey (DB1B) dataset. Seat capacities are obtained from the T-100 Domestic Segment (U.S. Carriers) dataset. All data are downloaded from https://www.transtats.bts.gov . A profit-maximizing assignment of flights to available slots determines the bundles’ valuations.

Revenue is estimated as the product of the average fare, the number of seats, and the average load factor. Operational costs follow the aircraft cost specification of Swan and Adler [2006] as a function of distance and seat capacity. 6.2 Benchmark Mechanisms 

Our framework is compared against two opposite ends of the welfare-fairness spectrum. 

Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG). As the canonical welfare-maximizing mechanism, VCG selects the maximum weighted independent set on the conflict graph. However, it is poorly suited for slot divestitures due to: (i) Low revenue , as VCG payments can be small relative to revenue-maximizing alternatives, providing insufficient compensation; and (ii) Market Concentration , as welfare maximization can concentrate allocations among high-value bidders. 

Random Serial Dictatorship (RSD / Lottery). The FAA procedure under 14 CFR §93.225 uses random draws to establish priority for sequential slot selection. It provides procedural symmetry and does not solicit valuation reports. Standard serial dictatorship is strategy-proof under its direct-choice model, but the administrative priority lottery is not the same as a direct-revelation combinatorial mechanism. Because it ignores monetary valuations, it can sacrifice welfare. For instance, a bidder with a $10M peak-hour valuation may lose to a $1M bidder based solely on draw order, failing to allocate capacity to its highest-value use. 

Learned Mechanism (CAGraph). Our CAGraph architecture serves as a tunable alternative to static benchmarks, via the mixing parameter λ, with fairness controlled separately through a soft Jain-index target. It enables regulators to navigate trade-offs among revenue, welfare, and fairness while maintaining approximate incentive compatibility. To ensure a consistent comparison, Sections 6.3-6.5 report ex-ante metrics derived from the fractional allocations Z ∈ [0 , 1] n×k, with realized ex-post outcomes analyzed in Section 6.7.

We audit approximate incentive compatibility using the aggregate validation regret produced by the adversarial misreport optimization procedure. As in prior differentiable mechanism design work, this regret is an empirical local-best-response estimate. Table 6 summarizes the maximum mean regret observed across each reported frontier. In all three families, empirical regret is small relative to the revenue gains obtained over VCG. These values audit the learned fractional mechanism. They do not by themselves establish low regret after discrete resolution and conditional pricing.

6.8 Structural Robustness and Scalability 

Parameter sensitivity. Because the airport valuations are model-based estimates, we regenerate bundle valuations after perturbing the calibrated operating-cost coefficient and time-of-day preference levels one at a time from 75% to 125% of baseline. Each level contains 100 independently regenerated profiles, and the fixed trained mechanism is evaluated using its training-data normalization and bidder-level dollar-payment conversion. Figure 7 shows distinct responses. Operating-cost changes are economically modest: at the ±25% endpoints, welfare changes by +3 .6% / − 3.3% and revenue by +3 .2% / − 2.8% , with local point elasticities of −0.13 and −0.12 , respectively. Time-of-day preferences are more consequential: the corresponding welfare changes are −24 .9% / + 24 .6% and the revenue changes are −19 .1% / + 21 .0% , with local point elasticities of 0.98 and 0.81 . The bootstrap bands show that the cost response is small relative to Monte Carlo uncertainty, whereas the endpoint time-preference effects remain separated from baseline. Reallocation remains concentrated between B6 and F9: the ±25% time perturbations move their allocation shares by about 1.1–1.2 percentage points in opposite directions, whereas NK and SY remain unchanged at the reported precision. The shaded bands are 95% nonparametric bootstrap intervals and appropriately reflect that the profiles are independently regenerated at each level. These results use the welfare–fairness checkpoint with target J = 0 .25 ; this differs from the target-J = 0 .875 mechanism in Figure 5(d). 

Allocation stability. We measure allocation stability by ∥Z(ϵ) − Z(0) ∥F , the Frobenius distance between perturbed and baseline allocation matrices. Even under ±25% perturbations, this distance remains below 0.08, with changes concentrated on marginal bundles rather than core allocations. 

Computational scalability. On an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000, a forward pass, including winner determination and pricing, takes under 15ms. Complexity analysis is presented in Appendix I. For larger slot systems, Appendix K outlines a possible hierarchical decomposition that would partition the conflict graph into weakly coupled temporal submarkets, solve local combinatorial auctions in parallel, and reconcile inter-block dependencies through a coordination layer. This extension is proposed for future work and is not evaluated in the present experiments.

Auditing Incentive Trade-offs Reporting empirical regret alongside revenue and welfare allows managers to compare economic gains with the largest deviations found by the configured misreport search. A regulator may use this evidence to screen candidate mechanisms. By accepting a regret budget, regulators can unlock higher revenue or welfare in complex markets, transforming incentive compatibility from a binary constraint into a manageable economic variable for the public interest. 

Policy Validation Support While neural mechanisms are trained for specific market distributions, their sub-15ms inference enables rapid re-evaluation as bids change within the trained market specification. Structural changes to the feasible bundles or conflict graph require a separate validation or retraining. The model may therefore supplement, rather than replace, transparent policies by providing rapid checks of candidate trade-offs. 

Flexible Policy Instrument Our multi-objective framework introduces flexible policy via the weighting factor λ or the Jain-index target J. By tuning these quantities, managers can prioritize different objectives, transforming a fixed auction into a tunable policy instrument. 

# 7 Conclusion 

Beyond extending differentiable economics to the combinatorial domain, this paper presents an airport-slot case study and addresses the “last-mile” problem of converting fractional combinatorial allocations into feasible discrete schedules. CAGraph models bundle conflicts while tracing the revenue–welfare–fairness frontier, and the Matching Pursuit-inspired decomposition provides a tractable discrete approximation whose residual quantifies the remaining implementation gap. Our empirical case study on airport slot allocation demonstrates that this integrated ap-proach—combining CAGraph architectures with discrete implementation logic—allows regulators to explicitly navigate the revenue–welfare–fairness Pareto frontier with unprecedented precision. We find expected revenue gains of up to 73.5% in synthetic settings and 180% in the airport-slot case study compared to VCG, while maintaining low empirical regret. Ultimately, this framework transforms auction design from a rigid mathematical exercise into a flexible, data-driven policy instrument for modern resource management. Beyond revenue, the mechanism’s millisecond inference enables agile management, such as intra-day reallocation. While our models are currently distribution-specific, future work in zero-shot transfer, or meta-learning could adapt these architectures to structural market changes without retraining. By treating incentive alignment as a manageable economic variable and providing the tools to visualize its impact on policy goals, this work offers a practical, high-performance path for modernizing large-scale resource allocation. 18 References

F.1 Implementability Limitation 

Let B denote the set of deterministic feasible schedules. Each B ∈ B is a binary matrix satisfying the item-wise and bidder-wise feasibility constraints. A fractional allocation Z is exactly implementable as a lottery over feasible deterministic schedules if and only if 

Z ∈ conv( B).

Equivalently, there exist weights {πB }B∈B such that 

Z = X

> B∈B

X

> B∈B

However, combinatorial bundle constraints generally produce set-packing polytopes whose linear relaxations are not integral. The bihierarchy characteri-zation of Budish et al. [2013] gives a structural condition under which universal implementation is possible for canonical two-sided constraint systems. When the constraint structure is not a bihierarchy, universal implementation fails: there exist feasible fractional allocations that cannot be expressed as lotteries over deterministic feasible allocations. Airport slot bundles can induce precisely this failure. A bundle may contain multiple physical or temporal resources, and two bundles conflict whenever they share at least one resource. Such overlaps can create cycles in the conflict structure, violating the nested or disjoint structure required by a bihierarchy. Therefore, feasibility of Z under the fractional constraints does not by itself imply 

Z ∈ conv( B). This proves Proposition 4. 

Minimal conflict-cycle example. Consider three resources a, b, c and three bundles 

S1 = {a, b }, S2 = {b, c }, S3 = {a, c }.

Each pair of bundles overlaps, so at most one bundle can be selected in any deterministic schedule. The fractional point 

zS1 = zS2 = zS3 = 1 /2

satisfies the per-resource constraints because each resource has total load one. However, the total bundle mass is 3/2, whereas any deterministic feasible schedule selects at most one bundle. Hence this fractional point cannot be represented as a convex combination of deterministic feasible schedules. # G Case Study Details 

G.1 Airport Valuation Construction with Gurobi 

The airport valuations are model-based estimates of the maximum operating profit that a carrier can obtain from a candidate slot bundle. The construction uses the DB1B Ticket and Coupon 28 tables. Coupon records are ordered by ITIN_ID ; the first origin and final destination are joined to the corresponding ticket record to recover each itinerary’s endpoints. For the reported experiment, itineraries are retained when either endpoint is Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport (DCA; BTS airport ID 11278). Each retained flight is marked as a departure if DCA is its origin and as an arrival if DCA is its destination. The resulting route, fare, distance, capacity, and direction fields are used to construct the flight–slot profit coefficients Vi(f, t ) described in Section 6.1. The candidate times are 08:00, 10:00, 17:00, and 22:00. Candidate bundles contain an even number of these times, yielding the six two-slot combinations and the single four-slot combination, for seven bundles in total. For a flight f , write Ff , Sf , and Df for its ITIN_FARE , SEAT_CAP , and DISTANCE .For each generated valuation profile, the reported training artifact fixes the per-mile operating-cost coefficient at cmile = 0 .019 and computes 

Revenue f = 0 .7Ff Sf , (53) 

Cost f = cmile (Df + 722)( Sf + 104) , (54)

G.1.1 Slot Allocation at U.S. Coordinated Airports: Practice and Precedent 

In the United States, a small number of high-demand airports—including John F. Kennedy Interna-tional (JFK), LaGuardia (LGA), and Ronald Reagan Washington National (DCA)—are designated as Level 3 coordinated airports under IATA’s Worldwide Slot Guidelines (WSG). Slot allocation at these airports is managed by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) through a hybrid system based on regulatory oversight and historical usage rights. 

Historical Priority and the 80% Rule The cornerstone of current U.S. slot allocation is the “grandfather rights” system, whereby carriers retain priority to operate a slot in future seasons if they use it at least 80% of the time during the current season. This “use-it-or-lose-it” rule is intended to deter slot hoarding and promote efficient utilization of limited runway capacity. Slots that are underutilized below this threshold are returned to a common pool for reallocation. 

Reallocation Mechanisms Slots may become available through several channels: voluntary sur-render, underutilization (per the 80% rule), or involuntary divestiture—particularly in the context of antitrust enforcement or airline mergers. For example, during the 2011 Delta–US Airways slot exchange, the DOT required the divestiture of 24 slot pairs at LGA and DCA to preserve market competitiveness. These divested slots were bundled and auctioned to eligible new entrants or limited incumbents, marking a rare instance of competitive slot auction in the U.S. When slots return to the pool, the FAA typically uses a randomized lottery to determine the order of selection by eligible carriers. This mechanism resembles a Random Serial Dictatorship (RSD): carriers sequentially select individual slots from the available pool without submitting bids or revealing valuations. These procedures are formalized in 14 CFR §93.225, which governs slot lotteries at constrained airports. Historically, slot lotteries have been employed under the FAA’s High Density Rule (HDR), particularly during the 1980s and early 2000s. For example, the FAA issued a Federal Register notice in 2003 clarifying the lottery process at DCA, including eligibility conditions for new entrants and limited incumbents (link). Additionally, the 2015 Slot Management and Transparency Rule reiterated the FAA’s use of randomized procedures to allocate new or temporarily available slots, often with priority given to new entrants (link). While auctions remain rare in U.S. slot allocation, these precedents illustrate that both randomized and market-based mechanisms are feasible within the current regulatory framework. Our case study builds on these insights by evaluating combinatorial allocation approaches under realistic, small-scale scenarios, such as divestiture or reallocation events involving limited bidders and bundles. 30 G.1.2 Comparison to Lottery and Serial Dictatorship Mechanisms 

While our mechanism builds on a learned, data-driven allocation and payment rule, it is instructive to compare it with simpler mechanisms commonly used or considered in practice, such as pure lotteries and serial dictatorship. These mechanisms have intuitive appeal but lack important economic guarantees that our approach approximates. 

Pure Lottery. In the stylized pure-lottery benchmark used in the main text, bidders receive equal allocation shares without regard to their valuations. This provides procedural symmetry but can be inefficient and yields no revenue. Because the benchmark solicits no reports, report-based incentive compatibility is vacuous; its equal shares should not be interpreted as equal utility. 

Serial Dictatorship. In this mechanism, bidders are ordered (randomly or by priority), and each in turn selects from the remaining options. Serial dictatorship is strategy-proof under its standard model, in which each agent directly chooses its most preferred available alternative. An item-by-item adaptation is not equivalent when bidders have complementarities over slot bundles, because a sequence of individual picks need not represent preferences over complete bundles. The administrative FAA priority lottery is also not a direct-revelation auction and should not be assigned the same incentive properties without specifying its choice protocol. 

Our Mechanism (Learned Auction). Our approach uses a neural mechanism that outputs a fractional allocation rule over structured bundles and an expected payment target. The allocation is resolved into a lottery using Section H, and realized payments use the conditional rule described there. The mechanism is trained for low empirical regret and to optimize a chosen revenue–welfare objective; these are approximate empirical properties rather than exact guarantees.

G.1.3 The 2011 Slot Auction 

While slot auctions have been proposed repeatedly — notably by the FAA in 2008–2009 — such proposals have met resistance from incumbent airlines and legal challenges, and have not been implemented. An instance of an actual slot auction at U.S. airports occurred in 2011, as a result of a DOT-imposed condition on the Delta–US Airways slot exchange at LGA and DCA. To preserve competition, the DOT required the divestiture of: 16 slot pairs at LGA, and 8 slot pairs at DCA. These were auctioned off via a sealed-bid, one-round auction, with eligibility restricted to carriers holding less than 5% of the slots at the respective airport. The slots were sold in predefined bundles: Two bundles of 8 slot pairs at LGA, One bundle of 8 slot pairs at DCA. Winning bids were determined solely by price, and payments went directly to the divesting airlines. JetBlue and WestJet were the winning bidders. According to the GAO, JetBlue paid $32 million for LGA slots and $40 million for DCA slots, while WestJet paid $17.6 million for the second LGA bundle. The 2011 auction thus serves as a rare but valuable precedent. It confirms that sealed-bid, small-scale slot auctions are legally and operationally feasible under the right regulatory framework. This makes it a compelling test case for mechanism design approaches, particularly when applied to limited, high-value slot reallocations involving a small number of bidders and bundles. 31 H Greedy Discrete Approximation and Proportional Pricing 

The neural mechanism produces a fractional allocation matrix Z ∈ [0 , 1] n×| K| satisfying the relaxed combinatorial feasibility constraints. In the airport slot-allocation setting, however, the implemented outcome must be a deterministic feasible schedule. This appendix describes the resolution procedure used to map the fractional allocation to a lottery over deterministic schedules, and the associated ex-post payment rule. A key distinction is important. In simple assignment problems, fractional feasibility can imply exact implementability as a lottery over deterministic assignments. In the combinatorial setting considered here, overlapping bundles can induce non-bihierarchical constraint structures, so fractional feasibility alone does not guarantee that Z ∈ conv( B), where B denotes the set of feasible deterministic schedules. Accordingly, the procedure below should be interpreted as a greedy discrete approximation rather than a guaranteed exact convex decomposition.

# I Computational Complexity 

Let n denote the number of bidders, m the number of items, and k = |K| the number of candidate bundles (equal to 2m − 1 when every nonempty bundle is enumerated). In the fully expressive combinatorial representation, each bidder can submit a value for each bundle, so the bid tensor contains O(nk ) entries. This exponential growth in k is the dominant scalability bottleneck of any fully enumerative combinatorial auction method, including ours. For CANet, the input valuation vector is flattened and passed through L fully connected layers of hidden dimension H. Ignoring batch size, the forward-pass complexity is 

O(nkH + LH 2),

where the first term corresponds to the input and output projections and the second term corresponds to the hidden layers. For CAFormer, the complexity depends on the number of tokens used to represent bidders, items, and bundles. With O(n + k) tokens and hidden dimension H, each self-attention layer has worst-case complexity 

O(( n + k)2H + ( n + k)H2).

Thus, CAFormer can be expressive but becomes increasingly expensive as the number of feasible bundles grows. For CAGraph, we construct a conflict graph whose nodes correspond to bidder-bundle pairs. Hence, the graph has 

|V | = O(nk )

nodes. Edges connect mutually incompatible allocations, including allocations involving overlapping bundles or the same bidder. A graph attention layer has complexity 

O(|E|H),

where |E| is the number of conflict edges. In the worst case, |E| = O(n2k2), but in practical slot-allocation instances the graph is substantially sparser because edges are induced only by actual bundle conflicts. Moreover, the airport application uses a filtered set of operationally valid bundles rather than all 2m − 1 possible bundles. The Capacity Normalization layer adds only polynomial overhead relative to the enumerated bundle set. Its operations consist primarily of normalization and masking over bidder-bundle and item-bundle tensors, which scale as 

O(nk + mk ).

Therefore, the constraint layer does not change the fundamental exponential dependence on m; rather, it ensures that the network output satisfies the combinatorial feasibility constraints by construction. Overall, the dominant computational bottlenecks are the exponential growth of the bundle set and, for discrete implementation, the repeated winner-determination oracle used in the Matching Pursuit decomposition. The neural feasibility layer itself adds only polynomial overhead in the number of enumerated bidder-bundle variables. The proposed framework is therefore most appropriate for targeted combinatorial allocation problems with a moderate number of valid bundles, such as regulatory slot divestitures, spectrum packages, or infrastructure-capacity assignments.
