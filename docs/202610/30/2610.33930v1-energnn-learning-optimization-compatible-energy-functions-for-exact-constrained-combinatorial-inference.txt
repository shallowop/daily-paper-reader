# Abstract 

Learning-based approaches to combinatorial optimization often predict solutions directly or use predictions to restrict search, while expressive energy-based models typically rely on approximate inference. We introduce EnerGNN, a framework that learns energy functions over complete binary configurations and minimizes them under explicit hard constraints through mixed integer programming (MIP). En-erGNN combines a graph neural network encoder with an optimization-compatible readout and a nonlinear energy head, enabling exact constrained inference over the learned energy. An optimality-aware contrastive loss trains the energy to reflect solution quality and constraint violations. Across Quadratic Knapsack, Combinatorial Auctions, and a personalized cell therapy supply chain problem, EnerGNN achieves near-optimal solutions with 100% feasibility. On the supply chain problem, it achieves a 0.05% mean primal gap and a 17.4 × speedup over the monolithic MIP. The learned energy also generalizes to larger instances and supports zero-shot constraint injection, adapting to changing constraints without retraining while preserving feasibility. 

# 1 Introduction 

A central challenge in energy-based structured prediction is to combine expressive learned repre-sentations with tractable inference over structured outputs. Energy-based models (EBMs) assign scalar energies to complete output configurations, allowing dependencies and interactions between output variables to be captured through learned energy functions (LeCun et al., 2006; Belanger and McCallum, 2016). However, minimizing expressive neural energies over discrete decision spaces is generally challenging, particularly when solutions must satisfy hard operational, physical, or logical constraints. Mixed integer programming (MIP) provides a framework for enforcing such constraints and obtaining globally optimal solutions, but learned energy functions must admit suitable mathematical representations to be optimized directly. This creates an interface problem between neural learning and exact optimization: How can a neural model learn expressive energy functions that an exact optimizer can minimize under hard constraints? 

Existing approaches address different aspects of this challenge. In learning-based combinatorial optimization, neural models have been used to construct solutions, predict variable assignments, and guide exact solvers (Bengio et al., 2021; Khalil et al., 2017; Gasse et al., 2019). Methods such as Neural Diving (Nair et al., 2020) and ConPaS (Huang et al., 2024) use learned variable-level predictions to identify promising regions of the solution space, which are then explored through restricted optimization. Meanwhile, ReLU neural networks can be represented exactly using mixed integer formulations (Fischetti and Jo, 2018; Anderson et al., 2020), providing a mechanism for optimizing learned nonlinear functions under explicit constraints. Together, these developments motivate learning energy functions that are designed to be directly optimized under explicit hard constraints, bringing expressive structured prediction and exact combinatorial inference into a unified framework. We introduce EnerGNN , an energy-based framework that learns optimization-compatible energy functions over complete binary configurations and minimizes them subject to explicit hard constraints. As illustrated in Figure 1, EnerGNN combines a bipartite message-passing graph neural network (GNN) encoder with a decision-dependent readout and a piecewise linear ReLU energy head. The GNN encoder produces representations of the problem instance that are computed once and remain fixed during optimization. The readout combines these fixed representations with the binary decision variables to construct a configuration-dependent representation. Together, the readout and nonlinear ReLU energy head allow EnerGNN to model interactions between decision variables while admitting an exact MIP representation. At inference, the learned energy is minimized over the full encoded feasible region using a MIP solver, yielding a feasible solution that is globally optimal with respect to the learned energy when solved to optimality. A key feature of EnerGNN is the separation of learned preferences from explicit feasibility con-straints. The energy-based model learns to distinguish high-quality configurations from suboptimal alternatives through optimality-aware contrastive training, while hard constraints are enforced directly during inference. This separation allows the same learned energy to be optimized under different feasible regions, without retraining the model. In particular, deployment-time constraints can be added, removed, or modified by changing the inference MIP, enabling zero-shot constraint injection. This approach is complementary to neural algorithmic reasoning (Veliˇ ckovi´ c and Blundell, 2021): rather than learning to execute an optimization algorithm, EnerGNN learns an energy function over structured decisions and delegates constrained inference to an exact optimizer. Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate constrained combinatorial inference as learning an optimization-compatible energy over complete binary configurations, separating learned preferences from explicit hard constraints. Second, we develop a graph-based energy architecture that captures nonlinear interactions between decision variables while admitting exact MIP representation and globally optimal inference with respect to the learned energy. Third, we develop an optimality-aware contrastive learning objective using feasible and infeasible candidate configurations to learn solution-level energy landscapes. We evaluate EnerGNN on Quadratic Knapsack (QKP), Combinatorial Auctions (CA), and a personalized cell therapy supply chain problem (CART). Our experiments demonstrate the benefits of nonlinear energy modeling for interaction-heavy objectives, the ability to obtain near-optimal solutions while satisfying hard constraints, generalization to larger problem sizes, and zero-shot constraint injection without retraining. The code and data used in this work are available at https://github.com/nikitrian/EnerGNN .

# 2 Related Work 

Learning-based methods for combinatorial optimization. Learning-based approaches to com-binatorial optimization commonly use neural models to amortize expensive search across related problem instances. Neural combinatorial optimization methods learn policies that construct solutions sequentially using graph-based or attention-based architectures, often trained through reinforcement learning (Khalil et al., 2017; Bello et al., 2016; Kool et al., 2019; Kwon et al., 2020). Another 2line of work integrates learning into branch-and-bound algorithms, including branching-variable selection (Khalil et al., 2016; Gasse et al., 2019; Gupta et al., 2020) and search-tree node selection (He et al., 2014). Learning-based predict-and-search methods instead use neural predictions to identify promising variable assignments and solve reduced or restricted optimization problems. Neural Diving predicts distributions over variable assignments and fixes subsets of variables before solving reduced subproblems (Nair et al., 2020). Predict-and-Search (PaS) uses GNN predictions to define search neighborhoods around predicted solutions (Han et al., 2023), while Contrastive Predict-and-Search (ConPaS) extends this approach with contrastive learning to improve solution prediction (Huang et al., 2024). 

Differentiable optimization and constraint enforcement. Decision-focused learning trains predic-tive models using downstream decision quality, while differentiable optimization methods integrate optimization problems into neural networks and propagate learning signals through their solutions (Amos and Kolter, 2017; Agrawal et al., 2019; Ferber et al., 2020; Paulus et al., 2021; Parmentier, 2025). These approaches enable learning of optimization problem parameters, including objective coefficients and, in some formulations, constraints. Related methods enforce feasibility of neural predictions through differentiable correction, projection, or constraint-enforcement layers (Donti et al., 2021; Min and Azizan, 2024; Wang et al., 2023; Tordesillas et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024). Unlike differentiable optimization approaches, EnerGNN uses the MIP solver for inference without differentiating through it during training. 

Energy-based structured prediction. Structured Prediction Energy Networks (SPENs) learn expressive energy functions over structured outputs and perform inference through continuous relaxation and gradient-based optimization (Belanger and McCallum, 2016). Input Convex Neural Networks (ICNNs) impose convexity with respect to the optimized inputs, enabling globally optimal inference in continuous convex settings (Amos et al., 2017). Graph Structured Prediction Energy Networks combine explicit local structure with learned higher-order interactions and perform iterative inference over a constrained relaxation (Graber and Schwing, 2019). Blondel et al. (2022) develop generalized Fenchel–Young losses for training energy networks without differentiating through the inference optimization problem. Energy-based models have also been integrated into decision-focused stochastic optimization, where learned energy functions are used to approximate optimization landscapes (Kong et al., 2022). More recently, Huang et al. (2026) propose an energy-based Langevin sampler for mixed integer optimization, combining neural sampling of integer variables with linear programming for continuous variables. EnerGNN builds on energy-based structured prediction but designs its learned energy function to admit an exact MIP representation, enabling global minimization over binary configurations under explicit hard constraints without requiring convexity. 

Mixed integer representations of neural networks. Piecewise linear neural networks, particularly ReLU networks, can be represented exactly using mixed integer linear constraints (Tjeng et al., 2017; Fischetti and Jo, 2018; Anderson et al., 2020; Tsay et al., 2021). These formulations have been used for neural network verification and for embedding trained neural surrogate models inside optimization problems. Bound-tightening techniques can improve the strength and computational performance of these formulations (Grimstad and Andersson, 2019). EnerGNN uses these formulations to optimize a learned energy over binary configurations, with the GNN encoder evaluated before optimization and the readout and ReLU energy head represented exactly within the MIP formulation.

We first formulate the learning and inference problems, then describe the graph-based energy architecture, the training objective, and its exact MIP encoding. Finally, we establish the theoretical properties of the resulting framework.

3.4 Inference via Neural-MIP Encoding 

At inference, the trained GNN encoder is evaluated once, while the decision-dependent readout and ReLU energy head are represented exactly within the MIP using auxiliary variables and linear constraints. Valid pre-activation bounds are obtained through interval bound propagation using benchmark-specific input bounds (Gowal et al., 2018). Neurons with fixed activation states are elimi-nated to reduce the formulation size. The exact Neural-MIP formulation is detailed in Appendix C, with the bound-tightening procedure described in Appendix C.2. 

3.5 Theoretical Properties 

We summarize the formal properties induced by the architectural design. Detailed proofs are provided in Appendix A. 

Proposition 3.1 (MIP-representable energy) . For a fixed instance graph G, assume that the encoder is evaluated once, the readout z0(y) admits an exact mixed integer linear representation over a bounded domain, and the energy head is a feed-forward ReLU network. Then y 7 → Eθ (G, y) is exactly MIP-representable on that domain. Proof sketch. For fixed G, encoder outputs are constants. The readout admits an exact mixed integer linear representation by construction. A ReLU network is a composition of affine maps and ReLU operators, each of which admits an exact mixed integer linear encoding over bounded input domains. # 4 Experiments 

We evaluate EnerGNN on three combinatorial optimization problems: Quadratic Knapsack (QKP), Combinatorial Auctions (CA), and a Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy supply chain (CART). QKP and CA evaluate direct decision inference, while CART considers hierarchical decomposition, where EnerGNN selects high-level capacitated facility location decisions before solving a reduced downstream patient scheduling problem. Our experiments assess solution quality, feasibility, the contribution of nonlinear energy modeling, cross-scale generalization, and zero-shot constraint injection. Each benchmark examines a different aspect of the framework: QKP tests nonlinear interactions between variables, CA evaluates inference under packing constraints, and CART examines learning over complicating variables in hierarchical optimization. 

4.1 Benchmarks Quadratic Knapsack. QKP is an interaction-heavy problem involving the selection of items under a capacity constraint, with both individual profits and pairwise interactions contributing to the objective. We consider instances with 150 binary item-selection variables and an interaction density of 0.30. 

Cell Therapy Supply Chain. CART is a time-indexed, capacitated facility location and patient allocation MIP for personalized CAR T-cell therapy supply chains. The network comprises 16 candidate manufacturing facilities. The formulation incorporates 2 transportation modes and daily scheduling over a 130-day horizon, with patient demand varying from 10 to 650 across different demand distributions. EnerGNN optimizes the 16 first-stage facility location variables, with each configuration of these variables inducing a tactical patient scheduling MIP. 

Combinatorial Auctions. CA is a packing problem with 175 binary bid-selection variables, extended to 250 in the cross-scale evaluation. Each bid consumes a subset of items, and each item can be allocated at most once. Because the original objective is linear and these instances are comparatively easy for modern MIP solvers, CA primarily evaluates constrained inference and generalization rather than computational speedup. Detailed benchmark problem formulations are given in Appendix B. Instance generation procedures and dataset statistics are described in Appendix D. Benchmark-specific Neural-MIP input bounds and hard feasibility constraints are detailed in Appendices C.3 and C.4, respectively. 

4.2 Baselines and Metrics 

We compare against three classes of baselines that differ in how learning is combined with optimiza-tion. Full MIP solves the original problem using branch-and-cut and provides a reference optimum. 

Predict+Search , Neural Diving (Nair et al., 2020), and ConPaS (Huang et al., 2024) use learned predictions to restrict or guide the solver’s search. Predict+Search fixes variables based on prediction 6confidence and solves the resulting reduced problem. Neural Diving samples variable assignments, fixes subsets of variables, and solves multiple reduced subproblems. ConPaS instead imposes a neighborhood constraint around its learned prediction while retaining the original objective. 

Linear+MIP and EnerGNN learn objectives that are optimized directly under explicit hard constraints. Linear+MIP uses additive per-variable scores, whereas EnerGNN learns a joint energy over complete configurations that can capture interactions between decisions. We evaluate Linear+MIP with binary cross-entropy (BCE) and contrastive supervision, and include EnerGNN (Linear) as an ablation that replaces the ReLU energy head with a linear head over the same readout. This isolates the contribution of the nonlinear energy head. All learning-based methods use the same bipartite GNN encoder and input features where applicable, but differ in their prediction heads, training objectives, and inference procedures. Full implementation details and inference settings are provided in Appendix K. We report mean primal gap, runtime, feasibility, and the fraction of instances with a primal gap of at most 1% relative to the certified global optimum. 4.4 Cross-Scale Generalization 

We evaluate whether EnerGNN transfers to different problem sizes without retraining. In the CART setting, the model is trained on instances with m = 16 candidate facilities, and is evaluated at m = 12 

and m = 20 . In CA, the number of binary bid variables increases from 175 during training to 250 at test time. Table 2 shows that EnerGNN maintains 100% feasibility across all evaluated scales. In CART, the model achieves a mean gap of 0.98% when transferred to m = 12 facilities and 1.23% when extrapolated to m = 20 , compared with 0.05% at the training scale. For CA, we report the linear head variant of EnerGNN, which achieves the best performance on this benchmark. Results for the nonlinear energy head are provided in Appendix I. 4.8 Limitations 

EnerGNN requires the decision-dependent energy head to remain MIP-representable, creating an expressiveness–tractability trade-off in the embedded component. In our experiments, compact shallow heads are sufficient, while increasing width or depth can substantially increase inference time without systematically improving solution quality. Additional representational capacity can instead be placed in the GNN encoder, which is evaluated before optimization and does not increase Neural-MIP size. Training also relies on solver-generated candidate configurations and labels, introducing a one-time offline cost that is most appropriate for repeated-deployment settings; a complete cost and amortization analysis is provided in Appendix D.1. Finally, exact inference is with respect to the learned energy rather than the original objective. Constraint injection guarantees feasibility with respect to the encoded constraints, but solution quality may degrade under sufficiently large distribution or feasible-set shifts. 

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced EnerGNN, an energy-based framework that learns optimization-compatible en-ergy functions over binary configurations and minimizes them under explicit hard constraints through mixed integer optimization. The framework combines a graph-based encoder with a MIP-representable readout and nonlinear energy head, enabling exact optimization of the learned energy. Our experiments demonstrate the framework across direct decision and hierarchical decomposition settings. EnerGNN achieves near-optimal solutions with 100% feasibility across Quadratic Knapsack, Combinatorial Auctions, and a personalized cell therapy supply chain problem. On the latter, EnerGNN achieves substantial computational speedups over the monolithic MIP and learning-based baselines while maintaining near-optimal solution quality. EnerGNN also generalizes to larger problem instances without retraining and supports zero-shot constraint injection, allowing the same learned energy to be optimized under changing operational constraints while preserving feasibility. This adaptability is particularly valuable in deployment settings where operational requirements and resource availability may change over time. More broadly, EnerGNN suggests a design principle for reliable AI systems. When discrete decisions must satisfy hard constraints, learning can shape objectives that algorithmic modules optimize rather than producing final decisions directly. This shifts the learned object from the decision itself to an optimization-compatible objective over decisions, allowing learned preferences and explicit constraints to remain separate at inference.

# D Dataset Splits and Candidate Pool Construction 

Dataset splits. For each benchmark, we use 800 training instances, 100 validation instances, and 100 held-out test instances. For each training instance, we construct a candidate pool Y = F ∪I containing feasible and infeasible configurations. Pool construction is benchmark-specific. 

CART. For CART, each instance has 16 candidate facilities. We first solve the unrestricted patient-level MIP to global optimality and collect incumbent facility selection configurations from branch-and-bound. Additional candidates are generated using capacity- and transport-guided heuristics, local Hamming perturbations, swaps, facility additions and removals, and random configurations with varying cardinality. Each candidate is evaluated by solving the corresponding tactical patient scheduling MIP. Feasible configurations attaining the proven global optimum within numerical tolerance are labeled positive, other feasible configurations are labeled feasible negatives, and 23 configurations certified infeasible are labeled infeasible negatives. We retain 160 candidates per instance through stratified selection, initially keeping up to three positives and targeting 30% infeasible negatives among the remaining candidates. 

CA. For CA, we first solve each instance with Gurobi and collect incumbent solutions from branch-and-bound. Feasible solutions with relative primal gap at most ϵpos = 0 .01 are labelled positive, up to a maximum of 50 positives per instance. We then construct 64 feasible negatives using a quota-driven mixture of additional incumbent solutions, guided local Hamming perturbations, and farther Hamming perturbations of the best solution. The feasible-negative selection is balanced across Hamming distance buckets and primal gap bands. Finally, we generate 50 infeasible negatives by perturbing feasible masks into different violation bands, corresponding to mild, moderate, and severe item conflicts. 

QKP. For QKP, we impose a one-hour time limit on Gurobi per instance. Training and validation may include instances for which global optimality is not certified within this limit. In contrast, the test set contains instances with Gurobi certified global optima, allowing all reported primal gaps to be measured against proven optimal solutions. For each training instance, we retain the best available solution together with all incumbent solutions whose relative primal gap is at most ϵpos = 0 .01 ; these form the positive subset. We then generate 128 feasible negatives and 64 infeasible negatives using stochastic mask proposals. Each proposal is either a random binary mask with random density or a mutation of the best mask obtained by flipping a random subset of items. Proposed masks that satisfy the knapsack constraint are retained as feasible negatives, while those that violate the capacity constraint are retained as infeasible negatives.

All neural methods use the same bipartite GNN encoder architecture, with method-specific prediction or energy heads. We describe these heads, training objectives, and inference procedures below. 

Predict+Search. Predict+Search applies a single linear layer to each variable embedding, followed by a sigmoid, to predict its probability pj of taking value one. The model is trained using binary cross entropy supervision on the variable assignments of positive candidate solutions. At inference, we rank variables by confidence |pj − 0.5| and fix the most confident 85% for QKP and 80% for CA to their predicted values 1[pj ≥ 0.5] . For CART, we instead use a two-sided confidence band, where facility variables with pj ≥ 0.6 are fixed to one, those with pj ≤ 0.4 are fixed to zero, and the remaining facility variables are left free. We then solve the reduced MIP. 

Linear+MIP. Linear+MIP applies a single linear layer to each variable embedding to produce a scalar score sj . These scores define an additive objective s(y) = P 

> j

sj yj , which is maximized under the original hard constraints. We evaluate two training variants with the same architecture: (i) binary cross entropy supervision on variable assignments, using σ(sj ) as the predicted probability, and (ii) contrastive supervision over candidate solutions, using the energy E(y) = −s(y). At inference, both variants optimize the learned linear objective over all decision variables using a MIP solver. 

Neural Diving. Our Neural Diving (Nair et al., 2020) baseline uses a two-layer variable-level MLP head, with one hidden layer of width dh, a ReLU activation, and dropout, followed by a sigmoid, to predict an independent Bernoulli probability pj for each binary decision variable. The model is trained with binary cross entropy supervision on the variable assignments of positive candidate solutions. At inference, we perform K stochastic dives by sampling candidate assignments from these Bernoulli probabilities. For each dive, a confidence-selected subset of variables is fixed to the sampled assignment, and the reduced MIP is optimized over the remaining free variables. The final solution is the best feasible solution found across all dives. We use K = 20 dives for QKP, K = 8 for CART, and K = 100 for CA. The smaller dive budget for CART reflects the higher computational cost of solving its reduced MIPs. For QKP and CA, we fix the most confident 85% and 50% of variables, respectively. For CART, because the number of facility variables is small, we instead use the same two-sided confidence band as Predict+Search: variables with pj ≥ 0.6 or pj ≤ 0.4 are fixed to their sampled values, and the remaining variables are left free.

# Broader Impact 

This work develops learning-augmented optimization methods for constrained combinatorial decision-making. A potential positive impact is to make learned decision systems more reliable in settings where feasibility is essential, such as logistics, supply chain planning, manufacturing, and healthcare operations. Rather than producing decisions directly, EnerGNN learns an objective landscape that is optimized under explicit hard constraints. This may help decision-support tools adapt to changing demand, capacity, or operational requirements while preserving feasibility through the optimization layer. In personalized medicine supply chains, such methods could support the evaluation of strategic planning decisions under different patient demand and capacity scenarios. More broadly, the approach may be useful in domains where learned models must interact with exact algorithms to produce valid decisions. The method is not intended to replace domain expertise, clinical judgement, or human oversight in high-stakes settings. Exact constrained inference preserves feasibility with respect to the specified constraints, but it does not guarantee that the learned energy ranks all decisions correctly or that the specified objective captures all clinical, ethical, or operational priorities. If deployed without careful validation, a learned energy could favor suboptimal allocations or reinforce undesirable trade-offs. 31
