# Understanding Large Language Models as Numerical Optimizers 

Lingyun Guo and Ali Bereyhi, Senior Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as potential tools for numerical optimization. Despite promising results, their intrinsic optimization capabilities and limitations remain poorly understood, particularly in high dimensions. This paper investigates the ability of LLMs to exploit mathematical problem structures and generate feasible, near-optimal solutions. We develop an evaluation framework that distinguishes solution quality from execution failures, including numerical inaccu-racies, constraint violations, and output-format errors. Using representative problems from convex optimization, fractional programming, and constrained projection, we assess LLM per-formance against deterministic reference solvers across different dimensions. Our results reveal that LLMs can capture useful optimization structures and produce near-optimal solutions, even when their outputs fail to satisfy numerical or formatting require-ments. However, reliability deteriorates with increasing problem dimension and depends strongly on the underlying optimization structure. To further investigate the limitations, we deploy an iterative refinement mechanism that provides targeted feedback on feasibility and solution quality. The results demonstrate that iterative refinement substantially improves reliability and extends the range of problem dimensions over which LLMs produce valid solutions. Nevertheless, persistent numerical and structural errors limit their applicability as standalone optimizers. These findings provide insights into the capacity and limits of LLM-based optimization and motivate hybrid approaches that combine LLM reasoning with deterministic numerical verification. 

Impact Statement —Large language models (LLMs) are increas-ingly being considered for optimization and automated decision-making in engineering and other computational applications. Deploying these models without understanding their numerical limitations may lead to infeasible or unreliable decisions, par-ticularly in systems governed by strict operational constraints. This work provides empirical insights into the capabilities and limitations of LLMs as numerical optimizers. It depicts that the ability of LLMs to identify useful structures does not necessarily translate into accurate numerical execution. By characterizing failure modes and investigating the impact of iterative feedback, this study highlights the importance of independent feasibility verification and numerical validation. The findings can inform the development of more reliable LLM-aided optimization systems, in which LLMs support problem interpretation and solution gener-ation while deterministic algorithms enforce mathematical rigour. Such hybrid approaches may facilitate the responsible integration of LLMs into engineering optimization and automated decision-making workflows. 

Index Terms —Artificial intelligence, large language models, constrained optimization, iterative refinement, numerical opti-mization, resource allocation.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated their ability to interpret natural language context, take feedback, and generate solutions through reasoning [1]– [4]. These capabilities motivate the exploration of LLMs as numerical optimizers for different tasks, especially in applica-tions such as resource allocation and scheduling [5]–[7]. Existing work has studied LLM-based optimization methods in various contexts. Yang et al. [8] consider optimization tasks described in natural language, including linear regression, the traveling salesman problem, and prompt optimization for task accuracy, and propose the optimization by prompting (OPRO) 

scheme, which iteratively generates candidate solutions using the guidance of an external evaluator. The LLM-driven evo-lutionary algorithm (LMEA) approach applies mutation and crossover operations on high-quality candidates to search for better solutions [9]. Closely related lines of work have further explored learning-based approaches instead of direct LLM prompting [10]–[13]. A recent example is the unified learning-based optimization (LLMOPT) framework in [13] that pro-poses fine-tuning LLMs to represent and solve optimization problems more reliably. LLMOPT also introduces mechanisms for self-correction and improved output validation. 

A. Motivation: Reliability of LLM-based Optimizers 

The mentioned studies suggest that LLMs can serve as a useful component in optimization pipelines. Nevertheless, the available lines of work focus mostly on developing new designs that improve the solution inferred from a given context and pay less attention to understanding the intrinsic optimiza-tion capabilities of LLMs. In numerical optimization, however, baseline algorithms rely on the problem structure deterministi-cally and exploit it to determine the solution, e.g., water-filling for parallel-channel power allocation, Dinkelbach iterations for fractional programming, and the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) for constrained optimization [14]– [18]. This enables the algorithms to guarantee satisfaction of feasibility constraints and numerical precision requirements. Although prior work demonstrates the capability of LLM-based pipelines for inferring the solution, it fails to understand the underlying intrinsic process. The lack of this knowledge, restricts the reliability and efficiency of these optimizers, especially in high-dimensional and high-precision settings. In particular, there is limited empirical evidence on how LLM performance scales with problem dimension or whether LLMs effectively exploit the underlying problem structure throughout the reasoning tree. Existing studies do not clearly distinguish arXiv:2610.37932v1 [cs.AI] 27 Oct 2026 2

poor decisions from execution failures. This lack of under-standing makes the guaranties for the feasibility, precision, and optimality gap of the output unclear. This work develops a framework that addresses this research gap. 

B. Contributions and Organization 

We study the intrinsic behaviour of LLMs, when deployed as numerical optimizers. Our contributions are as follows. 1) We propose a framework that evaluates how an LLM exploits the problem structure throughout its optimization pipeline. The framework uses a loop to assess the main feature extracted by the LLM and distinguish poor in-context learning from execution errors. 2) Motivated by recent findings on reasoning procedure in LLMs [19], [20], we propose an iterative refinement protocol for numerical optimization via LLMs. In this protocol, the LLMs receive an assessment about the structure of the solutions they previously produced and are prompted to propose an improvement. This protocol enables LLMs to correct execution errors and improve their solutions over multiple attempts, which as a result provides means to evaluate the understanding of problem structure by the LLM. 3) We study three different optimization families, namely convex optimization, fractional programming, and opti-mization problems with mixed equality and inequality constraints. For each family, we evaluate whether LLMs exploit the problem structure in its reasoning chain and investigate the feasibility and optimality of inferred solu-tions by comparing the results with that of deterministic optimal solvers. Our findings suggest that LLMs use the problem context to estimate the structure of the underlying problem and utilize it to infer the solution. 4) We study the scalability of LLM-based optimization for the considered three problem families. For each family, we characterize how the reliability and accuracy of the inferred solution scales with dimension and identify prac-tical failure modes. The results are compared to determin-istic reference solvers in terms of feasibility, optimality, and execution time. The results are consistent with our earlier findings: in problems with known structure, LLM-based optimization can better scale, while in problems with unknown algorithmic solution, the evaluation loop starts to report failure at lower dimensions. This finding suggests that the underlying structure actively contributes to the inferred solution by LLM. The remainder of this manuscript is organized as follows: Section II reviews the background and related work on LLM-based optimization. We present our proposed framework in Section III and elaborate the evaluation metrics. Experimental settings are presented in Section IV, where we report our findings. The results and their implications for LLM-based optimization are discussed in Section V. Finally, the paper is concluded in Section VI. II. R ELATED WORK 

The work on LLM-based optimization can be categorized into two main directions: ( i) design of LLM-assisted optimiz-ers, and ( ii ) evaluation of LLM-based optimization pipelines. In the sequel, we go over these two directions. 

A. LLMs as Optimizers 

Classical LLM-based optimizers use prompting methods to infer a solution for the underlying optimization. A rep-resentative approach that deploys LLMs as general-purpose optimizers is OPRO [8]. The approach solves problems in an iterative manner. An LLM first proposes candidate solutions, and an external scorer evaluates them. The best candidates are then summarized and returned to the LLM to guide the next round of proposals. This black-box workflow is attractive for problems in which ( i) gradients are unavailable, ( ii ) the objective is only accessible through simulation, and ( iii ) the constraints are difficult to encode in standard solvers. An alternative, called LMEA, approach is proposed in [9]. LMEA applies mutation and crossover operations to diversify high-quality candidate solutions and combine them. The exper-iments in [9] depict that LMEA can achieve competitive performance on combinatorial benchmarks. A prior line of work on the use of learning models for opti-mization considers learning-based approaches to train models as optimizers [10]–[13]. Early studies in this direction focus on explicit training of learning models as solvers; see for example [10], [11]. With advances in LLMs, this approach has been extended to LLM-based optimization. The study in [13] proposes the LLMOPT framework that uses textual problem information to fine-tune LLMs as optimization solvers. The post-training in LLMOPT yields more reliability, as the LLM learns to attend to the properties of the underlying problem. LLMOPT further includes mechanisms for self-correction and improved output validity. The study in [5] develops a retrieval-based approach for the specific problem of power allocation in wireless networks. In the proposed approach, an LLM is fed with numerical representations of the network state and selects the best allocations from a quantized action set. Selections are evaluated using a task-specific reward. The solution to each sample problem is then stored in an experience pool. For subsequent decisions, examples are retrieved from this pool and fed back to LLM as prompts to adapt its behaviour to the underlying optimization problem. Although findings in the former studies suggest that LLMs can serve as proposal generators in optimization pipelines, they highlight various practical challenges when dealing with strict feasibility requirements, e.g., non-negativity of solutions, equality budget constraints, and fixed dimensionality [5], [13]. The limits of LLM-based optimizers in these challenging set-tings come from two different sources: ( i) failure in grasping the problem structure, and ( ii ) errors in execution. While the latter can be refined through feedback loops in the pipeline, the former seems to be more restrictive. This work develops a framework to distinguish between these sources and charac-terize the limits of LLM-based optimizers. 

B. LLM Capability Evaluation 

Recent work has developed basic schemes to evaluate the performance of LLMs as optimizers in applications such as GUO AND BEREYHI: UNDERSTANDING LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS AS NUMERICAL OPTIMIZERS 3

code generation and mathematical problem solving [21]–[23]. The measuring massive multitask language understanding (MMLU) approach [24] evaluates LLMs across a wide range of subjects, including mathematics, history, and computer science. The initial results in [24] showed that the evaluated models were particularly weak in socially important areas such as morality and law. More recent studies, including MMLU-Pro [25] and MMLU-Redux [26], demonstrate substantial improvements in LLM performance while also highlighting re-maining limitations and considerable variation across domains. MMLU-Pro further shows that more challenging, reasoning-intensive questions remain difficult for modern LLMs, while MMLU-Redux identifies errors in the original MMLU bench-mark that can affect reported performance. The beyond the imitation game benchmark (BIG-bench) evaluates language models across 204 diverse tasks designed to be challenging for existing models. The authors investigate whether performance trends observed in smaller models can be used to predict the capabilities of larger models [27]. Their results show that model performance generally improves with scale, but the rate and pattern of improvement vary substantially across tasks. The study also shows that apparent capability breakthroughs can depend on how performance is measured. This highlights the importance of evaluation metrics and separating distinct failure modes when assessing LLMs. In addition to LLMs performance evaluation on generic subjects, several recent studies have focused on evaluation of LLMs as mathematical problem solvers. Cobbe et al. introduce the GSM8K dataset and evaluate the mathematical capability of LLMs when tested on this dataset. The results show that LLMs can perform multi-step mathematical reasoning. Interestingly, the verification mechanism they proposed can substantially improve answer accuracy [28]. This can follow from the fact that many inconsistencies in the LLM responses are due to execution errors . The study, however, does not eval-uate the scalability of this behaviour, i.e., to characterize the performance scaling with problem dimension. Recent studies in [19], [20] elaborate the importance of such evaluations, as they provide means to distinguish different failure modes. III. P ROPOSED FRAMEWORK

The proposed protocol enables us to distinguish between execution error and failure mode of LLMs. We then present the representative optimization families and deterministic ref-erence solvers and define the key evaluation metrics.

validity rate is then defined as the fraction of valid outputs over the total number of attempts.

B. Understanding LLM Optimization Capabilities 

The empirical results indicate that the evaluated models capture useful high-level optimization structure. In sum-rate maximization, LLM-generated allocations often resemble the deterministic water-filling solution. However, a small budget violation may still lead to invalid outputs, as shown in Ap-pendix A-B. In the projection task, the high optimality rate among valid outputs indicates that the model can generate solutions close to the constrained optimum as long as fea-sibility is achieved. However, LLMs cannot reliably perform the exact arithmetic and strict constraint enforcement required by numerical optimization. Structured feedback helps models correct some formatting, counting, and feasibility errors and improve objective quality. Nevertheless, iterative refinement does not guarantee success, particularly at larger dimensions. Meanwhile, although μ-O4 and φ-G3 exhibit different optimization capabilities, their results reveal similar overall trends. φ-G3 shows a larger operating range, but still suffers from failures at larger dimensions. This suggests that stronger models can extend the usable range of direct LLM opti-mization, while reliable large-scale applications still require additional mechanisms for verification and repair.

They should specify the expected output length, and numerical format. All constraints should be given in a machine-checkable form. B. Minimum-Distance Projection 

The following trace shows the model solving a projec-tion problem instance with K = 40 variables, one equality constraint, and one inequality constraint. The model applies KKT conditions and Lagrangian analysis from first principles to derive the solution structure. The trace was truncated by the model’s reasoning token limit before the numerical computation was completed. However, the reasoning up to that point demonstrates correct formulation and case analysis. 

Okay, here’s the deal. I’m faced with aminimization problem. The goal is to make f(x) = 1/2 ||x - y||ˆ2 as small as possible, effectively finding the point x closest to afixed point y. However, there are rules. I’m dealing with a vector xin 40 dimensions (K=40, y is a 40-dim vector, x is the variable I’m optimizing over), so that the objective is to find avector x, subject to afew constraints. First, all components of x must be non-negative: x_i >= 0. This is straightforward. Second, there’s an equality constraint: Ax + b = 0. Think of A as a row vector (1x40 matrix), and b is just a number. It defines a hyperplane. My solution x must lie on this plane, since I want Ax=-b, given A_i (40 element vector) and b (the intercept), and x_i which are the elements of x (40 element vector). Finally, I’ve got an inequality constraint: Ux <= d. Here U is another row vector (1x40 matrix), d a scalar. So, I need to keep x "below" this hyperplane too. To handle these constraints formally, I’m using the Lagrangian method.
